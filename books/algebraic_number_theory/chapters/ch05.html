Chapter 05

Local Class Field Theory

The abstract machinery of Chapter IV becomes concrete: every abelian extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is named, computed, and built by hand.

Neukirch Chapter V ~55 min reading time Prereqs: Ch. II (local fields), Ch. IV (abstract CFT), Galois cohomology

Orientation: what this chapter is for

Chapter IV built a machine. Given a profinite group $G$, a $G$-module $A$, and a surjection $d\colon G \to \hat{\mathbb{Z}}$ satisfying a short list of axioms (the "class field axiom" and a henselian valuation), it produced for every finite Galois extension a canonical reciprocity isomorphism. That was abstract: it never looked at a single number.

This chapter feeds a local field into that machine and watches the abstract objects turn into things you can hold. The group $G$ becomes the absolute Galois group of a $p$-adic field; the module $A$ becomes the multiplicative group of the separable closure; the abstract reciprocity map becomes the local norm residue symbol. And then the chapter does something the abstract theory never could: it computes. Over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ the symbol has an explicit closed form; the Hilbert symbol reduces to congruences mod $p$; and Lubin–Tate formal groups manufacture the abelian extensions out of "division points" the way roots of unity manufacture cyclotomic fields.

Intuition

Keep one analogy in your pocket for the whole chapter: prime elements correspond to Frobenius. In the abstract theory a uniformizer $\pi$ of $K$ is sent by the reciprocity map to something acting like the Frobenius on the unramified tower. Almost every explicit formula in this chapter — the Lubin–Tate condition $[\pi]_F(X)\equiv X^q \bmod \pi$, the value of the norm residue symbol on a uniformizer — is just this slogan made precise.

Notation for the whole chapter

Let $K$ be a local field — complete with respect to a discrete valuation, with finite residue field. By Chapter II these are exactly the finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ or $\mathbb{F}_p((t))$. We write:

  • $v_K$ — the normalized valuation with $v_K(K^\times)=\mathbb{Z}$;
  • $\mathcal{O}_K$ — the valuation ring, $\mathfrak{p}_K$ its maximal ideal, $\kappa=\mathcal{O}_K/\mathfrak{p}_K$ the residue field with $q=\#\kappa$;
  • $U_K$ — the units $\{a\in K^\times \mid v_K(a)=0\}$, and $U_K^{(n)}=1+\mathfrak{p}_K^n$ the $n$-th higher units (with $U_K^{(0)}=U_K$);
  • $\pi_K$ (or $\pi$) — a prime element, so $\mathfrak{p}_K=\pi\mathcal{O}_K$;
  • $\mu_n$ — the $n$-th roots of unity, $\mu_n(K)=\mu_n\cap K^\times$.

5.1 The Local Reciprocity Law

To turn the crank of Chapter IV we must check that the multiplicative group of a local field satisfies the class field axiom. Everything in this section is in service of that one verification, after which the abstract theorems apply for free.

Theorem (1.1) — the class field axiom

For a cyclic extension $L\mid K$ of local fields,

$$\#\widehat{H}^{i}\big(G(L\mid K),\,L^\times\big)=\begin{cases}[L:K] & i=0,\\[2pt] 1 & i=-1.\end{cases}$$
Reminder — what $\widehat{H}^i$ means

The hat marks Tate cohomology: it splices ordinary homology (in the negative degrees) and cohomology (in the non-negative degrees) into one sequence $\widehat{H}^i$ indexed by all integers $i$, so that a long exact sequence runs in both directions. For a finite group $G$ the two degrees we use are concrete: $\widehat{H}^0(G,L^\times)=K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times$ (the cokernel of the norm map), and $\widehat{H}^{-1}(G,L^\times)$ is the kernel of the norm modulo the augmentation ideal (the elements killed by the norm, up to $\sigma x/x$ terms). So the theorem says exactly two things: $K^\times/N L^\times$ has order $[L:K]$, and the norm-one elements are all of the trivial form.

The case $i=-1$ is Hilbert 90 (Chapter IV). The content is the case $i=0$, which is equivalent to saying the Herbrand quotient $h(G,L^\times)$ equals $[L:K]$. Here is the strategy in plain language.

Definition — the Herbrand quotient

For a cyclic group $G$ and a $G$-module $A$ whose two Tate groups are finite, the Herbrand quotient is the ratio of their orders, $h(G,A)=\dfrac{\#\widehat{H}^0(G,A)}{\#\widehat{H}^{-1}(G,A)}.$ Two properties do all the work. It is multiplicative in short exact sequences: if $0\to A\to B\to C\to 0$ then $h(G,B)=h(G,A)\,h(G,C)$ (whenever any two are defined). And it is invariant under finite modules: $h(G,A)=1$ if $A$ is finite. So $h$ ignores finite pieces and only sees the "size" of the infinite part — which is why the unit count below comes out to exactly $1$.

How the proof goes

Break $L^\times$ apart with the exact sequence $1\to U_L\to L^\times \xrightarrow{v_L} \mathbb{Z}\to 0$. Herbrand quotients are multiplicative, and $h(G,\mathbb{Z})=[L:K]$ (the trivial module). So it suffices to show the units contribute nothing: $h(G,U_L)=1$.

For the units one uses a normal basis of $L\mid K$ to find a $G$-stable open lattice $M$ in $\mathcal{O}_L$. The neighbourhoods $V_n=1+\pi_K^n M$ are, for large $n$, subgroups of $U_L$ with successive quotients $V_n/V_{n+1}\cong M/\pi_K M$ — an induced module (an induced module has the shape $\mathbb{Z}[G]\otimes M_0$: one copy of a base module $M_0$ for each group element, permuted by $G$). By Shapiro's lemma the cohomology of an induced module collapses to that of the trivial group — i.e. it vanishes in every nonzero Tate degree — so such a module is cohomologically trivial and its Herbrand quotient is $1$. Hence $h(G,V_n)=1$, and since $U_L/V_n$ is finite the unit Herbrand quotient is $1$.

Corollary (1.2) — the unramified case is trivial

If $L\mid K$ is unramified, then for $i=0,-1$ all the unit cohomology vanishes, and consequently $N_{L\mid K}U_L = U_K, \qquad N_{L\mid K}U_L^{(n)}=U_K^{(n)}.$ In words: over an unramified extension every unit of the base is a norm. This is the technical fact that makes "unramified $\Leftrightarrow$ conductor 1" work later.

Reminder — the Chapter IV axioms we are about to feed

The abstract machine of Chapter IV runs on three ingredients, and "satisfies every axiom" below means exactly these:

  • a surjection $d\colon G\to\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$ picking out a Frobenius direction (here: reduction to the Galois group of the residue field);
  • a henselian valuation $v$ with respect to $d$, normalized so that $v(K^\times)=\mathbb{Z}$ (here: the usual $p$-adic valuation $v_K$);
  • the class field axiom (1.1) just proved — the Tate-cohomology counts $\#\widehat{H}^0=[L:K]$, $\widehat{H}^{-1}=1$ for cyclic $L\mid K$.

With these three in hand, Chapter IV hands back the reciprocity isomorphism with no further work.

Now assemble the inputs the abstract theory wants. Over the maximal unramified extension $\tilde{K}\mid K$ the residue field becomes the algebraic closure $\bar\kappa$, and Chapter II gives a canonical isomorphism $G(\tilde K\mid K)\cong G(\bar\kappa\mid\kappa)\cong\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$, sending $1$ to the Frobenius $x\mapsto x^q$. This is the surjection $d\colon G\to\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$. Together with the valuation $v_K$ (which is henselian with respect to $d$), the pair $(d, v_K)$ satisfies every axiom of a class field theory.

Theorem (1.3) — Local Reciprocity Law

For every finite Galois extension $L\mid K$ of local fields there is a canonical isomorphism

$$r_{L\mid K}\colon G(L\mid K)^{\mathrm{ab}} \;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\; K^\times / N_{L\mid K}L^\times.$$

Its inverse is the norm residue symbol $(\,\cdot\,,L\mid K)\colon K^\times \to G(L\mid K)^{\mathrm{ab}}$, which is surjective with kernel exactly $N_{L\mid K}L^\times$.

What the symbol does, concretely

The local theory is where the abstract definition of the reciprocity map becomes transparent. Given $\sigma\in G(L\mid K)$, lift it to $\tilde\sigma$ on the maximal unramified extension with $d(\tilde\sigma)\in\mathbb{N}$. Let $E$ be the fixed field of $\tilde\sigma$ and $\pi_E$ a prime element of $E$. Then $r_{L\mid K}(\sigma)=N_{E\mid K}(\pi_E)\bmod N_{L\mid K}L^\times$. Prime elements of cleverly chosen fixed fields are the reciprocity map.

Micro-example to ground it. Let $L\mid K$ be unramified of degree $f$ and take $\sigma=\mathrm{Frob}$, so $d(\tilde\sigma)=1$. Its fixed field on the unramified tower is just the base, $E=K$, so a prime element of $E$ is a prime element of $K$: $\pi_E=\pi_K$. The formula then reads $r_{L\mid K}(\mathrm{Frob})=\pi_K\bmod N_{L\mid K}L^\times$. In a slogan: Frobenius maps to a uniformizer — the abstract reciprocity map, made fully explicit in the simplest case.

The archimedean place

Global theory later needs $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$ too. For the unique nontrivial extension $\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R}$ the norm residue symbol is $(a,\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R})\sqrt{-1}=\operatorname{sgn}(a)\sqrt{-1}$, with kernel $\mathbb{R}_+ = N_{\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}^\times$. A real number is a norm from $\mathbb{C}$ exactly when it is positive.

The reciprocity isomorphism instantly classifies abelian extensions. This is the heart of the local theory.

Theorem (1.4) — Existence Theorem

The rule $L\mapsto \mathcal{N}_L=N_{L\mid K}L^\times$ is a bijection between the finite abelian extensions of $K$ and the open subgroups of finite index in $K^\times$. It is inclusion-reversing and intertwines compositum/intersection with intersection/product of norm groups:

$$L_1\subseteq L_2 \iff \mathcal{N}_{L_2}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{L_1},\qquad \mathcal{N}_{L_1 L_2}=\mathcal{N}_{L_1}\cap\mathcal{N}_{L_2},\qquad \mathcal{N}_{L_1\cap L_2}=\mathcal{N}_{L_1}\mathcal{N}_{L_2}.$$

The word "existence" refers to the surjectivity: for every open finite-index subgroup $\mathcal{N}$ there is an abelian "class field" $L$ with that exact norm group. The hard half is showing open finite-index subgroups are precisely the norm groups; for $n$ prime to the residue characteristic this is pure Kummer theory, and when $p\mid n$ it is exactly what §4's Lubin–Tate theory will supply.

Definition (1.6) — conductor

For a finite abelian $L\mid K$, let $n$ be the smallest integer $n\ge 0$ with $U_K^{(n)}\subseteq N_{L\mid K}L^\times$. The ideal $\mathfrak{f}=\mathfrak{p}_K^{\,n}$ is the conductor of $L\mid K$.

Proposition (1.7) — conductor detects ramification

$L\mid K$ is unramified $\iff$ its conductor is $\mathfrak{f}=1$. One direction is Corollary (1.2): unramified means $U_K=N_{L\mid K}U_L$, so already $n=0$ works.

Worked example (1.8) — cyclotomic norm groups over Q_p

Take $K=\mathbb{Q}_p$ and $L=\mathbb{Q}_p(\mu_{p^n})$. The norm group is $N_{L\mid K}L^\times = \langle p\rangle \times U_K^{(n)}.$ Why: by Chapter II this extension is totally ramified of degree $p^{n-1}(p-1)$, and if $\zeta$ is a primitive $p^n$-th root of unity then $1-\zeta$ is a prime element of $L$ with $N_{L\mid K}(1-\zeta)=p$. So $p$ is a norm. To see the norm computation in the cleanest case $n=1$, recall the cyclotomic polynomial factors as $\Phi_p(X)=\frac{X^p-1}{X-1}=\prod_{j=1}^{p-1}(X-\zeta^j)$. The conjugates of $1-\zeta$ over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ are exactly the $1-\zeta^j$, so $N_{L\mid K}(1-\zeta)=\prod_{j=1}^{p-1}(1-\zeta^j)=\Phi_p(1)=p$, the last step because $\Phi_p(1)=1+1+\cdots+1=p$ (substitute $X=1$ into $1+X+\cdots+X^{p-1}$). The exponential map identifies the higher units, and a count of indices (both sides have index $p^{n-1}(p-1)$) forces equality. The conductor of $\mathbb{Q}_p(\mu_{p^n})$ is therefore $\mathfrak{p}^n=(p^n)$.

Corollary (1.9) — local Kronecker–Weber

Every finite abelian extension $L\mid\mathbb{Q}_p$ sits inside some $\mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta)$ with $\zeta$ a root of unity. Equivalently, $\mathbb{Q}_p^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is generated by all roots of unity. The proof just decomposes the relevant norm group $\langle p^f\rangle\times U_K^{(n)}$ into an unramified part (giving the prime-to-$p$ roots of unity) and a ramified part (giving the $p^n$-th roots of unity).

From the local statement one even recovers the global Kronecker–Weber theorem (1.10): every finite abelian $L\mid\mathbb{Q}$ lies in a cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$. The argument completes each ramified prime locally, builds $n$ from the local conductors, and uses Minkowski's theorem (no unramified-everywhere extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ exists) to pin the inertia groups exactly.

5.2 The Norm Residue Symbol over Q_p

Existence and uniqueness are nice, but we want a formula. Over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ the norm residue symbol can be written down explicitly. This is the single most important computation in the chapter, and it feeds directly into global class field theory.

The unramified piece

If $\zeta$ is a primitive $m$-th root of unity with $(m,p)=1$, then $\mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta)\mid\mathbb{Q}_p$ is unramified, and the symbol is just a power of Frobenius: $(a,\mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta)\mid\mathbb{Q}_p)\,\zeta=\zeta^{\,p^{v_p(a)}}.$ The valuation of $a$ tells you how many times to apply Frobenius.

Theorem (2.4) — the ramified formula

Write $a=u\,p^{v_p(a)}\in\mathbb{Q}_p^\times$ with $u\in\mathbb{Z}_p^\times$, and let $\zeta$ be a primitive $p^n$-th root of unity. Read the exponent $u^{-1}$ below as an inverse taken mod $p^n$: since $\zeta$ has order $p^n$, only the residue of $u^{-1}\bmod p^n$ affects $\zeta^{u^{-1}}$. Then

$$(a,\mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta)\mid\mathbb{Q}_p)\,\zeta=\zeta^{\,u^{-1}},$$

where $\zeta^{u^{-1}}$ means $\zeta^r$ for any integer $r\equiv u^{-1}\bmod p^n$.

Read the two formulas together

Identify $G(\mathbb{Q}_p(\mu_{p^n})\mid\mathbb{Q}_p)\cong(\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z})^\times$ via $\sigma_c\colon\zeta\mapsto\zeta^c$. Then the symbol sends the unit part $u$ of $a$ to the inverse automorphism $\sigma_{u^{-1}}$, while the $p$-part of $a$ contributes Frobenius on the unramified side. The minus-one exponent is not a typo — it is the same inversion that makes the norm group come out right (the symbol's kernel is the norms, and $p$ must be a norm).

A fully numeric instance

Let $p=5,\ n=1$, so $L=\mathbb{Q}_5(\zeta_5)$ with group $(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^\times$. Take $a=2$ (a unit, $v_5(2)=0$). Since $2\cdot 3\equiv 1$, $2^{-1}\equiv 3\bmod 5$, so $(2,L\mid\mathbb{Q}_5)\,\zeta_5=\zeta_5^{\,3}$. And $(5,L\mid\mathbb{Q}_5)$ acts trivially on $\zeta_5$ because $5$ is a norm — consistent with the norm group $\langle 5\rangle\times U^{(1)}$.

Caveat — no elementary proof

Strikingly, there is no purely algebraic proof of Theorem (2.4) known. Neukirch proves it by a transcendental method using the completion $\hat{K}$ of the maximal unramified extension and explicit power series — the very Lubin–Tate series that §4 makes systematic. The technical engine is the following lemma.

Lemma (2.1) — solving Frobenius equations in the completion

Here $\hat K$ is the completion of the maximal unramified extension $K^{\mathrm{nr}}=\tilde K$ of $K$; its residue field is the algebraic closure $\bar\kappa$, which is what makes the residue-level equations solvable. Extend Frobenius $\varphi$ continuously to $\hat{K}$. For every $c\in\mathcal{O}_{\hat K}$ (resp. $c\in U_{\hat K}^{(n)}$) the equation $x^\varphi-x=c$ (resp. $x^\varphi/x=c$) has a solution there; and if $x^\varphi=x$ then $x\in\mathcal{O}_K$. The proof solves the congruence one residue-degree at a time, using that the residue field of $\hat K$ is algebraically closed, then passes to the limit.

Why these two equations? The additive $x^\varphi-x=c$ and multiplicative $x^\varphi/x=c$ Frobenius equations are the engine that builds the Lubin–Tate power series coefficient by coefficient: each new coefficient of the series is precisely the solution $x$ of one such equation. Being able to solve them in $\hat K$ is the entire transcendental content behind theorem (2.4) — there is no algebraic shortcut, which is why we pass to the completion at all.

Lubin–Tate series, first appearance

A Lubin–Tate series for a prime element $\pi$ of $K$ is a power series $e(X)\in\mathcal{O}_K[[X]]$ with $e(X)\equiv \pi X \bmod \deg 2$ and $e(X)\equiv X^q \bmod \pi.$ The collection is written $\mathcal{E}_\pi$; the polynomials $e(X)=X^q+\pi X$ are the simplest examples. Over $K=\mathbb{Q}_p$ with $\pi=p$, the polynomial $e(X)=(1+X)^p-1$ is a Lubin–Tate polynomial. Already you can see roots of unity hiding inside it.

The functional equation that pins down everything (2.2)–(2.3)

The key existence/uniqueness statement: given Lubin–Tate series $e,\tilde e$ and a linear form $L$ with $\pi L = \tilde\pi\, L^\varphi$, there is a unique power series $F$ with $F\equiv L\bmod\deg 2$ and $e(F)=F^\varphi(\tilde e)$. The proof builds $F$ degree by degree, at each step solving an equation of the form $\alpha-\gamma\alpha^\varphi=\beta$ (with $v(\gamma)\ge 1$) by a convergent geometric series. Out of this come the series $\theta$ intertwining two Lubin–Tate setups (2.3), which is exactly the tool that proves (2.4).

What this buys us: because $F$ is unique, the series $\theta$ lets you swap one uniformizer or one Lubin–Tate series for another without changing the field they generate — the two setups are formally isomorphic. That independence is precisely why the extension $L_n$ in §5.5 turns out to depend only on the prime $\pi$ and not on the particular series $e$ you chose to define the formal group.

5.3 The Hilbert Symbol

Now suppose $K$ contains the $n$-th roots of unity $\mu_n$, with $n$ prime to $\operatorname{char}K$. Two theories now apply to $K$ at once: Kummer theory and class field theory. The Hilbert symbol is their interaction, and it generalizes Gauss's reciprocity to $n$-th power residues.

Definition — the Hilbert symbol

Kummer reminder. When $K$ contains $\mu_n$, Kummer theory says every cyclic degree-$n$ extension is $K(\sqrt[n]{b})$ for some $b\in K^\times$, and abelian extensions of exponent $n$ correspond bijectively to subgroups of $K^\times/K^{\times n}$ — i.e. choosing a radicand mod $n$-th powers is the same as choosing the extension. Now let $L=K(\sqrt[n]{K^\times})$ be the maximal abelian extension of exponent $n$. Class field theory gives $G(L\mid K)\cong K^\times/K^{\times n}$; Kummer theory gives $\operatorname{Hom}(G(L\mid K),\mu_n)\cong K^\times/K^{\times n}$. Pairing them yields the nondegenerate $\left(\tfrac{\,\cdot,\cdot\,}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\colon K^\times/K^{\times n}\times K^\times/K^{\times n}\to\mu_n.$ Explicitly, writing $\sigma_a$ for the image of $a$ under the reciprocity map in $G(L\mid K)$, the symbol is the ratio by which $\sigma_a$ moves a chosen radical: $(a,b)\ \longmapsto\ \dfrac{\sigma_a\!\left(\sqrt[n]{b}\right)}{\sqrt[n]{b}}\in\mu_n.$

Proposition (3.1) — the bridge to the norm residue symbol

For $a,b\in K^\times$, $(a,K(\sqrt[n]{b})\mid K)\,\sqrt[n]{b}=\left(\tfrac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\sqrt[n]{b}.$ In words: the Hilbert symbol is the norm residue symbol acting on a chosen $n$-th root. Everything about it follows from this and the properties of $(\,\cdot\,,L\mid K)$.

Proposition (3.2) — the calculus of the symbol
  • Bilinearity: $\left(\tfrac{aa',b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=\left(\tfrac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\left(\tfrac{a',b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)$ and likewise in the second slot.
  • Norm criterion: $\left(\tfrac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=1 \iff a$ is a norm from $K(\sqrt[n]{b})\mid K$.
  • Skew-symmetry: $\left(\tfrac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=\left(\tfrac{b,a}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)^{-1}$.
  • Steinberg relations: $\left(\tfrac{a,1-a}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=1$ and $\left(\tfrac{a,-a}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=1.$
  • Nondegeneracy: if $\left(\tfrac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=1$ for all $b$, then $a\in K^{\times n}$.
The real symbol

For $K=\mathbb{R}$ we have $n=1$ or $2$, and for $n=2$ $\left(\tfrac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{\operatorname{sgn}a-1}{2}\cdot\frac{\operatorname{sgn}b-1}{2}}.$ It is $-1$ exactly when $a$ and $b$ are both negative — i.e. when $ax^2+by^2=z^2$ has no nontrivial real solution.

Proposition (3.4) — the tame symbol

Suppose $(n,p)=1$ (so $n\mid q-1$). Write $\alpha=v_K(a),\ \beta=v_K(b)$. Then the Hilbert symbol is given by a single congruence mod $\mathfrak{p}_K$:

$$\left(\tfrac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\equiv\left((-1)^{\alpha\beta}\,\frac{a^{\beta}}{b^{\alpha}}\right)^{\!(q-1)/n}\!\!\!\!\pmod{\mathfrak{p}_K}.$$

The symbol depends only on residues, and (Lemma 3.3) $K(\sqrt[n]{x})\mid K$ is unramified iff $x\in U_K K^{\times n}$.

Run it concretely. Take $K=\mathbb{Q}_p$ ($p$ odd), $n=2$ — allowed since $\mu_2\subseteq\mathbb{Q}_p$ and indeed $2\mid p-1$ — with $a=p$ (so $\alpha=v_p(p)=1$) and $b=u$ a unit (so $\beta=v_p(u)=0$). The formula collapses: the $(-1)^{\alpha\beta}$ factor is $1$, $a^{\beta}=p^0=1$, and what remains is $\left(\tfrac{p,u}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\equiv u^{-(q-1)/2}\equiv u^{(p-1)/2}\equiv\left(\tfrac{u}{p}\right)\pmod{p}$ — exactly the Legendre symbol $\left(\tfrac{u}{p}\right)$, by Euler's criterion. The tame Hilbert symbol literally is quadratic residue theory.

The n-th power residue symbol (3.5)

Because the tame symbol is independent of the choice of prime, for a unit $u$ one defines the power residue symbol $\left(\tfrac{u}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)_n=\left(\tfrac{\pi,u}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)$, the root of unity with $\left(\tfrac{u}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)_n\equiv u^{(q-1)/n}\bmod\mathfrak{p}_K.$ And $\left(\tfrac{u}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)_n=1 \iff u$ is an $n$-th power mod $\mathfrak{p}_K$ — exactly Euler's criterion when $n=2$. This is the genuine generalization of the Legendre symbol.

Theorem (3.6) — the wild case n = 2 over Q_p, with all values

Write $a=p^{\alpha}a',\ b=p^{\beta}b'$ with $a',b'$ units. If $p\neq 2$, $\left(\tfrac{a,b}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\alpha\beta\frac{p-1}{2}}\left(\tfrac{a'^{\beta}b'^{\alpha}}{p}\right).$ If $p=2$ and $a,b\in U_{\mathbb{Q}_2}$, $\left(\tfrac{a,b}{2}\right)=(-1)^{\varepsilon(a)\varepsilon(b)},\quad \varepsilon(x)=\tfrac{x-1}{2}\bmod 2,$ and in general one also needs $\omega(x)=\tfrac{x^2-1}{8}\bmod 2$ for the factors of $2$.

The 2-adic computation, by hand

The proof for $p=2$ is wonderfully concrete. Both $\eta$ and $\varepsilon$ are additive mod $2$, so the symbol is bilinear and you only need it on generators of $U_{\mathbb{Q}_2}/U_{\mathbb{Q}_2}^2=\{\pm 1,\pm 5\}$. Check by hand: $\left(\tfrac{-1,-1}{2}\right)=-1$ (since $-1$ is not a sum of two squares in $\mathbb{Q}_2$), $\left(\tfrac{-1,2}{2}\right)=\left(\tfrac{-1,5}{2}\right)=1$, and $\left(\tfrac{2,5}{2}\right)=-1$ (else $2$ would be a square). These four values match the formula exactly.

The general wild symbol (3.7) — Brückner's formula

For $n=p^v$ with odd residue characteristic, the explicit symbol was only found in 1964 (Brückner). Writing elements as Laurent series $x=f(\pi)$ over the Witt ring $W$, the symbol becomes a trace of a residue of differentials: $\left(\tfrac{x,y}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=\zeta^{\,\omega(x,y)}$ with $\omega(x,y)=\operatorname{Tr}_{W\mid\mathbb{Z}_p}\operatorname{Res}\!\Big(h\big(\tfrac{1}{p}\log f\,d\log g-\tfrac{1}{p}\log g\,d\log f^\varphi\big)\Big).$ You do not need the details — note only that the wild symbol is genuinely transcendental, built from formal logarithms and residues. Treat this as a "look but don't touch" landmark. Two glosses for the unfamiliar symbols: $W$ is the ring of Witt vectors, the natural unramified coefficient ring (over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ it is the ring of integers of the unramified part, lifting the residue field); and $\operatorname{Res}$ is the formal residue of a differential — the coefficient of $d\pi/\pi$, i.e. the $-1$ term of the Laurent expansion, just as in complex analysis. The $d\log$ is the formal logarithmic derivative $\frac{g'}{g}\,d\pi$.

Artin–Hasse / Iwasawa (3.8)

Over $\Phi_v=\mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta_{p^v})$ with $\pi=1-\zeta$ and trace $S$ to $\mathbb{Q}_p$, the symbol has the clean shape $\left(\tfrac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=\zeta^{\,S(\log a\, D\log b)/p^v},$ with supplementary laws $\left(\tfrac{a,\pi}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=\zeta^{-S(\log a)/p^v}$ and $\left(\tfrac{a,\zeta}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=\zeta^{S((\zeta/\pi)\log a)/p^v}$. These are the explicit reciprocity laws of Artin, Hasse, and Iwasawa.

5.4 Formal Groups

The cyclotomic story over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ was so explicit because roots of unity are concrete. Over a general $K$ there is no obvious supply of "extra" roots of unity. Formal groups manufacture a substitute: a new kind of root of unity, the "division points" of a formal group, that does for $K$ what $p^n$-th roots of unity do for $\mathbb{Q}_p$.

Definition (4.1) — formal group

A (one-dimensional, commutative) formal group over a ring $o$ is a power series $F(X,Y)\in o[[X,Y]]$ with

  • $F(X,Y)\equiv X+Y\bmod\deg 2$ (it looks like addition to first order),
  • $F(X,Y)=F(Y,X)$ (commutativity),
  • $F(X,F(Y,Z))=F(F(X,Y),Z)$ (associativity).

If $o$ is a complete valuation ring with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}$, then $x+_F y := F(x,y)$ turns $\mathfrak{p}$ into an honest abelian group (the series converge).

Why a power-series group law rather than an honest group, and why only on $\mathfrak{p}$? The object $F$ is a recipe — a formula $F(x,y)$ — not a set with an operation. Because $F$ has no constant term (it starts at $X+Y$), the infinite series $F(x,y)$ converges precisely when $x,y$ are small — topologically nilpotent, i.e. in the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}$, where high powers tend to $0$. So the group law is one you can only evaluate on those small elements; on all of $o$ the sum would diverge. The formal group is the law; $\mathfrak{p}$ is where you may legally plug in numbers.

The two basic examples
  • Additive: $\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_a(X,Y)=X+Y$.
  • Multiplicative: $\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_m(X,Y)=X+Y+XY$. Since $X+Y+XY=(1+X)(1+Y)-1$, the new addition $+_{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_m}$ is ordinary multiplication under the translation $x\mapsto x+1$. This is the formal shadow of "roots of unity".
Definitions (4.2)–(4.4) — homomorphisms and formal modules

A homomorphism $f\colon F\to G$ is a series $f(X)=a_1 X+a_2X^2+\cdots$ with $f(F(X,Y))=G(f(X),f(Y))$; an isomorphism when $a_1$ is a unit. The endomorphisms of $F$ form a ring $\operatorname{End}_o(F)$ under $(f+g)(X)=F(f(X),g(X))$ and $f\circ g$. A formal $o$-module is a formal group together with a ring map $o\to\operatorname{End}_o(F),\ a\mapsto[a]_F(X)$ where $[a]_F(X)\equiv aX\bmod\deg 2$.

Definition (4.5) — Lubin–Tate module

A Lubin–Tate module over $\mathcal{O}_K$ for the prime $\pi$ is a formal $\mathcal{O}_K$-module $F$ with $[\pi]_F(X)\equiv X^q\bmod\pi.$ This is the slogan again: the endomorphism by the prime element $\pi$ reduces to the Frobenius $X^q$ on the residue field.

The model example

$\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_m$ is a formal $\mathbb{Z}_p$-module via $[a](X)=(1+X)^a-1$, and it is Lubin–Tate for $\pi=p$ because $[p](X)=(1+X)^p-1\equiv X^p\bmod p.$ Its division points will be precisely $\zeta-1$ for roots of unity $\zeta$. The whole point of §5 is to do this over any $K$.

Theorem (4.6) — classification

For each Lubin–Tate series $e\in\mathcal{E}_\pi$ the functional equation (2.2) produces a unique formal group $F_e$ with $e(F_e(X,Y))=F_e(e(X),e(Y))$ and endomorphisms $[a]_e$. Then:

  • The Lubin–Tate modules for $\pi$ are exactly the $F_e$, with structure $a\mapsto[a]_e$.
  • Each $[a]_{e,\tilde e}$ is a homomorphism $F_{\tilde e}\to F_e$, an isomorphism when $a$ is a unit.

Consequence: up to isomorphism the Lubin–Tate module for $\pi$ is unique, and one may always work with the convenient model $e(X)=X^q+\pi X$.

5.5 Generalized Cyclotomic Theory

We now harvest the formal groups. Evaluating a Lubin–Tate module on the maximal ideal of $\bar K$ turns its "torsion" into an explicit tower of abelian extensions — the exact analogue of the cyclotomic tower, but over any local field.

Proposition (5.1)–(5.2) — division points

For a formal $\mathcal{O}_K$-module $F$, the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}$ of $\bar K$ becomes a genuine $\mathcal{O}_K$-module $\mathfrak{p}_F$ under $x+y=F(x,y),\ a\cdot x=[a]_F(x)$. For a Lubin–Tate module, the $\pi^n$-division points are $F(n)=\ker[\pi^n]_F=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{p}_F \mid \pi^n\cdot\lambda=0\}.$ Crucially, $F(n)$ is a free $\mathcal{O}_K/\pi^n\mathcal{O}_K$-module of rank $1$ — just like $\mu_{p^n}\cong\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$.

Corollary (5.3) — the automorphism dictionary

Sending $a\mapsto[a]_F$ gives $\mathcal{O}_K/\pi^n\mathcal{O}_K\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{O}_K}(F(n))$ and $U_K/U_K^{(n)}\cong\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{O}_K}(F(n)).$ Compare $(\mathbb{Z}/p^n)^\times\cong\operatorname{Aut}(\mu_{p^n})$ — same shape, with $U_K/U_K^{(n)}$ in place of $(\mathbb{Z}/p^n)^\times$.

Theorem (5.4) — Lubin–Tate extensions

Define $L_n=K(F(n))$, the field of $\pi^n$-division points. It depends only on $\pi$, not on the chosen module. Then $L_n\mid K$ is totally ramified abelian of degree $q^{n-1}(q-1)$ with $G(L_n\mid K)\cong\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{O}_K}(F(n))\cong U_K/U_K^{(n)}.$ Concretely, for $\sigma\in G(L_n\mid K)$ there is a unique unit class $u$ with $\lambda^\sigma=[u]_F(\lambda)$. A division point $\lambda_n\in F(n)\setminus F(n-1)$ is a prime element of $L_n$ (Eisenstein!), and $N_{L_n\mid K}(-\lambda_n)=\pi.$

It really is cyclotomic over Q_p

Take $K=\mathbb{Q}_p,\ F=\widehat{\mathbb{G}}_m$. Then $e_n(X)=(1+X)^{p^n}-1$, whose roots are $\zeta-1$ for $p^n$-th roots of unity $\zeta$. So $F(n)=\{\zeta-1\}$ and $L_n=\mathbb{Q}_p(\mu_{p^n})$ exactly. Lubin–Tate theory is cyclotomic theory in this case, and a fresh, axiomatic theory everywhere else.

Theorem (5.5) — explicit norm residue symbol

For $a=u\,\pi^{v_K(a)}\in K^\times$ with $u\in U_K$, the norm residue symbol on division points is

$$(a,L_n\mid K)\,\lambda=[u^{-1}]_F(\lambda),\qquad \lambda\in F(n).$$

This is theorem (2.4) reborn in full generality: the unit part acts through its inverse $[u^{-1}]$, the prime-element part contributes Frobenius on the unramified side. Over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ it collapses back to $\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{u^{-1}}$.

Corollaries (5.6)–(5.7) — class fields and local Kronecker–Weber, generalized

$L_n$ is the class field of the group $\langle\pi\rangle\times U_K^{(n)}$. Taking the union $L_\pi=\bigcup_n L_n$ and combining with the maximal unramified extension $K^{\mathrm{nr}}$, $K^{\mathrm{ab}}=K^{\mathrm{nr}}\cdot L_\pi.$ Every finite abelian extension of $K$ is the unramified part times a Lubin–Tate part — the exact analogue of "all abelian extensions of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ come from roots of unity". (One subtlety: $L_\pi$ depends on $\pi$, but the compositum $K^{\mathrm{ab}}$ does not.)

5.6 Higher Ramification Groups

The norm residue symbol relates two filtered groups. On the left, $K^\times$ carries the chain of higher unit groups; on the right, $G(L\mid K)$ carries the chain of ramification groups. The main theorem of this section says the symbol matches the two filtrations exactly.

The two filtrations

On the unit side: $K^\times\supseteq U_K=U_K^{(0)}\supseteq U_K^{(1)}\supseteq U_K^{(2)}\supseteq\cdots$. On the Galois side, the upper-numbering ramification groups: $G(L\mid K)\supseteq G^0\supseteq G^1\supseteq G^2\supseteq\cdots$. Recall lower numbering $G_i=\{\sigma\mid v_L(\sigma\alpha-\alpha)\ge i+1\ \forall\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_L\}$, converted to upper numbering by the Herbrand function $\psi=\eta^{-1}$.

Worked instance — the Herbrand function for one Lubin–Tate $L_n\mid K$

The Herbrand function is the integral $\eta(s)=\int_0^s \frac{dx}{[G_0:G_x]}$ — it averages the "size" of the ramification groups as you walk up. Let us integrate it for the staircase that Proposition (6.1) below produces. There the lower-numbering groups are constant on the blocks $q^{k-1}-1\le x\le q^k-1$, with index $[G_0:G_x]=q^k$ there. So on the $k$-th block (of length $q^k-q^{k-1}$) the integrand is the constant $1/q^k$, contributing $\dfrac{q^k-q^{k-1}}{q^k}=1-\tfrac{1}{q}$ on the first block and $\tfrac{1}{q^{k-1}}\big(1-\tfrac1q\big)$ on later ones. Summing the geometric pieces up to $s=q^n-1$ telescopes to exactly $\eta(q^n-1)=n.$ That is the whole point: a lower jump sitting at the awkward value $q^k-1$ is carried by $\eta$ to the clean upper jump at the integer $k$. Integrating the staircase straightens the jumps onto the integers — which is exactly what Hasse–Arf will need.

Proposition (6.1) — ramification of Lubin–Tate extensions

For the field $L_n$ of $\pi^n$-division points, the lower-numbering groups are a clean staircase: $G_i(L_n\mid K)=G(L_n\mid L_k)\quad\text{for}\quad q^{k-1}-1\le i\le q^k-1.$ The proof computes $v_{L_n}(\lambda^\sigma-\lambda)$ directly using the explicit symbol (5.5). The bridge to ramification is the definition of the ramification number $i_{L_n\mid K}(\sigma)=v_{L_n}(\sigma\lambda-\lambda),$ and because the division point $\lambda$ is itself a uniformizer of $L_n$, this single valuation already computes the lower numbering (no need to range over all of $\mathcal{O}_{L_n}$ — a uniformizer generates everything). Concretely: if $\sigma\leftrightarrow u=1+s\pi^m$, then $i_{L_n\mid K}(\sigma)=q^m$ when $m<n$. The jumps sit at $i=q^k-1$.

Theorem (6.2) — the main theorem of higher ramification theory

For a finite abelian $L\mid K$, the norm residue symbol maps the higher unit group onto the upper-numbering ramification group:

$$(\,\cdot\,,L\mid K)\colon\quad U_K^{(n)}\ \twoheadrightarrow\ G^n(L\mid K),\qquad n\ge 0.$$

So the symbol is not just an isomorphism of groups — it is an isomorphism of filtered groups. The proof reduces to the totally ramified case, embeds $L$ into a Lubin–Tate extension $L_m$, and reads off the answer from (6.1) after converting lower to upper numbering via $\eta_{L\mid K}(q^n-1)=n$.

Why upper numbering

Upper numbering is the "right" indexing because it is compatible with quotients: it survives passage to subextensions, where lower numbering does not. That compatibility is exactly what lets the symbol — which is functorial in $L$ — line up with the upper filtration rather than the lower one. The unit filtration $U_K^{(n)}$ "speaks upper numbering" natively.

Proposition (6.3) — Hasse–Arf

For a finite abelian extension $L\mid K$, the jumps of the upper-numbering filtration $\{G^t(L\mid K)\}_{t\ge -1}$ occur at integers. (A "jump" at $t$ means $G^t\neq G^{t+\varepsilon}$ for every $\varepsilon>0$.)

Why Hasse–Arf falls out for free

Theorem (6.2) makes Hasse–Arf almost a corollary. Reduce to a Lubin–Tate extension $L_m$; by (6.1) its lower-numbering jumps are $q^n-1$, and the Herbrand function sends these to the integers $\eta(q^n-1)=n$. Since upper-numbering jumps are preserved under the abelian quotient $L_m\to L$, the jumps of $L\mid K$ are integers too. (The lone exception is $q=2$, where $0$ is not a jump.)

Hasse–Arf is not a curiosity: it is the input that makes the Artin conductor of an abelian character an integer, and so underwrites the theory of Artin $L$-functions in Chapter VII. The explicit Lubin–Tate computation of ramification is the cleanest route to it.

Summary

This chapter turned the abstract class field theory of Chapter IV into explicit local arithmetic. The thread:

  • 5.1 — The multiplicative group of a local field satisfies the class field axiom (via the Herbrand quotient of the units), so the abstract machine applies: a canonical $G(L\mid K)^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times$, the existence theorem, and the conductor detecting ramification.
  • 5.2 — Over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ the norm residue symbol is explicit: $(a,\mathbb{Q}_p(\zeta))\,\zeta=\zeta^{u^{-1}}$ on the ramified part, Frobenius powers on the unramified part. Proved transcendentally with Lubin–Tate series.
  • 5.3 — The Hilbert symbol pairs Kummer and class field theory. Tame case: a congruence mod $\mathfrak{p}$ generalizing Legendre/Euler. Wild case: explicit but transcendental (Brückner, Artin–Hasse, Iwasawa). The $n=2$ over $\mathbb{Q}_2$ case computed fully on four generators.
  • 5.4 — Formal groups and Lubin–Tate modules: $[\pi]\equiv X^q\bmod\pi$, classified up to isomorphism by their prime element.
  • 5.5 — Division points $F(n)$ generate totally ramified abelian extensions $L_n\mid K$ with $G(L_n\mid K)\cong U_K/U_K^{(n)}$, explicit symbol $[u^{-1}]_F$, and $K^{\mathrm{ab}}=K^{\mathrm{nr}}L_\pi$ — a Kronecker–Weber theorem for every local field.
  • 5.6 — The symbol is a filtration isomorphism: $U_K^{(n)}\twoheadrightarrow G^n(L\mid K)$, from which Hasse–Arf (integer jumps for abelian extensions) follows.
The one slogan

If you remember one thing: prime elements correspond to Frobenius, and the unit part of $K^\times$ acts by its inverse. Every explicit formula in this chapter — the cyclotomic symbol $\zeta^{u^{-1}}$, the Lubin–Tate symbol $[u^{-1}]_F$, the matching of filtrations — is that single principle, written out.
