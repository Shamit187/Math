Chapter 03

Riemann–Roch Theory

Neukirch's "Arakelov chapter": treat a number field as a compact curve, the infinite primes as its points at infinity, and watch the classical Riemann–Roch theorem reappear as a statement about lattices in Minkowski space.

Neukirch Chapter III ~75 min reading time Prereqs: Ch. I (ideals, Minkowski theory), Ch. II (valuations, completions)

The big analogy you should keep in your head

This chapter is dominated by a single guiding picture. In the geometry of compact Riemann surfaces (or smooth projective curves), a divisor is a finite formal combination of points, a meromorphic function has zeros and poles, and the celebrated Riemann–Roch theorem relates the dimension of the space of functions with prescribed pole behaviour to the degree of a divisor and the genus of the surface. Neukirch's goal is to set up number theory so that exactly the same theorems hold, with the number field $K$ playing the role of the curve.

Intuition

Think of $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{O})$ as a curve. Here $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{O})$ just means the set of prime ideals of $\mathcal{O}$, pictured as the points of a geometric space — so the dictionary "points of the curve = prime ideals" is literally the definition, not a metaphor. Its "points" are exactly those prime ideals — but a finite curve has no points at infinity, and that is what makes $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{O})$ feel non-compact. The fix is to throw in the archimedean (infinite) primes, coming from the embeddings $K \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$. These behave like points at infinity, and adding them "compactifies" the curve. That single move is the heart of Arakelov's idea.

The price of admission is that the infinite primes carry real data, not integer data. So divisors will have real coefficients at infinity, dimensions of "spaces of sections" become real-valued logarithms of point counts, and the genus becomes a real number. Once you accept that, every classical formula survives. Let us build the dictionary carefully.

3.1 Primes

We start over from the valuation-theoretic viewpoint of Chapter II. A prime (or place) $\mathfrak{p}$ of a number field $K$ is an equivalence class of valuations. The nonarchimedean classes are the finite primes (the usual prime ideals of $\mathcal{O}$); the archimedean ones are the infinite primes.

Definition

An infinite prime comes from an embedding $\tau : K \to \mathbb{C}$. It is real if the image lies in $\mathbb{R}$ (so the completion $K_\mathfrak{p}$ is $\mathbb{R}$), and complex if it comes from a conjugate pair of non-real embeddings (so $K_\mathfrak{p}$ is $\mathbb{C}$). We write $\mathfrak{p} \mid \infty$ for infinite primes and $\mathfrak{p} \nmid \infty$ for finite ones.

To every prime we attach a normalized homomorphism $v_\mathfrak{p} : K^* \to \mathbb{R}$. For a finite prime it is the usual exponential valuation normalized so that $v_\mathfrak{p}(K^*) = \mathbb{Z}$. For an infinite prime defined by $\tau$ we set

$$v_\mathfrak{p}(a) = -\log|\tau a|.$$

The genius of the normalization is uniformity. Define the absolute value $|\cdot|_\mathfrak{p}$ by $|a|_\mathfrak{p} = \mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{p})^{-v_\mathfrak{p}(a)}$. Then for a real prime $|a|_\mathfrak{p} = |\tau a|$, while for a complex prime $|a|_\mathfrak{p} = |\tau a|^2$ — the square is deliberate, and it is exactly what makes the product formula clean.

Intuition — why the complex place counts twice

A complex place is a single place, but it secretly packages two embeddings — a conjugate pair $\tau,\bar\tau$ that give the same absolute value. The square $|\tau a|^2$ is really $|\tau a|\cdot|\bar\tau a|$, one factor per hidden embedding. So when the product formula sums "zeros minus poles" over all places, a complex place automatically contributes its fair share of two. Concretely on $\mathbb{Q}$ every infinite place is real (just $|\cdot|_\infty$, no square), which is why the bookkeeping in the example below is so simple; a field like $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ with a complex place would carry the square.

Pause

Neukirch suggests we picture $e$ as an "infinite prime number": the residue degree of $\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R}$ is set to $2$ and the extension is declared unramified. Recall the vocabulary from Chapters I–II: an infinite prime is one of the archimedean places coming from an embedding into $\mathbb{C}$ (as opposed to a finite prime ideal); the residue degree measures how much the residue field grows, here the degree of $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{R}$; and a prime is ramified if it "thickens" in the extension (ramification index above 1) and unramified otherwise. Do not over-interpret these labels at infinity — they are pure bookkeeping, chosen so the later formulas come out right; in §3.3 we will see the choice is forced on us by the Riemann–Hurwitz formula.

Example — the product formula on Q

Take $K = \mathbb{Q}$ and the number $a = 12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$. Then $|12|_2 = \tfrac14$, $|12|_3 = \tfrac13$, the ordinary absolute value $|12|_\infty = 12$, and every other place gives $1$. The product is $\tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac13 \cdot 12 = 1$. The finite primes record the "zeros and poles", the infinite prime balances the books.

Definition — Product formula (1.3)

For every $a \in K^*$ one has $|a|_\mathfrak{p} = 1$ for almost all $\mathfrak{p}$, and

$$\prod_{\mathfrak{p}} |a|_\mathfrak{p} = 1.$$

It says: a nonzero number has, counted correctly across all places including infinity, "as many zeros as poles". This is the arithmetic shadow of the fact that a meromorphic function on a compact surface has equal numbers of zeros and poles.

Replete ideals and replete divisors

To do geometry we must enlarge fractional ideals to include the infinite primes. A replete ideal (Neukirch's term; others say Arakelov divisor in multiplicative form) is an element of

$$\widehat{J}(\mathcal{O}) = J(\mathcal{O}) \times \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid\infty} \mathbb{R}_+^{*}.$$

Here $J(\mathcal{O})$ is the group of fractional ideals of $\mathcal{O}$ — recall a fractional ideal is just an ordinary ideal allowed to have denominators (like $\tfrac12\mathbb{Z}$ inside $\mathbb{Q}$), and these form a group under multiplication. And $\mathbb{R}_+^{*}$ denotes the multiplicative group of positive real numbers, one copy for each infinite place. So $\widehat{J}(\mathcal{O})$ is "finite ideals, decorated with a positive real at each place at infinity."

So a replete ideal is an ordinary fractional ideal $\mathfrak{a}_f$ together with a choice of positive real number at each infinite place — the "archimedean component" $\mathfrak{a}_\infty$. Writing $\mathfrak{p}^v := e^v$, every replete ideal has a unique product representation

$$\mathfrak{a} = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}} \mathfrak{p}^{v_\mathfrak{p}}, \qquad v_\mathfrak{p}\in\mathbb{Z}\ (\mathfrak{p}\nmid\infty),\quad v_\mathfrak{p}\in\mathbb{R}\ (\mathfrak{p}\mid\infty).$$

The additive version is the replete divisor (or Arakelov divisor): a formal sum $D = \sum_\mathfrak{p} v_\mathfrak{p}\,\mathfrak{p}$ with integer coefficients at finite primes and real coefficients at infinite primes, almost all zero. These form the group

$$\widehat{\mathrm{Div}}(\mathcal{O}) = \mathrm{Div}(\mathcal{O}) \oplus \bigoplus_{\mathfrak{p}\mid\infty}\mathbb{R}\mathfrak{p},$$

which carries a natural topology (discrete on the finite part, real on the infinite part) making it a locally compact group — a crucial improvement over the purely discrete classical divisor group.

Intuition

Why allow real coefficients at infinity? Because the archimedean "order of vanishing" $-\log|\tau f|$ of a function is a real number, not an integer. The space of divisors must be large enough to contain all principal divisors $\mathrm{div}(f)$, and those have real archimedean parts. Allowing real coefficients is the cost of compactification.

The map $\mathrm{div}: K^* \to \widehat{\mathrm{Div}}(\mathcal{O})$, $\mathrm{div}(f) = \sum_\mathfrak{p} v_\mathfrak{p}(f)\,\mathfrak{p}$, has kernel exactly the roots of unity $\mu(K)$, and its image $\widehat{P}(\mathcal{O})$ is a discrete subgroup. The proof is precisely Dirichlet's unit theorem in disguise: the image of the units lands in the trace-zero hyperplane as a complete lattice.

Definition — replete (Arakelov) class group (1.10)

The quotient $\widehat{\mathrm{CH}}^1(\mathcal{O}) = \widehat{\mathrm{Div}}(\mathcal{O})/\widehat{P}(\mathcal{O})$ is the replete divisor class group (Arakelov class group). It is a locally compact Hausdorff group — the correct analogue of the divisor class group of a function field.

On a function field the divisor class group maps onto $\mathbb{Z}$ by the degree. Here we get a degree map onto $\mathbb{R}$:

$$\deg(D) = \sum_\mathfrak{p} v_\mathfrak{p}\,\log\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{p}) = \log\Big(\textstyle\prod_\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{p})^{v_\mathfrak{p}}\Big).$$

By the product formula, $\deg(\mathrm{div}(f)) = 0$, so the degree descends to $\widehat{\mathrm{CH}}^1(\mathcal{O}) \to \mathbb{R}$. Its kernel $\widehat{\mathrm{CH}}^1(\mathcal{O})^0$ sits in an exact sequence built from the unit lattice and the ideal class group.

Theorem (1.12) — compactness

The degree-zero part $\widehat{\mathrm{CH}}^1(\mathcal{O})^0$ is compact.

If this looks abstract, anchor it on $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ (worked out in the example just below): for $K=\mathbb{Q}$ there are no nontrivial units and class number one, so this compact kernel collapses to a single point. The content of the theorem is that for a general number field the kernel stays compact even though it is now a genuine torus times a finite group.

Pause — why this is profound

This single compactness statement contains the two pillars of classical algebraic number theory. The exact sequence is

$$0 \to H/\Gamma \to \widehat{\mathrm{CH}}^1(\mathcal{O})^0 \to \mathrm{CH}^1(\mathcal{O}) \to 0.$$

Let us unpack the two ends. The unit lattice $\Gamma$ is the image of the units $\mathcal{O}^*$ under the logarithmic embedding $u\mapsto(\log|\sigma u|)_\sigma$ — taking logs turns the multiplicative units into a genuine lattice (a grid of points) sitting inside the real vector space $H$, which is the trace-zero hyperplane. Dirichlet's unit theorem says this lattice is complete: it spans $H$ with finite covolume, leaving no direction unfilled. Now the quotient $H/\Gamma$ is "the plane modulo a full grid", and quotienting a real vector space by a complete lattice always yields a compact torus (the way $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is a circle, or $\mathbb{R}^2/\mathbb{Z}^2$ a doughnut) — so its compactness is Dirichlet's theorem. At the other end, the finiteness of $\mathrm{CH}^1(\mathcal{O})$ (the ideal class group) is finiteness of the class number. A compact torus extended by a finite group is compact, so both classical theorems merge into "the kernel of the degree map is compact".

Example — the curve $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$

For $K = \mathbb{Q}$ there is one infinite prime and class number one. A replete divisor is $\sum_{p} n_p\,p + x\,\infty$ with $n_p\in\mathbb{Z}$, $x\in\mathbb{R}$. The degree-zero condition cuts out a copy of $\mathbb{R}/0$ after killing principal divisors, and the Arakelov class group is just $\mathbb{R}$ via the degree — Neukirch's Exercise 3 says $\mathfrak{N}: \widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathbb{Z}) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathbb{R}_+^*$.

3.2 Different and Discriminant

Before pushing the analogy further we need the two invariants that measure ramification in an extension $L\mid K$: the different (an ideal of $\mathcal{O}$ upstairs) and the discriminant (an ideal of $\mathcal{o}$ downstairs). Geometrically the different will be the "ramification divisor" of the covering of curves, the analogue of the branch locus.

Everything flows from the trace form $T(x,y) = \mathrm{Tr}_{L\mid K}(xy)$, a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form on $L$. To any fractional ideal $\mathfrak{A}$ of $L$ it associates the dual module

$$\mathfrak{A}^{*} = \{\, x \in L \mid \mathrm{Tr}_{L\mid K}(x\mathfrak{A}) \subseteq \mathcal{o} \,\}.$$
Definition (2.1) — the different

The dual of the whole ring, $\mathfrak{C}_{\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o}} = \mathcal{O}^{*} = \{x\in L : \mathrm{Tr}(x\mathcal{O})\subseteq\mathcal{o}\}$, is Dedekind's complementary module (the inverse different). Its inverse

$$\mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K} = \mathfrak{C}_{\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o}}^{-1}$$

is the different of $\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o}$. Since $\mathfrak{C}_{\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o}}\supseteq\mathcal{O}$, the different is an honest integral ideal of $\mathcal{O}$.

The different is well-behaved: it is multiplicative in towers ($\mathfrak{D}_{M\mid K} = \mathfrak{D}_{M\mid L}\,\mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K}$), it localizes, and it is compatible with completion, so it can be computed prime by prime: $\mathfrak{D} = \prod_{\mathfrak{P}}\mathfrak{D}_\mathfrak{P}$.

Definition — Dedekind's element-different

If $\alpha\in\mathcal{O}$ with $L = K(\alpha)$ and minimal polynomial $f$, define the different of the element by $\delta_{L\mid K}(\alpha) = f'(\alpha)$. In the monogenic case $\mathcal{O} = \mathcal{o}[\alpha]$ one has the clean formula $\mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K} = (f'(\alpha))$ (2.4). In general (2.5) the different is the gcd of all element-differents $f'(\alpha)$.

Example — $\mathbb{Z}[i]$

Here $\alpha = i$ has minimal polynomial $X^2+1$, so $f'(i) = 2i$ and the different is $\mathfrak{D} = (2i) = (2) = (1+i)^2$. The only ramified prime is $(1+i)$ above $2$, exactly as expected — $2$ is the unique ramified prime. The discriminant ideal is the norm of the different: $\mathfrak{d} = N_{\mathbb{Q}(i)\mid\mathbb{Q}}\big((2i)\big) = \big(|N(2i)|\big) = (4)$ — a one-line computation, since $N(2i)=(2i)(\overline{2i})=(2i)(-2i)=4$. So the discriminant ideal has norm $4$ (it carries no sign — an ideal is just $(4)$). The classical signed discriminant $d_{\mathbb{Q}(i)} = -4$ is a different gadget: a genuine integer whose sign records the number of complex places, and whose absolute value $|{-4}| = 4$ is exactly the norm of the ideal. So "4" and "$-4$" are not in conflict — one is $\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{d})$, the other the signed integer.

Theorem (2.6) — different detects ramification

A prime $\mathfrak{P}$ of $L$ is ramified over $K$ iff $\mathfrak{P}\mid\mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K}$. If $\mathfrak{P}^s\,\|\,\mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K}$ and $e$ is the ramification index, then $s = e-1$ when $\mathfrak{P}$ is tamely ramified, and $e \le s \le e-1+v_\mathfrak{P}(e)$ when wildly ramified.

Intuition — the Kähler differentials picture (2.7)

The different equals the annihilator of the module of differentials $\Omega_{\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o}}$. (Optional/advanced — you may skip the homological details on a first pass.) In plain terms, $\Omega$ is an algebraic gadget built from formal symbols $dx$, one for each element $x$, subject to the usual rules of calculus ($d(x+y)=dx+dy$, the product rule, and $dc=0$ for constants): it records "infinitesimal variation" purely algebraically, with no limits. That is the bridge to geometry: differentials measure how the covering bends, and their "support" is precisely the ramification divisor. In a curve, that support is the branch locus; the different is its arithmetic incarnation.

Definition (2.8) & Theorem (2.9) — the discriminant

The discriminant $\mathfrak{d}_{L\mid K}$ is the ideal of $\mathcal{o}$ generated by the discriminants $d(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ of all integral bases. It is the norm of the different:

$$\mathfrak{d}_{L\mid K} = N_{L\mid K}(\mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K}).$$

Consequently (2.12) a prime $\mathfrak{p}$ ramifies in $L$ iff $\mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{d}$; the extension is unramified iff $\mathfrak{d} = (1)$.

Combining the discriminant with Minkowski's lattice-point theorem yields two jewels of classical theory, both about the impossibility of "too little" ramification.

Two classical consequences

Hermite (2.16): there are only finitely many number fields with bounded discriminant. Minkowski (2.17): every number field $K\ne\mathbb{Q}$ has $|d_K| > 1$. Hence (2.18): $\mathbb{Q}$ admits no nontrivial unramified extension at all.

Intuition

The bound underlying these is $\sqrt{|d_K|} \ge \frac{n^n}{n!}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^s$, and by Stirling the right side grows with the degree $n$. So a field of large degree is forced to ramify a lot — there is no room for an everywhere-unramified cover of $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$. This is the arithmetic version of "the projective line is simply connected" and was the model for Fontaine's and Faltings's higher-dimensional theorems.

3.3 Riemann–Roch

Now the payoff. Recall the classical setup: on a compact Riemann surface $X$ a divisor $D$ gives a line bundle $\mathcal{O}(D)$ whose global sections form $H^0(X,\mathcal{O}(D)) = \{f : \mathrm{ord}_P(f)\ge -v_P\}$. Riemann–Roch computes the Euler characteristic

$$\chi(\mathcal{O}(D)) = \dim H^0(X,\mathcal{O}(D)) - \dim H^1(X,\mathcal{O}(D)) = \deg(D) + 1 - g.$$

We want the arithmetic mirror. To a replete divisor $D$ with replete ideal $\mathfrak{a} = \mathcal{O}(D)$ we attach the finite set of "global sections"

$$H^0(\mathfrak{a}) = \{\, f \in K^* \mid v_\mathfrak{p}(f) \ge v_\mathfrak{p}\ \text{for all }\mathfrak{p} \,\}.$$
Warning

$H^0(\mathfrak{a})$ is not a vector space — it is a finite set (lattice points inside a box), and there is no honest $H^1$ at all in arithmetic. (Why not? On a curve, $H^1$ measures higher-dimensional cohomology, but $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{O})$ is 0-dimensional and affine — it is just a discrete set of primes — so there is simply no room for a nonzero $H^1$ to live.) The whole trick of this section is to not attack $H^0$ directly, but to define a Euler characteristic by measuring volumes of lattices, where everything still makes sense.

The Euler–Minkowski characteristic

Refresher — Minkowski space and covolume

From Chapter I: Minkowski space $K_\mathbb{R}$ is the real vector space $\mathbb{R}^r\times\mathbb{C}^s$ into which $K$ is embedded by sending each element to the tuple of all its real and complex embeddings $x\mapsto(\sigma x)_\sigma$. Under this embedding the ring of integers (and any ideal) lands as a complete lattice — a regular grid of points that spans the whole space. The covolume is the volume of one fundamental cell of that grid (the "mesh"): roughly, how much room each lattice point occupies. Baby case: for $K=\mathbb{Q}$ there is one real embedding, Minkowski space is just $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}$ is the lattice of integer points with covolume $1$ (each integer "owns" a unit interval).

Each replete ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ determines a complete lattice in Minkowski space $K_\mathbb{R}$: take the lattice of the finite ideal $\mathfrak{a}_f$ and scale each archimedean coordinate by the factor coming from $\mathfrak{a}_\infty$. Let $\mathrm{vol}(\mathfrak{a})$ be the covolume of a fundamental mesh.

Definition (3.1)

The Euler–Minkowski characteristic of $\mathfrak{a}$ is

$$\chi(\mathfrak{a}) = -\log\,\mathrm{vol}(\mathfrak{a}).$$

It depends only on the class of $\mathfrak{a}$ in $\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathcal{O})$ (proof: a principal ideal scales the lattice by a map of determinant $1$, by the product formula).

The key computation comes from Chapter I's volume formula $\mathrm{vol}(\mathfrak{a}) = \sqrt{|d_K|}\,\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a})$ (3.3). Writing the degree as $\deg(\mathfrak{a}) = -\log\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a})$ and $\chi(\mathcal{O}) = -\log\sqrt{|d_K|}$, we immediately get the first Riemann–Roch formula:

$$\chi(\mathfrak{a}) = \deg(\mathfrak{a}) + \chi(\mathcal{O}).$$
Pause — compare line by line

Function field: $\chi(\mathcal{O}(D)) = \deg(D) + \chi(\mathcal{O}_X)$ with $\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = 1-g$. Number field: $\chi(\mathfrak{a}) = \deg(\mathfrak{a}) + \chi(\mathcal{O})$ with $\chi(\mathcal{O}) = -\log\sqrt{|d_K|}$. The discriminant plays the role of the genus.

The genus of a number field

To split $\chi(\mathcal{O})$ into "$1-g$" we need an arithmetic analogue of $\dim H^0$. Since $H^0(\mathfrak{a})$ is finite, count it: set $\ell(\mathfrak{a}) = 0$ if empty, and otherwise

$$\ell(\mathfrak{a}) = \log\frac{\#H^0(\mathfrak{a})}{\mathrm{vol}(W)},$$

where $\mathrm{vol}(W) = 2^r(2\pi)^s$ is the normalizing volume of the unit polydisc. With this normalization $H^0(\mathcal{O}) = \mu(K)$ and one defines:

Definition (3.5) — genus (after Weil, 1939)

The genus of a number field $K$ is

$$g = \ell(\mathcal{O}) - \chi(\mathcal{O}) = \log\frac{\#\mu(K)}{2^r(2\pi)^s} + \log\sqrt{|d_K|}.$$

One checks $g_{\mathbb{Q}} = 0$, mirroring the genus-zero projective line. Let us actually verify it: for $\mathbb{Q}$ there is one real place and no complex places, so $r=1,\,s=0$; the roots of unity are just $\pm1$, so $\#\mu(\mathbb{Q})=2$; and the discriminant is $d_{\mathbb{Q}}=1$. Plugging in,

$$g_{\mathbb{Q}} = \log\frac{2}{2^1(2\pi)^0} + \log\sqrt{1} = \log\frac{2}{2} + 0 = \log 1 = 0.$$

The normalizing factor $2^r(2\pi)^s$ is engineered precisely so this comes out to $0$. The Riemann–Roch formula then reads $\chi(\mathfrak{a}) = \deg(\mathfrak{a}) + \ell(\mathcal{O}) - g$ (3.6).

The strong Riemann–Roch theorem

The classical strong form, $\ell(D) - \ell(\mathcal{K}-D) = \deg(D) + 1 - g$, uses Serre duality to identify $H^1$ with sections of the canonical bundle. The arithmetic analogue rests on a deep counting theorem of Serge Lang, an asymptotic Serre duality.

Theorem (3.7, S. Lang)

As $\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a})\to\infty$,

$$\#H^0(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}) = \frac{2^r(2\pi)^s}{\sqrt{|d_K|}}\,\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a}) + O\!\big(\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a})^{1-1/n}\big).$$

The proof is a careful lattice-point count: the leading term is "volume of the box / covolume of the lattice", and the error is bounded by counting boundary cells, using the mean value theorem to control how the parametrizing map distorts diameters.

Theorem (3.9) — Riemann–Roch

For every replete divisor $D$, with index of specialty $i(D) = \ell(D) - \chi(D)$,

$$\ell(D) = \deg(D) + \ell(\mathcal{O}) - g + i(D),$$

and the correction is exponentially small: $i(D) = O\big(e^{-\frac{1}{n}\deg(D)}\big)$, so $i(D)\to 0$ as $\deg(D)\to\infty$.

Intuition

In the curve case the "index of specialty" $\ell(\mathcal{K}-D)$ vanishes once $\deg D > 2g-2$. Here it does not vanish exactly but decays exponentially — a genuinely analytic flavor that has no counterpart over a finite field. This is the "approximate" nature of arithmetic Serre duality.

Canonical module and Riemann–Hurwitz

Definition (3.10) — canonical module

The canonical module of $K$ is $\omega_K = \mathfrak{C}_{K\mid\mathbb{Q}} = \mathrm{Hom}_\mathbb{Z}(\mathcal{O},\mathbb{Z})$ — the inverse different of $K\mid\mathbb{Q}$. It is the dualizing object, playing the role of the canonical line bundle $\omega = \Omega^1_X$.

Proposition (3.12)

Its degree is $\deg\omega_K = -2\chi(\mathcal{O}) = 2g - 2\ell(\mathcal{O}) = \log|d_K|$. The degree of the canonical bundle is the logarithm of the discriminant — the cleanest possible statement of "discriminant = genus".

Proposition (3.13) — Riemann–Hurwitz

For a finite extension $L\mid K$, since $\omega_L = \mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K}\,\omega_K$,

$$g_L - \ell(\mathcal{O}_L) = [L\!:\!K]\big(g_K - \ell(\mathcal{O}_K)\big) + \tfrac12\deg\mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K}.$$

The different appears as the ramification term — precisely as the branch divisor enters the Riemann–Hurwitz formula for a covering of Riemann surfaces.

Pause — why $\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R}$ "must" be unramified

Riemann–Hurwitz keeps its shape only if the infinite primes contribute nothing to the different — forcing us to declare $\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R}$ unramified. Neukirch then shows this was secretly a choice of metric on Minkowski space. With the "Minkowski metric" instead of the "canonical metric", $\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R}$ becomes ramified with $e_\mathfrak{p}=[K_\mathfrak{p}:\mathbb{R}]$. This leads to metrized number fields $\overline{K}=(K,\langle\,,\rangle_K)$, and Q is replaced by a whole continuous family $(\mathbb{Q},\alpha xy)$, all of genus 0 (3.14).

3.4 Metrized o-Modules

The replete-ideal theory of §3.3 is rank-1. To see its true nature — and to reach Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch — we promote everything to finitely generated modules carrying metrics. This is linear algebra over $\mathcal{o}$ "compactified at infinity".

The basic object is the complexification ring $K_\mathbb{C} = K\otimes_\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{C}$. Writing $X(\mathbb{C}) = \mathrm{Hom}(K,\mathbb{C})$, it decomposes as

$$K_\mathbb{C} = \bigoplus_{\sigma\in X(\mathbb{C})}\mathbb{C} \;=\; \mathrm{Hom}(X(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{C}).$$
Intuition — what tensoring with ℂ does

Tensoring with $\mathbb{C}$ takes the single field $K$ and splits it into one copy of $\mathbb{C}$ per embedding. Concretely $\mathbb{Q}(i)\otimes_\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ — the two factors are the two embeddings $i\mapsto+i$ and $i\mapsto-i$. (The minimal polynomial $X^2+1$ factors over $\mathbb{C}$ as $(X-i)(X+i)$, and each linear factor is one copy of $\mathbb{C}$.) That is exactly the direct sum $\bigoplus_\sigma\mathbb{C}$: coordinates indexed by the embeddings $\sigma$.

The complex conjugation $F_\infty$ on the embeddings induces an involution $F$ on $K_\mathbb{C}$, given by $(Fx)(\sigma)=\overline{x(\bar\sigma)}$. Neukirch calls $F$ the Frobenius correspondence — the name comes from the analogy that at a finite prime the Frobenius generates the decomposition group, and here complex conjugation is the decomposition group of the place at infinity (the Galois group of $\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R}$, which is exactly $\{1,\text{conjugation}\}$). So $F$ is the avatar at infinity of the Frobenius, consistent with treating $\mathbb{C}\mid\mathbb{R}$ as unramified.

Definition (4.1 & 4.2) — metrized o-module

For a finitely generated $\mathcal{o}$-module $M$, put $M_\mathbb{C} = M\otimes_\mathcal{o}K_\mathbb{C}$. A hermitian metric on $M_\mathbb{C}$ is a sesquilinear positive-definite form $\langle\,,\rangle_M : M_\mathbb{C}\times M_\mathbb{C}\to K_\mathbb{C}$; it is F-invariant if $F\langle x,y\rangle_M = \langle Fx,Fy\rangle_M$. A metrized o-module $\overline{M}$ is such an $M$ with an F-invariant hermitian metric. Equivalently, a hermitian inner product on each complex piece $M_\sigma$, compatible under conjugation.

Example — replete ideals are metrized modules

A fractional ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ with the trivial metric $\langle x,y\rangle = x\bar y$ is metrized. More generally, a replete ideal $\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}_f\cdot\mathfrak{a}_\infty$ with $\mathfrak{a}_\infty=\prod\mathfrak{p}^{v_\mathfrak{p}}$ defines the function $\alpha(\sigma)=e^{2v_{\mathfrak{p}_\sigma}}$ and the metric $\langle x,y\rangle_\alpha = \bigoplus e^{2v_{\mathfrak{p}_\sigma}}x_\sigma\bar y_\sigma$. The resulting rank-1 metrized module is written $L(\mathfrak{a})$. So the rank-1 metrized modules are exactly the replete ideals.

Proposition (4.5) — dictionary
  • $L(\mathfrak{a})\cong L(\mathfrak{b})$ iff $\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{b}[a]$ for a replete principal ideal;
  • every invertible metrized $\mathcal{o}$-module is some $L(\mathfrak{a})$;
  • $L(\mathfrak{ab})=L(\mathfrak{a})\otimes L(\mathfrak{b})$, $L(\mathfrak{a}^{-1})=\overline{L(\mathfrak{a})}$.

Metrized modules form a category closed under $\oplus,\otimes,(\cdot)^\vee,\bigwedge^n$, with the natural induced metrics (e.g. $\langle x_1\wedge\cdots,y_1\wedge\cdots\rangle = \det(\langle x_i,y_j\rangle)$). The projective metrized modules are the analogue of vector bundles; over a Dedekind domain projective = torsion-free = locally free, and every projective module is $\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathcal{o}^n$ (4.3).

Definition (4.6) — admissible exact sequence & determinant

A short exact sequence of metrized modules must split isometrically after complexifying. The determinant of a projective metrized module of rank $n$ is its top exterior power $\det\overline{M}=\bigwedge^n\overline{M}$, an invertible metrized module. Crucially (4.7), for an admissible $0\to\overline{M}'\to\overline{M}\to\overline{M}''\to 0$ there is a canonical isometry

$$\det\overline{M}'\otimes\det\overline{M}'' \;\cong\; \det\overline{M}.$$

This determinant-is-multiplicative property is the technical engine for everything that follows.

3.5 Grothendieck Groups

We now manufacture an abelian group (in fact a ring) that records all metrized modules up to the relation "the middle is the sum of the ends". This is Grothendieck's universal construction.

Definition (5.1) — replete Grothendieck groups

Let $F_0(\mathcal{o})$ (resp. $F^0(\mathcal{o})$) be the free abelian group on isometry classes of projective (resp. coherent, i.e. arbitrary f.g.) metrized modules. Quotient by the subgroup generated by $\{M'\}-\{M\}+\{M''\}$ from admissible exact sequences. The results

$$\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o}),\qquad \widehat{K}^0(\mathcal{o})$$

are the replete (compactified) Grothendieck groups. In them, an exact sequence becomes the additive relation $[\overline{M}]=[\overline{M}']+[\overline{M}'']$.

Tensor product makes $\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})$ a commutative ring (unit $[\mathcal{O}]$), and $\widehat{K}^0(\mathcal{o})$ a module over it. The natural map $\widehat{K}_0\to\widehat{K}^0$ — including projectives into all coherent modules — is the Poincaré homomorphism.

Theorem (5.4)

The Poincaré homomorphism $\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})\xrightarrow{\sim}\widehat{K}^0(\mathcal{o})$ is an isomorphism.

Intuition

Why care? Because the moment you do real work — quotients like $\mathcal{o}/\mathfrak{a}$ — you leave the projective world. The theorem says every coherent module already has a class in the ring $\widehat{K}_0$, computed from a "metrized projective resolution" $0\to\overline{E}\to\overline{F}\to\overline{M}\to 0$ via $[\overline{M}]=[\overline{F}]-[\overline{E}]$. A projective resolution is just the standard move of approximating an awkward module by a difference of two nice (projective) ones: write the bad module $\overline{M}$ as "a big nice module $\overline{F}$ minus the relations $\overline{E}$ among its generators," so its class becomes $[\overline{F}]-[\overline{E}]$. The two lemmas (5.2, 5.3) build such resolutions and show the recipe is well-defined by dominating any two resolutions with a fibre-product third.

Propositions (5.5–5.6) — connection to the Picard group

$[\mathfrak{a}]\mapsto[L(\mathfrak{a})]$ gives a homomorphism $\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathcal{o})\to\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})^*$, and $\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})$ is generated as an additive group by the classes $[\mathfrak{a}]$. So the whole ring is built from line bundles.

Proposition (5.7) — the key relation (Tamme)

For any two replete ideals,

$$([\mathfrak{a}]-1)\,([\mathfrak{b}]-1) = 0.$$

Equivalently $[\mathfrak{a}]+[\mathfrak{b}]=[\mathfrak{ab}]+1$. This is the metrized analogue of the fact that line bundles on a curve satisfy $c_1$-additivity with vanishing higher products — it makes the Chern theory of §3.6 completely explicit.

3.6 The Chern Character

Two homomorphisms organize $\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})$. First the rank (augmentation) $\mathrm{rk}:\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})\to\mathbb{Z}$, whose kernel $I=\ker(\mathrm{rk})$ is the augmentation ideal. By (5.7),

$$I^2 = 0 \qquad\text{(6.2)}.$$
Definition (6.3) — Chern character

Set $\mathrm{gr}\,\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o}) = \mathbb{Z}\oplus I$ with $I\cdot I=0$. The first Chern class is $c_1(\xi)=\xi-\mathrm{rk}(\xi)$, and the Chern character is

$$\mathrm{ch}(\xi) = \mathrm{rk}(\xi) + c_1(\xi).$$
Theorem (6.4 & 6.6) — explicit structure

The Chern character is a ring isomorphism. Why is $\det: I\xrightarrow{\sim}\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathcal{o})$ (6.5) an isomorphism? Because $I^2=0$: once products of augmentation-ideal classes vanish, a bundle has no invariants beyond its rank except its top exterior power — the determinant line. There is simply nothing else left to remember, so the augmentation ideal $I$ and the Picard group $\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathcal{o})$ of line bundles must coincide. Using this,

$$\mathrm{ch}: \widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o}) \xrightarrow{\sim} \mathbb{Z}\oplus\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathcal{o}),\qquad \mathrm{ch}(\xi)=\mathrm{rk}(\xi)\oplus\det(\xi).$$
Intuition — what we've actually proved

A metrized vector bundle on $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{o})$ is, up to $K$-theory, determined by exactly two invariants: its rank and its determinant line bundle. The right-hand side $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathcal{o})$ is the "replete Chow ring" $\widehat{\mathrm{CH}}(\mathcal{o})$, and $\mathrm{ch}$ is the realization map from $K$-theory to Chow theory — the very bridge Neukirch hinted at in §3.1's philosophical aside about $K_i(X)$ versus $\mathrm{CH}^i(X)$.

3.7 Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch

Finally we compare the Grothendieck groups of $K$ and a finite extension $L$, with rings of integers $\mathcal{o}\subset\mathcal{O}$. The inclusion $i:\mathcal{o}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{O}$ gives two maps:

  • Pullback $i^*:\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})\to\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{O})$, $\overline{M}\mapsto\overline{M}\otimes_\mathcal{o}\mathcal{O}$ (extend scalars; the metric extends sesquilinearly). A ring homomorphism.
  • Pushforward $i_*:\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{O})\to\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})$, restrict a metrized $\mathcal{O}$-module to an $\mathcal{o}$-module, taking orthogonal sums of metrics over $\tau\mid\sigma$. Only additive.

They satisfy the projection formula (7.1): $i_*(i^*x\cdot y)=x\cdot i_*y$. The Riemann–Roch problem is to compute $\mathrm{ch}(i_*\overline{M})$ from $\mathrm{ch}(\overline{M})$ — by (6.6) this means computing $\det(i_*\overline{M})$.

Theorem (7.2)

For a projective metrized $\mathcal{O}$-module $\overline{M}$:

$$\mathrm{rk}(i_*\overline{M}) = \mathrm{rk}(\overline{M})\,[L\!:\!K],\quad \det(i_*\overline{M}) = N_{L\mid K}(\det\overline{M})\otimes(\det i_*\mathcal{O})^{\mathrm{rk}(\overline{M})}.$$
Proposition (7.4) — the determinant of the trace

The mysterious correction term squares to the discriminant: there is a canonical isometry

$$(\det i_*\mathcal{O})^{\otimes 2} \cong \mathfrak{d}_{L\mid K},$$

via the trace pairing $(x,y)\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}_{L\mid K}(xy)$, whose image is by definition the discriminant ideal.

Reformulating in Grothendieck's language: $i_*$ induces a Gysin map $i_*:\mathrm{gr}\,\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{O})\to\mathrm{gr}\,\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})$, which on $\mathbb{Z}\oplus\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}$ is just $[L\!:\!K]\oplus N_{L\mid K}$ (7.5). The diagram relating $\mathrm{ch}\circ i_*$ and $i_*\circ\mathrm{ch}$ does not commute — and the failure is measured by the Todd class.

The moral, before the jargon

Strip away the vocabulary (Gysin map, Todd class, $f_!$, "categorified") and the idea is simple. We have the big curve $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{O})$ sitting over the small one $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{o})$, and we want to push a bundle down from big to small (that "pushing down" is the Gysin map, also written $f_!$). Pushing down distorts the invariants: the rank and determinant of the pushed-down bundle are not simply the images of the originals. The remarkable fact is that the distortion is universal — it does not depend on which bundle you push — and the single correction factor that fixes it is the Todd class. Even better, here that universal correction turns out to be nothing new: it is the different (the ramification of the covering) reappearing in yet another guise. Everything below just makes this precise.

Definition (7.6) — Todd class

Using the (coherent) module of differentials $\Omega_{\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o}}$, whose class lies in $I$,

$$\mathrm{Td}(\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o}) = 1 - \tfrac12[\Omega_{\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o}}] = \tfrac12\big(1+[\mathfrak{D}_{L\mid K}]\big) \in \mathrm{gr}\,\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{O})\otimes\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12].$$

The factor $\tfrac12$ is exactly the $\tfrac12\deg\mathfrak{D}$ that appeared in Riemann–Hurwitz (3.13) — the Todd class is Riemann–Hurwitz, categorified.

Theorem (7.7) — Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch

The corrected diagram commutes: for every $\xi\in\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{O})$,

$$\mathrm{ch}(i_*\xi) = i_*\big(\mathrm{Td}(\mathcal{O}\mid\mathcal{o})\cdot\mathrm{ch}(\xi)\big).$$
Pause — read the formula geometrically

This is literally Grothendieck's $\mathrm{ch}(f_!\,\cdot) = f_*(\mathrm{Td}_f\cdot\mathrm{ch}(\cdot))$ for the morphism of "curves" $f:\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{o})$. The "$\mathrm{ch}$ doesn't commute with pushforward; the discrepancy is the relative Todd class" — and here the relative tangent / cotangent is the different. Number theory has been swallowed whole by algebraic geometry.

3.8 The Euler–Minkowski Characteristic

The last section closes the loop: it shows the ad-hoc $\chi(\mathfrak{a})=-\log\mathrm{vol}(\mathfrak{a})$ of §3.3 is the special case $K\mid\mathbb{Q}$ of GRR, by viewing a rank-1 $\mathcal{o}$-module as a rank-$[K\!:\!\mathbb{Q}]$ metrized $\mathbb{Z}$-module.

Proposition / Definition (8.1)

The degree map $\deg_K([\mathfrak{a}])=-\log\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a})$ on $\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathcal{o})$ extends uniquely to the Euler–Minkowski characteristic

$$\chi_K = \deg\circ\det : \widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o}) \to \mathbb{R}.$$
Theorem (8.2) — Riemann–Roch via GRR

Applying $\deg_K$ to (7.2), for every coherent $\mathcal{O}$-module $\overline{M}$:

$$\chi_K(i_*\overline{M}) = \deg_L(\det\overline{M}) + \mathrm{rk}(\overline{M})\,\chi_K(i_*\mathcal{O}),$$

and for invertible $\overline{M}$ this is exactly $\chi_K(i_*\overline{M}) = \deg_L(\overline{M}) + \chi_K(i_*\mathcal{O})$ — Riemann–Roch for the covering.

Specializing to $K=\mathbb{Q}$: a metrized $\mathbb{Z}$-module is just a finitely generated abelian group with a euclidean metric on $M\otimes\mathbb{R}$. Since $\deg:\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathbb{Z})\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{R}$, the Euler–Minkowski characteristic $\chi=\deg\circ\det$ is fully explicit.

Proposition (8.3) — the master formula

For a coherent metrized $\mathbb{Z}$-module $M$,

$$\chi(M) = \log\#M_{\mathrm{tor}} - \log\mathrm{vol}(M/M_{\mathrm{tor}}).$$
Intuition — torsion vs covolume

Read this as the arithmetic Euler characteristic: a finite group contributes $+\log\#$ (like an $H^0$), while the lattice part contributes $-\log\mathrm{vol}$ (like an $H^1$ defect). The two pieces are the arithmetic $h^0-h^1$.

Proposition (8.4) — it all reconciles

Viewing the rank-1 metrized $\mathcal{o}$-module $L(\mathfrak{a})$ as a rank-$[K\!:\!\mathbb{Q}]$ metrized $\mathbb{Z}$-module $i_*L(\mathfrak{a})$,

$$\chi(\mathfrak{a}) = \chi(i_*L(\mathfrak{a})).$$

So the volume-defined Euler–Minkowski characteristic of §3.3 is precisely the determinant-degree of the pushed-forward $\mathbb{Z}$-module. The first Riemann–Roch formula $\chi(\mathfrak{a})=\deg(\mathfrak{a})+\chi(\mathcal{O})$ is the case $K\mid\mathbb{Q}$ of (8.2). The circle is closed.

Summary

This chapter built, brick by brick, a dictionary in which a number field is a compact curve and Riemann–Roch holds.

  • §3.1 Adjoin the infinite primes; replete ideals/divisors and the Arakelov class group $\widehat{\mathrm{CH}}^1(\mathcal{o})$, whose degree-zero part is compact — encoding both the unit theorem and finiteness of the class number.
  • §3.2 The different and discriminant measure ramification ($\mathfrak{d}=N(\mathfrak{D})$); Minkowski/Hermite show $\mathbb{Q}$ has no unramified extension.
  • §3.3 The Euler–Minkowski characteristic $\chi(\mathfrak{a})=-\log\mathrm{vol}(\mathfrak{a})$ gives $\chi(\mathfrak{a})=\deg(\mathfrak{a})+\chi(\mathcal{O})$; the discriminant is the genus, and Lang's lattice count gives the strong theorem.
  • §3.4–3.5 Metrized modules = vector bundles with metrics at infinity; their Grothendieck ring $\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})$, with the structural relation $([\mathfrak{a}]-1)([\mathfrak{b}]-1)=0$.
  • §3.6 The Chern character $\widehat{K}_0(\mathcal{o})\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\widehat{\mathrm{Pic}}(\mathcal{o})$: a bundle is its rank and its determinant.
  • §3.7 Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch $\mathrm{ch}(i_*\xi)=i_*(\mathrm{Td}\cdot\mathrm{ch}\,\xi)$, with the Todd class built from the different — Riemann–Hurwitz categorified.
  • §3.8 Everything reduces over $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\chi(M)=\log\#M_{\mathrm{tor}}-\log\mathrm{vol}(M/M_{\mathrm{tor}})$, recovering §3.3.
The one thing to remember

Add the archimedean places, replace integers by real volumes, and the geometry of curves becomes the arithmetic of number fields — letter for letter. The discriminant is the genus, the different is the ramification divisor, and Riemann–Roch counts lattice points.
