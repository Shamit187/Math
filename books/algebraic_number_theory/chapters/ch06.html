Chapter 06

Global Class Field Theory

The summit: we glue the local class field theories of every completion into one object — the idèle class group — and read off the abelian extensions of a number field, ending with Gauss reciprocity falling out for free.

Neukirch Chapter VI ~75 min reading time Prereqs: Ch. IV (abstract class field theory, Herbrand quotient), Ch. V (local class field theory), Dirichlet's unit theorem

Orientation: what is this chapter doing?

In local class field theory (Chapter V) the hero was the multiplicative group $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$ of a local field, and the punchline was a reciprocity isomorphism between $K_\mathfrak{p}^*/N L_\mathfrak{P}^*$ and the abelianized Galois group. The whole point of global class field theory is to do the same thing for a number field $K$ — to classify all abelian extensions of $K$ and describe how primes split — but a number field has infinitely many completions, one for each prime $\mathfrak{p}$, and the answer must somehow involve all of them at once.

The naïve guess — "use $K^*$ the way we used $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$" — fails. The reason is concrete: $K^*$ by itself carries no useful topology — it is not locally compact, and as a bare group it cannot "see" all the completions $K_\mathfrak{p}$ at once. The abstract machine of Chapter IV needs a locally compact group to run; bundling the local groups $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$ together is precisely what supplies that structure while keeping every completion in view. The right replacement is the idèle class group $C_K$. It is built by bundling together all the local groups $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$ into a single locally compact group (the idèle group), then quotienting by the diagonal copy of $K^*$. Almost the entire chapter is the verification that this object satisfies the class field axiom of Chapter IV — once it does, the abstract machine of Chapter IV hands us the reciprocity law, the existence theorem, and the classification, all at once.

The shape of the argument

The chapter is one long pincer movement on a single inequality. Compute the Herbrand quotient of $C_L$ (§3) to get $(C_K : N C_L) \ge n$. Then bound the other direction via Kummer theory (§4) to get $\le n$. Equality is the class field axiom, and from there Chapter IV does the rest (§5–§7). Section 8 is the victory lap: power-residue and quadratic reciprocity.

6.1 Idèles and Idèle Classes

The notion of idèle is a modification of the notion of ideal, introduced by Claude Chevalley to give a clean home for the local-to-global principle: the idea that a problem over $K$ should be reducible to the same problem over each completion $K_\mathfrak{p}$. The trick is to package all the completions into one object without making it absurdly large.

Adèle ring

An adèle of $K$ is a family $\alpha = (\alpha_\mathfrak{p})$ with $\alpha_\mathfrak{p} \in K_\mathfrak{p}$, where $\mathfrak{p}$ runs over all primes of $K$ (finite and infinite), subject to the restriction that $\alpha_\mathfrak{p} \in \mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}$ for almost all $\mathfrak{p}$ (all but finitely many). With componentwise addition and multiplication these form the adèle ring

$$\mathbb{A}_K = {\prod_\mathfrak{p}}' \, K_\mathfrak{p} \quad (\text{restricted product w.r.t. } \mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}).$$

The word "restricted" is doing all the work — and it is exactly what the prime on the product symbol ${\prod}'$ abbreviates: that apostrophe is shorthand for "all but finitely many components must be integral." If we took the full product $\prod_\mathfrak{p} K_\mathfrak{p}$ the result would be too big and would carry the wrong (non-locally-compact) topology. The full direct sum, by contrast, is too small — it would forget the integral structure. The restricted product is the Goldilocks object: it allows finitely many components to be "large" while forcing the rest to live in the compact rings of integers.

Idèle group

The idèle group is the unit group of the adèle ring, $I_K = \mathbb{A}_K^*$. Concretely an idèle is a family $\alpha = (\alpha_\mathfrak{p})$ with $\alpha_\mathfrak{p} \in K_\mathfrak{p}^*$ such that $\alpha_\mathfrak{p} \in \mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$ for almost all $\mathfrak{p}$. It is the restricted product

$$I_K = {\prod_\mathfrak{p}}' \, K_\mathfrak{p}^* \quad (\text{w.r.t. } \mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*).$$
A concrete idèle of $\mathbb{Q}$

Take $K=\mathbb{Q}$. The places of $\mathbb{Q}$ are the primes $2,3,5,7,\dots$ together with the one infinite (real) place. Consider the idèle $\alpha$ whose components are

$$\alpha_\infty = \sqrt{2}\in\mathbb{R}^*, \qquad \alpha_3 = \tfrac{1}{3}\in\mathbb{Q}_3^*, \qquad \alpha_p = 1 \ \ (p\ne 3).$$

This is a legitimate idèle: every component is a nonzero local number, and $\alpha_p\in\mathbb{Z}_p^*$ for all but one finite prime (here only the component at $3$ fails to be a unit, since $v_3(1/3)=-1$). Applying the idèle-to-ideal map $\alpha\mapsto\prod_p p^{v_p(\alpha_p)}$ reads off only the finite valuations and forgets the rest, giving the fractional ideal $3^{-1}=(\tfrac{1}{3})$. The archimedean datum $\sqrt 2$ at infinity is exactly the information an ideal throws away.

Why not just use the ideal class group?

An ideal records, for each finite prime, only an integer exponent $v_\mathfrak{p}(\alpha)$ — it has thrown away the unit part and all the archimedean information. The idèle remembers the whole local element $\alpha_\mathfrak{p}$ at every place, including the infinite ones. That extra data is exactly what's needed to see ramified and archimedean phenomena that the ideal class group is blind to.

For a finite set $S$ of primes containing all infinite places, the idèles that are units outside $S$ form the group of S-idèles

$$I_K^S = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S} K_\mathfrak{p}^* \times \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\notin S} \mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*,$$

and $I_K = \bigcup_S I_K^S$. Each $I_K^S$ is an honest (unrestricted) product because we've fixed where the "large" components are allowed to sit.

Principal idèles and the idèle class group

The diagonal embedding sends $a \in K^*$ to the idèle whose every component is $a$ (viewed in $K_\mathfrak{p}$ via $K \hookrightarrow K_\mathfrak{p}$). This is injective, and its image consists of the principal idèles. The quotient

$$C_K = I_K / K^*$$

is the idèle class group of $K$. The class of an idèle $\alpha$ is written $[\alpha]$.

That the diagonal $K^*$ makes sense as a subgroup is itself a theorem in disguise: it relies on the fact that a nonzero element of $K$ is a unit at almost all primes (it has only finitely many zeros and poles). So principal idèles automatically satisfy the restricted-product condition.

From idèles back to ideals

There is a surjection from idèles to fractional ideals, $\alpha \mapsto (\alpha) = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\nmid\infty} \mathfrak{p}^{v_\mathfrak{p}(\alpha_\mathfrak{p})}$, sending an idèle to "what ideal it looks like." It carries principal idèles to principal ideals, and so descends to a surjection $C_K \twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{Cl}_K$. This is the bridge between the new object and the classical one.

The dictionary, in one line

Killing the unit information (the components at infinite places and the unit parts at finite places) turns $C_K$ into $\mathrm{Cl}_K$. Precisely (Prop. 1.3), $\mathrm{Cl}_K = I_K / I_K^\infty K^*$, where $I_K^\infty = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid\infty}K_\mathfrak{p}^* \times \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\nmid\infty}\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$ collapses everything the ideal forgets.

A useful finiteness statement (Prop. 1.4): for a large enough finite set $S$, every idèle class is represented by an S-idèle, i.e. $I_K = I_K^S K^*$. This is the idèle-theoretic shadow of the finiteness of the class number: take $S$ to contain the finitely many primes dividing a set of ideal-class representatives.

Topology and the norm-one subgroup

The idèle group carries a canonical topology in which a basis of neighborhoods of $1$ is given by products $\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S} W_\mathfrak{p} \times \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\notin S}\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$. Because each $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$ is compact, $I_K$ is a locally compact topological group. Crucially, this is not the subspace topology from $\prod K_\mathfrak{p}$; the restricted-product topology is finer, and it is what makes the inverse map continuous.

Proposition 1.5

$K^*$ is a discrete, hence closed, subgroup of $I_K$. (Reminder — the product formula: for any nonzero $x\in K^*$ the product of all its normalized absolute values over every place is $1$, $\prod_\mathfrak{p}|x|_\mathfrak{p}=1$. It is the global analogue of "the degree of the divisor of a rational function is $0$.") The proof is the product formula in action: the neighborhood $\{\alpha : |\alpha_\mathfrak{p}|_\mathfrak{p}=1 \text{ for } \mathfrak{p}\nmid\infty,\ |\alpha_\mathfrak{p}-1|_\mathfrak{p}<1 \text{ for } \mathfrak{p}\mid\infty\}$ contains no principal idèle but $1$, because $\prod_\mathfrak{p}|x|_\mathfrak{p}=1$ forbids a nontrivial $x\in K^*$ from being that close to $1$ everywhere.

Since $K^*$ is closed, the quotient $C_K$ is again a locally compact Hausdorff group. Define the absolute norm of an idèle by $\mathfrak{N}(\alpha) = \prod_\mathfrak{p}|\alpha_\mathfrak{p}|_\mathfrak{p}$. The product formula says $\mathfrak{N}(x)=1$ for $x\in K^*$, so this descends to a continuous homomorphism $\mathfrak{N}: C_K \to \mathbb{R}_+^*$.

Theorem 1.6 — the key compactness

The kernel $C_K^0 = \{[\alpha]\in C_K : \mathfrak{N}([\alpha])=1\}$ is compact. This single fact secretly contains both the finiteness of the class number and the finiteness of the unit rank (Dirichlet). It is the global analogue of the compactness of $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$ in the local theory, and it is what will let the abstract class field theory machinery turn over.

Generalized Dirichlet unit theorem (Prop. 1.1)

For $S$ containing all infinite places, the map $\lambda(a) = (\log|a|_\mathfrak{p})_{\mathfrak{p}\in S}$ on the S-units $K^S$ has kernel the roots of unity $\mu(K)$ and image a complete lattice in the trace-zero hyperplane $H = \{(x_\mathfrak{p}) : \sum x_\mathfrak{p}=0\}$ of dimension $\#S-1$. Setting $S=S_\infty$ recovers the classical unit theorem; allowing more primes is the S-unit version.

Congruence subgroups and ray class groups

The idèle class group comes with canonical subgroups that play the role of the higher unit groups $U^{(n)}=1+\mathfrak{p}^n$ in the local theory. Fix a module $\mathfrak{m} = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\nmid\infty}\mathfrak{p}^{n_\mathfrak{p}}$ (an integral ideal). For each place put $U_\mathfrak{p}^{(n_\mathfrak{p})}$ equal to $1+\mathfrak{p}^{n_\mathfrak{p}}$ at finite primes dividing $\mathfrak{m}$, the positive reals at real places, and all of $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$ at complex places.

Definition 1.7

The congruence subgroup mod $\mathfrak{m}$ is $C_K^\mathfrak{m} = I_K^\mathfrak{m} K^* / K^*$, where $I_K^\mathfrak{m} = \prod_\mathfrak{p} U_\mathfrak{p}^{(n_\mathfrak{p})}$. The quotient $C_K/C_K^\mathfrak{m}$ is the ray class group mod $\mathfrak{m}$.

Proposition 1.8 — why this matters

The closed subgroups of finite index in $C_K$ are precisely those containing some congruence subgroup $C_K^\mathfrak{m}$. So the congruence subgroups are cofinal (they sit below every finite-index subgroup): they give a complete overview of all the closed finite-index subgroups — and, once we have the existence theorem (§6), of all the finite abelian extensions of $K$.

The ray class group of $\mathbb{Q}$ (Prop. 1.10)

For $K=\mathbb{Q}$ and module $\mathfrak{m}=(m)$, the ray class group is

$$C_\mathbb{Q}/C_\mathbb{Q}^\mathfrak{m} \;\cong\; (\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^*.$$

This is no accident: $(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^*$ is the Galois group $G(\mathbb{Q}(\mu_m)|\mathbb{Q})$ of the cyclotomic field. So the cyclotomic fields are the ray class fields of $\mathbb{Q}$. The entire chapter is, in one sense, the generalization of this single isomorphism to arbitrary number fields.

There is also a purely ideal-theoretic description (Prop. 1.9): if $J_K^\mathfrak{m}$ is the group of fractional ideals prime to $\mathfrak{m}$ and $P_K^\mathfrak{m}$ the principal ones generated by $a\equiv 1\bmod\mathfrak{m}$ totally positive, then $C_K/C_K^\mathfrak{m} \cong J_K^\mathfrak{m}/P_K^\mathfrak{m}$. We return to this in §7.

6.2 Idèles in Field Extensions

To do class field theory we must track idèles as we move up a finite extension $L|K$. There are two operations: an inclusion $I_K\hookrightarrow I_L$ and a norm $N_{L|K}: I_L\to I_K$.

The inclusion

An idèle $\alpha=(\alpha_\mathfrak{p})$ of $K$ becomes the idèle of $L$ whose component at a prime $\mathfrak{P}\mid\mathfrak{p}$ is $\alpha_\mathfrak{p}\in K_\mathfrak{p}\hookrightarrow L_\mathfrak{P}$. So $\alpha'_\mathfrak{P} = \alpha_\mathfrak{p}$ for all $\mathfrak{P}\mid\mathfrak{p}$. An idèle of $L$ lies in $I_K$ exactly when its components are constant on each fiber above a prime of $K$ and lie in $K_\mathfrak{p}$.

Proposition 2.1 — Galois descent for idèles

If $L|K$ is Galois with group $G$, then the fixed points of the $G$-action on idèles are exactly the idèles of the base: $I_L^G = I_K$. The action permutes the primes above each $\mathfrak{p}$, and a fixed idèle must be constant on each fiber with values in $K_\mathfrak{p}$ — i.e. it comes from $K$.

The cleanest viewpoint groups primes by their place below. Writing $L_\mathfrak{p} = \prod_{\mathfrak{P}\mid\mathfrak{p}}L_\mathfrak{P}$, this is a $K_\mathfrak{p}$-algebra of degree $[L:K]$, and in fact $L_\mathfrak{p}\cong K_\mathfrak{p}\otimes_K L$. This gives the slick identity

$$\mathbb{A}_L = \mathbb{A}_K \otimes_K L,$$

under which the inclusion $\mathbb{A}_K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}_L$ is just $a\mapsto a\otimes 1$. This "back-and-forth-free" formalism is convenient and we'll lean on it.

The idèle norm (Prop. 2.2)

The norm $N_{L|K}: I_L\to I_K$ is assembled from local norms: the $\mathfrak{p}$-component of $N_{L|K}(\alpha)$ is

$$N_{L|K}(\alpha)_\mathfrak{p} = \prod_{\mathfrak{P}\mid\mathfrak{p}} N_{L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p}}(\alpha_\mathfrak{P}).$$

The norm has all the expected properties (Prop. 2.3): it is transitive in towers, it equals $\prod_{\sigma\in G/H}\sigma\alpha$ in the Galois case, it sends $\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{[L:K]}$ on idèles already in $I_K$, and on a principal idèle $x\in L^*$ it gives the principal idèle of the usual field norm $N_{L|K}(x)$. Because it respects principal idèles, it descends to a norm on idèle class groups $N_{L|K}: C_L\to C_K$.

Two descent facts for classes

Prop. 2.4: the inclusion induces an injection $C_K\hookrightarrow C_L$ — the idèle class group only grows in extensions. Prop. 2.5 (Galois descent): for $L|K$ Galois, $C_L^G = C_K$. This second one is genuinely subtle: surjectivity of $I_L^G\to C_L^G$ uses Hilbert 90 to trivialize a crossed homomorphism. Contrast with the ideal class group, which famously does not have Galois descent.

Reminder — what Hilbert 90 says

Hilbert 90 is the vanishing statement $\hat H^{-1}(G, L^*)=1$: for a cyclic $L|K$ with generator $\sigma$, every element of $L^*$ of norm $1$ has the form $\sigma x / x$. Here it kills the obstruction because the failure of an element to come from the base is measured by such a crossed homomorphism — and Hilbert 90 says every one of those is a coboundary, i.e. trivial, so the descent goes through.

6.3 The Herbrand Quotient of the Idèle Class Group

Goal: show $C_K$ satisfies the class field axiom of Chapter IV. The first half is to compute the Herbrand quotient $h(G,C_L)$ for a cyclic $L|K$. We do it by splitting $C_L$ into idèle and unit pieces and using the exact sequence $1\to L^*\to I_L\to C_L\to 1$.

Reminder — the Herbrand quotient

For a cyclic group $G$ acting on a module $A$, the Herbrand quotient is the ratio of the orders of the two Tate cohomology groups,

$$h(G,A) = \frac{\#\hat H^0(G,A)}{\#\hat H^{-1}(G,A)}.$$

Two properties carry the whole computation. First, it is multiplicative across exact sequences: given $1\to A\to B\to C\to 1$ one has $h(B)=h(A)\,h(C)$ — this is exactly why, when we divide the idèle piece by the unit piece below, the local factors cancel. Second, it is unchanged on passing to a finite-index sublattice, which lets us replace an awkward lattice by a convenient induced one without changing the answer.

Why everything localizes — induced modules

The decisive structural fact (Prop. 3.1) is that for each place $\mathfrak{p}$ of $K$, the local factor $L_\mathfrak{p}^* = \prod_{\mathfrak{P}\mid\mathfrak{p}}L_\mathfrak{P}^*$ is an induced $G$-module: $L_\mathfrak{p}^* = \mathrm{Ind}_{G_\mathfrak{P}}^G(L_\mathfrak{P}^*)$. Here the decomposition group $G_\mathfrak{P}$ is the stabilizer of a chosen prime $\mathfrak{P}$ above $\mathfrak{p}$ — the automorphisms fixing $\mathfrak{P}$ as a set — and it is canonically the local Galois group $G_\mathfrak{P}\cong G(L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p})$. Shapiro's lemma says the cohomology of an induced module equals the cohomology of the subgroup it was induced from: $\hat H^i(G,\mathrm{Ind}_{G_\mathfrak{P}}^G L_\mathfrak{P}^*)\cong\hat H^i(G_\mathfrak{P},L_\mathfrak{P}^*)$. So the global cohomology collapses into a sum of local cohomologies, one per place — this is where the local-to-global magic happens.

Consequently (Prop. 3.2), for a cyclic extension and $S$ containing all ramified primes,

$$\hat H^i(G, I_L) = \bigoplus_\mathfrak{p} \hat H^i(G_\mathfrak{P}, L_\mathfrak{P}^*).$$

Two payoffs drop out immediately. First, $\hat H^{-1}(G,I_L)=1$ by Hilbert 90 locally. Second, $\hat H^0$ gives the norm-locally-everywhere principle for idèles:

Local norms patch to global idèle norms

An idèle $\alpha\in I_K$ is a norm from $I_L$ if and only if every component $\alpha_\mathfrak{p}$ is locally a norm from $L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p}$. That is, $I_K/N_{L|K}I_L = \bigoplus_\mathfrak{p} K_\mathfrak{p}^*/N L_\mathfrak{P}^*$.

By local class field theory each local factor has order $n_\mathfrak{p}=[L_\mathfrak{P}:K_\mathfrak{p}]$, so the Herbrand quotient of the S-idèles is (Prop. 3.3) $h(G,I_K^S) = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S} n_\mathfrak{p}$.

The unit part (Prop. 3.5)

The Herbrand quotient of the group of S-units is

$$h(G, L^S) = \frac{1}{n}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S} n_\mathfrak{p}.$$

The proof realizes the lattice of S-units (via the logarithm map of §1) inside an explicit permutation representation, then uses a lattice lemma (3.4) to replace it by an induced sublattice of finite index, where the Herbrand quotient is computable place-by-place. The lone factor $1/n$ comes from splitting off a trivial $\mathbb{Z}$ (the "trace zero" constraint).

Theorem 3.6 — the Herbrand quotient of $C_L$

Dividing the idèle quotient by the unit quotient (using $1\to L^S\to I_L^S\to C_L\to 1$) and the multiplicativity of the Herbrand quotient, the local factors cancel perfectly. Writing it out, with $h(I_L^S)=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S}n_\mathfrak{p}$ (Prop. 3.3) and $h(L^S)=\tfrac1n\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S}n_\mathfrak{p}$ (Prop. 3.5):

$$h(C_L) = \frac{h(I_L^S)}{h(L^S)} = \frac{\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S}n_\mathfrak{p}}{\ \dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S}n_\mathfrak{p}\ } = n.$$

The entire product $\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S}n_\mathfrak{p}$ — all the local data — divides out top against bottom, and only the lone $1/n$ from the unit part survives, flipped up to give $n$. So

$$h(G,C_L) = \frac{\#\hat H^0(G,C_L)}{\#\hat H^{-1}(G,C_L)} = n = [L:K].$$

In particular $(C_K : N_{L|K}C_L) \ge n$. This is the "$\ge$" half of the class field axiom.

A free corollary: nonsplit primes exist (Cor. 3.7, 3.8)

If $L|K$ is cyclic of prime-power degree, there are infinitely many primes of $K$ that do not split in $L$ (3.7). Sharper (3.8): if almost all primes of $K$ split completely in $L$, then $L=K$. A number field is determined by its set of completely-split primes — a quiet but powerful rigidity statement we'll use repeatedly.

6.4 The Class Field Axiom

We have $(C_K : NC_L)\ge n$ from the Herbrand quotient. The class field axiom demands equality, so we need the reverse: $\#\hat H^0(G,C_L)\mid n$. Attacking $\hat H^{-1}$ directly is hopeless, so the strategy is to compute $\hat H^0$ explicitly in the one case we can — a Kummer extension — and then bootstrap to the general cyclic case by induction.

Why Kummer extensions?

If $K$ contains the $n$-th roots of unity, every abelian extension of exponent $n$ is $K(\sqrt[n]{\Delta})$ for some subgroup $\Delta\subseteq K^*$ (Kummer theory). The simplest case makes this concrete: for $n=2$, the extensions of exponent $2$ are exactly the quadratic fields $K(\sqrt d)$, one for each class $d\in K^*/(K^*)^2$ — so the "subgroup $\Delta$" is just a choice of square classes. The hypothesis that $\mu_n\subseteq K$ is what makes this clean: it guarantees that adjoining one $n$-th root of $d$ brings along all of them (since they differ by roots of unity already in $K$), so $K(\sqrt[n]{d})$ is Galois with cyclic group. This makes the norm group computable: it can be pinned down by congruence conditions at finitely many places.

Setup: $K$ contains the $n$-th roots of unity ($n$ a prime power), $L|K$ has group $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^r$. Choosing $S$ large (Prop. 4.1), one finds a set $T$ of $s-r$ auxiliary primes outside $S$ with $L = K(\sqrt[n]{\Delta})$, where $\Delta$ is the kernel of the map from S-units into $\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in T}K_\mathfrak{p}^*/K_\mathfrak{p}^{*n}$.

Theorem 4.2 — the norm group, explicitly

With $C_K(S,T) = I_K(S,T)K^*/K^*$ built from the idèle group $I_K(S,T) = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in S}K_\mathfrak{p}^{*n}\times\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\in T}K_\mathfrak{p}^*\times\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\notin S\cup T}\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$, one has

$$N_{L|K}C_L \subseteq C_K(S,T), \qquad (C_K : C_K(S,T)) = [L:K].$$

For $L|K$ cyclic these coincide: $N_{L|K}C_L = C_K(S,T)$. The index computation is a careful count of local indices; the inclusion uses that each local component is visibly a norm (an $n$-th power at $S$, a unit in an unramified extension elsewhere).

Theorem 4.4 — the Global Class Field Axiom

For every cyclic extension $L|K$ of number fields,

$$\#\hat H^i(G, C_L) = \begin{cases} [L:K] & i=0,\\ 1 & i=-1.\end{cases}$$

The proof reduces an arbitrary cyclic $L|K$ to prime-degree steps; in the hard prime-degree case it passes to $K' = K(\mu_p)$ to make a Kummer extension, where 4.2 applies, then descends via a norm/transfer argument.

Corollary 4.5 — the Hasse Norm Theorem

For $L|K$ cyclic, an element $x\in K^*$ is a global norm from $L$ if and only if it is a norm locally everywhere (a norm in each completion $L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p}$). This is the cleanest possible local-to-global statement and follows immediately from $\hat H^{-1}(G,C_L)=1$ feeding into the long exact sequence.

Cyclicity is essential

For a non-cyclic abelian extension the Hasse norm theorem can fail: a global element that is everywhere locally a norm need only be the norm of an idèle, not of a principal idèle. The classic counterexample lives in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{13},\sqrt{17})$. The cohomological reason: $\hat H^{-1}$ need not vanish for non-cyclic groups.

6.5 The Global Reciprocity Law

Now that $C_K$ satisfies the class field axiom, Chapter IV's abstract machine applies — but it needs a starting datum: a pair $(d: G_\mathbb{Q}\to\hat{\mathbb{Z}},\ v: C_\mathbb{Q}\to\hat{\mathbb{Z}})$ obeying the rules of abstract class field theory. We build it over $\mathbb{Q}$ using a canonical $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$-extension.

The base $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$-extension (Prop. 5.1)

Reminder: $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is the ring of profinite integers, $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}=\varprojlim\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\prod_p\mathbb{Z}_p$. Abstract class field theory (Chapter IV) needs a base extension whose Galois group is $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$ precisely because $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is what supports the degree map $d$ and the Frobenius — its quotients $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}/n\cong\mathbb{Z}/n$ give one cyclic layer of each degree, the global stand-in for "the unramified extensions" of the local theory. Concretely the cyclotomic layers do the job: $G(\mathbb{Q}(\mu_m)|\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^*$. Adjoin all roots of unity to get $\Omega|\mathbb{Q}$ with $G(\Omega|\mathbb{Q})=\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^*$. Quotient by the torsion subgroup: the fixed field $\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}|\mathbb{Q}$ is a $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$-extension, $G(\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}|\mathbb{Q})\cong\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$. Fix one such isomorphism — there is no canonical choice, but the reciprocity law won't depend on it. For a finite $K|\mathbb{Q}$, $\tilde K = K\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}$ is the cyclotomic $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$-extension of $K$.

The global norm residue symbol — local pieces glued

For a finite abelian $L|K$, define $[\alpha, L|K]: I_K\to G(L|K)$ as the product of local norm residue symbols:

$$[\alpha, L|K] = \prod_\mathfrak{p}(\alpha_\mathfrak{p}, L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p}).$$

Almost all factors are $1$ because almost all $L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p}$ are unramified and almost all $\alpha_\mathfrak{p}$ are units, so the product converges.

Proposition 5.3 — the crucial vanishing

For every root of unity $\zeta$ and every principal idèle $a\in K^*$, one has $[a, K(\zeta)|K]=1$. The proof reduces to $K=\mathbb{Q}$ and a prime-power root of unity, where it becomes an explicit and beautiful computation: the Frobenius contributions at the unramified primes, the wild contribution at $\ell$, and the sign at infinity all multiply to $1$ — precisely the product formula $a = \mathrm{sgn}(a)\prod p^{v_p(a)}$ in disguise. We flag this as a computation to take on faith here: the honest verification is a careful place-by-place tally (one term per prime dividing $a$, one wild term, one sign), and the punchline is simply that it reassembles the product formula. Read it as "the product formula reappearing" and move on — the structure, not the bookkeeping, is the point.

Because $\tilde K|K$ sits inside the field of all roots of unity, Prop. 5.3 gives $[a,\tilde K|K]=1$ for principal $a$. So the symbol descends to $C_K$, and composing with $d_K$ gives the henselian valuation $v_K: C_K\to\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$ (Prop. 5.4). Surjectivity uses the rigidity Cor. 3.8 (split-completely-implies-trivial); the splitting $C_K = C_K^0\times\mathbb{R}_+^*$ uses the compactness of $C_K^0$ from Theorem 1.6.

Theorem 5.5 — Artin Reciprocity

For every finite Galois extension $L|K$ of number fields there is a canonical isomorphism

$$r_{L|K}: G(L|K)^{\mathrm{ab}} \;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\; C_K / N_{L|K}C_L.$$

Its inverse is the surjective global norm residue symbol $(\,\cdot\,, L|K): C_K \to G(L|K)^{\mathrm{ab}}$ with kernel exactly $N_{L|K}C_L$. This is the theorem of the chapter.

Local-global compatibility (Prop. 5.6, Cor. 5.7)

The local and global symbols fit into a commutative square: for any place $\mathfrak{p}$, the local symbol $(\,\cdot\,, L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p})$ on $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$ agrees with the global symbol applied to the idèle supported at $\mathfrak{p}$. Hence for any idèle $(\alpha, L|K) = \prod_\mathfrak{p}(\alpha_\mathfrak{p}, L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p})$, and for principal $a$ the product formula $\prod_\mathfrak{p}(a, L_\mathfrak{P}|K_\mathfrak{p})=1$ holds. This product formula is the engine behind every reciprocity law in §8.

Corollary 5.8 — local norms are global norms ∩ $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$

For finite abelian $L|K$, $N C_L \cap K_\mathfrak{p}^* = N_\mathfrak{p}L_\mathfrak{P}^*$. This pins the global norm group down place-by-place and will be the key to computing conductors in §6.

6.6 Global Class Fields

Reciprocity gives a map; the existence theorem says that map is a perfect dictionary between subgroups and fields.

Theorem 6.1 — the Existence Theorem

The assignment $L\mapsto \mathcal{N}_L = N_{L|K}C_L$ is an inclusion-reversing bijection between finite abelian extensions $L|K$ and closed subgroups of finite index in $C_K$, with

$$G(L|K) \cong C_K / \mathcal{N}_L, \quad L_1\subseteq L_2 \Leftrightarrow \mathcal{N}_{L_1}\supseteq \mathcal{N}_{L_2}, \quad \mathcal{N}_{L_1 L_2}=\mathcal{N}_{L_1}\cap\mathcal{N}_{L_2}.$$

The field $L$ attached to a subgroup $\mathcal{N}$ is the class field of $\mathcal{N}$.

The content of the proof is topological: closed of finite index for the natural topology coincides with open in the norm topology. The compactness $C_K^0$ (Theorem 1.6) again does the heavy lifting in showing norm groups are closed.

Definition 6.2 — ray class fields

The class field of the congruence subgroup $C_K^\mathfrak{m}$ is the ray class field mod $\mathfrak{m}$ $K^\mathfrak{m}$, with

$$G(K^\mathfrak{m}|K) \cong C_K/C_K^\mathfrak{m} = \text{ray class group mod }\mathfrak{m}.$$

Since every closed finite-index subgroup contains some $C_K^\mathfrak{m}$ (Prop. 1.8), every finite abelian extension lies in some ray class field (Cor. 6.3). The ray class fields are the global analogue of the cyclotomic fields, and they exhaust the abelian extensions.

Conductor (Def. 6.4, Prop. 6.5, Cor. 6.6)

The conductor $\mathfrak{f}$ of $L|K$ is the gcd of all modules $\mathfrak{m}$ with $L\subseteq K^\mathfrak{m}$ — so $K^\mathfrak{f}$ is the smallest ray class field containing $L$. It factors into local conductors $\mathfrak{f} = \prod_\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{f}_\mathfrak{p}$ (using Cor. 5.8), and the upshot is the clean criterion:

$$\mathfrak{p} \text{ ramifies in } L \iff \mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{f}.$$
Proposition 6.7 — cyclotomic fields are the ray class fields of $\mathbb{Q}$

For $K=\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathfrak{m}=(m)$, the ray class field mod $\mathfrak{m}$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\mu_m)$. As a bonus this reproves the Kronecker–Weber theorem: every finite abelian $L|\mathbb{Q}$ sits in some cyclotomic field, because its norm group contains some $C_\mathbb{Q}^\mathfrak{m}$.

The Hilbert class fields (Prop. 6.8, 6.9)

The ray class field mod $1$ is the big Hilbert class field — the maximal unramified abelian extension of $K$ (where "unramified" allows the infinite places to ramify). Cutting down so that real places stay real gives the small Hilbert class field $H|K$, the maximal abelian extension unramified everywhere including infinity, with the strikingly clean

$$G(H|K) \cong \mathrm{Cl}_K, \qquad [H:K]=h_K.$$

So the Galois group of the Hilbert class field is the ideal class group, and its degree is the class number.

Kronecker's Jugendtraum (Thm. 6.10, 6.11)

The existence theorem tells us ray class fields exist but not how to generate them. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ we generate them with values of $e^{2\pi i z}$ at rational points (roots of unity). Over an imaginary quadratic field $K$, Kronecker's "dream of his youth" delivers an analogous explicit construction: the $j$-invariant $j(\mathfrak{a})$ of the elliptic curve $\mathbb{C}/\mathfrak{a}$ (with complex multiplication by $\mathfrak{o}_K$) generates the Hilbert class field, and Weber functions generate the higher ray class fields. This is the analytic theory of complex multiplication — beautiful, genuinely beyond our scope here, and the subject of a whole separate book. Treat it as a signpost, not a result we will prove.

6.7 The Ideal-Theoretic Version of Class Field Theory

Historically, class field theory was created in the language of ideals (Weber, Takagi, Artin) long before Chevalley's idèles. This section translates the idèle-theoretic results back into that classical language — not just for history, but because ideal groups are more concrete and more usable in many applications.

The Artin symbol

For an unramified prime $\mathfrak{p}$ in an abelian $L|K$, the decomposition group is generated by the Frobenius $\varphi_\mathfrak{p}$, the unique automorphism with $\varphi_\mathfrak{p}(a)\equiv a^q\bmod\mathfrak{P}$ (where $q=\#(\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p})$). (Reminder: when $\mathfrak{p}$ is unramified the residue extension is cyclic and generated by the $q$-power map $x\mapsto x^q$ on the residue field; this canonical generator lifts uniquely to the decomposition group, and that lift is $\varphi_\mathfrak{p}$ — which is exactly why the Frobenius exists and is well-defined.) Write $\left(\frac{L|K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=\varphi_\mathfrak{p}$. Extending multiplicatively over ideals prime to a module of definition $\mathfrak{m}$ gives the Artin map

$$\left(\frac{L|K}{\cdot}\right): J_K^\mathfrak{m}\to G(L|K), \qquad \mathfrak{a}=\prod\mathfrak{p}^{v_\mathfrak{p}}\mapsto \prod\left(\frac{L|K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)^{v_\mathfrak{p}}.$$
Theorem 7.1 / Cor. 7.2 — ideal-theoretic Artin reciprocity

If $\mathfrak{m}$ is a module of definition for $L|K$, the Artin map induces an isomorphism

$$J_K^\mathfrak{m}/H_\mathfrak{m} \;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\; G(L|K), \qquad H_\mathfrak{m} = (N_{L|K}J_L^\mathfrak{m})\,P_K^\mathfrak{m}.$$

The subgroup $H_\mathfrak{m}$ — the "ideal group defined mod $\mathfrak{m}$" — is generated by ray-principal ideals and norms of ideals from $L$. The correspondence $L\mapsto H_\mathfrak{m}$ is the classical Takagi bijection between subfields of $K^\mathfrak{m}$ and subgroups of $J_K^\mathfrak{m}$ containing $P_K^\mathfrak{m}$.

Theorem 7.3 — the Decomposition Law

For an unramified prime $\mathfrak{p}$, let $f$ be the order of $\mathfrak{p}\bmod H_\mathfrak{m}$ in the class group $J_K^\mathfrak{m}/H_\mathfrak{m}$ — the least $f$ with $\mathfrak{p}^f\in H_\mathfrak{m}$. Then $\mathfrak{p} = \mathfrak{P}_1\cdots\mathfrak{P}_r$ into $r=n/f$ distinct primes of residue degree $f$ over $\mathfrak{p}$. The splitting of $\mathfrak{p}$ is read off purely from its ideal class.

Two beautiful special cases

For the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\mu_m)|\mathbb{Q}$, the decomposition law says $p$ splits completely iff $p\equiv 1\bmod m$. For the Hilbert class field (Cor. 7.4): a prime ideal splits completely in the Hilbert class field iff it is principal. The class group governs splitting exactly.

Theorem 7.5 — the Principal Ideal Theorem

In the Hilbert class field $K_1$ of $K$, every ideal of $K$ becomes principal. Note this does not say $K_1$ has class number $1$ — only that ideals of the base capitulate. The proof is a stunning reduction: via the transfer map ($\mathrm{Ver}$) and the identification $G(K_1|K)=\mathrm{Cl}_K$, the statement becomes the purely group-theoretic Principal Ideal Theorem 7.6: for a finitely generated group $G$ with $(G:G')<\infty$, the transfer $\mathrm{Ver}: G/G'\to G'/G''$ is trivial. Witt's elegant proof uses the augmentation ideal of the group ring.

The class field tower problem

Iterating — $K\subset K_1\subset K_2\subset\cdots$, each the Hilbert class field of the last — gives the class field tower. Does it terminate? A finite tower would force a field of class number $1$ in which all ideals of $K$ capitulate. Golod–Shafarevich (1964) showed it can be infinite. So this hope fails in general.

Artin's non-abelian example (exercises)

The polynomial $f(X)=X^5-X+1$ has discriminant $19\cdot 151$ and splitting field of Galois group $S_5$ (degree $120$). One shows $k=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ has class number $1$, and the splitting field is an unramified (non-abelian!) extension of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{19\cdot 151})$ — showing unramified non-abelian extensions exist and live beyond class field theory's reach.

6.8 The Reciprocity Law of the Power Residues

The payoff. Class field theory was born from Gauss's quadratic reciprocity; here we watch the full $n$-th power reciprocity law — and quadratic reciprocity as its baby case — fall directly out of the global product formula of §5. Let $K$ contain the $n$-th roots of unity.

Theorem 8.1 — the Hilbert symbol product formula

Recall the local $n$-th Hilbert symbol $\left(\frac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)$, defined so that $(a, K_\mathfrak{p}(\sqrt[n]{b})|K_\mathfrak{p})\sqrt[n]{b} = \left(\frac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\sqrt[n]{b}$. For all $a,b\in K^*$,

$$\prod_\mathfrak{p}\left(\frac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right) = 1.$$

This is immediate from Cor. 5.7: the symbols are the local pieces of the global norm residue symbol of a principal idèle, whose product is forced to be $1$.

The $n$-th power residue symbol $\left(\frac{a}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)$ for $\mathfrak{p}\nmid n$ is defined via the Hilbert symbol and satisfies $\left(\frac{a}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\equiv a^{(q-1)/n}\bmod\mathfrak{p}$ — the familiar Euler-style criterion. Extending multiplicatively (Def. 8.2) to $\left(\frac{a}{\mathfrak{b}}\right)=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid\mathfrak{b}}\left(\frac{a}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)^{v_\mathfrak{p}}$ gives a symbol multiplicative in both slots.

Theorem 8.3 — the general power reciprocity law

For $a,b\in K^*$ coprime to each other and to $n$,

$$\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{-1} = \prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid n\infty}\left(\frac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right).$$

The proof rearranges the product formula 8.1: the symbols at primes dividing $a$ assemble into $\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$, those dividing $b$ into $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)$, and what survives is exactly the "bad" places dividing $n\infty$. Reciprocity is bookkeeping on the product formula.

Theorem 8.4 — Gauss reciprocity recovered

Take $K=\mathbb{Q}$, $n=2$. The only "bad" places are $2$ and $\infty$, and plugging in the explicit Hilbert symbols there gives, for odd coprime $a,b$,

$$\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)\left(\frac{b}{a}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{a-1}{2}\frac{b-1}{2}}\cdot(-1)^{\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(a)-1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(b)-1}{2}},$$

together with the two supplementary laws $\left(\frac{-1}{b}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{b-1}{2}}$ and $\left(\frac{2}{b}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{b^2-1}{8}}$. For positive odd $a,b$ this is exactly the classical quadratic reciprocity.

The two bad places, explicitly

Here is the specialization in full. For $n=2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, the product formula $\prod_\mathfrak{p}\left(\frac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=1$ has only two nontrivial factors for odd coprime $a,b$ — at $2$ and at $\infty$ — once the symbols at the odd primes have been collected into the residue symbols $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right),\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$. The two surviving local symbols are

$$\left(\frac{a,b}{2}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{a-1}{2}\frac{b-1}{2}}, \qquad \left(\frac{a,b}{\infty}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(a)-1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(b)-1}{2}}.$$

Now read the product formula as "everything multiplies to $1$" and move the two bad factors to the other side: $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)=\left(\frac{a,b}{2}\right)\left(\frac{a,b}{\infty}\right)$. Substituting the two formulas above gives the displayed reciprocity law verbatim. For positive $a,b$ the infinite factor is $1$ (both signs are positive), and we recover the bare $(-1)^{\frac{a-1}{2}\frac{b-1}{2}}$ of Gauss.

It actually computes

The Jacobi symbol $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)$ can be evaluated by iterating reciprocity and reduction — no factorization needed. For instance one flips and reduces $\left(\frac{40077}{65537}\right)$ down through $\left(\frac{65537}{40077}\right),\left(\frac{25460}{40077}\right),\dots$ to a final value of $1$. This is the algorithmic heart of quadratic residue computations.

The arc of the whole subject

Class field theory began as the search for a power-residue analogue of Gauss's law. The all-embracing answer turned out to be Artin's reciprocity (Thm. 5.5), and what we once called "reciprocity" is now just one corollary of it. The one piece left unfinished by the abstract theory — explicit formulas for the wild Hilbert symbols at $\mathfrak{p}\mid n$ — was completed by Brückner and Vostokov in the 1960s.

Summary

This chapter built the global theory in one disciplined sweep, mirroring the abstract framework of Chapter IV with $C_K$ in the role local theory gave to $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$.

  • §1 The object. The idèle class group $C_K = I_K/K^*$, built from the restricted product of all local groups. Key facts: it's locally compact, $K^*$ is discrete, and the norm-one part $C_K^0$ is compact (encoding class number + unit rank finiteness). Congruence subgroups are cofinal among finite-index subgroups.
  • §2 Functoriality. Inclusion $C_K\hookrightarrow C_L$ and norm $N: C_L\to C_K$; Galois descent $C_L^G=C_K$ via Hilbert 90.
  • §3–§4 The axiom. Herbrand quotient $h(C_L)=n$ (giving $\ge n$) plus Kummer computation (giving $\le n$) prove the class field axiom. Corollaries: Hasse norm theorem; primes don't all split.
  • §5 Reciprocity. The Artin reciprocity law $G(L|K)^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong C_K/N C_L$, with the global norm residue symbol a product of local ones and a product formula for principal idèles.
  • §6 Classification. The existence theorem: finite abelian extensions $\leftrightarrow$ closed finite-index subgroups. Ray class fields, conductors, ramification criterion; cyclotomic fields and Hilbert class fields as special cases; Kronecker's Jugendtraum.
  • §7 Ideal language. Artin symbol, the decomposition law (splitting from ideal class), the principal ideal theorem (via transfer), the class field tower.
  • §8 Reciprocity payoff. The Hilbert symbol product formula yields $n$-th power reciprocity, with Gauss's quadratic reciprocity as the case $K=\mathbb{Q},n=2$.
One sentence to remember

Global class field theory says: the abelian extensions of $K$, and how every prime splits in them, are completely encoded in the subgroup structure of the single locally compact group $C_K = I_K/K^*$ — and all the classical reciprocity laws are just the product formula for its norm residue symbol.

Next we leave the algebraic edifice and turn to the analytic side — zeta and L-functions — where the same arithmetic reappears encoded in special values and functional equations.

Knowledge check

These are designed to make you think — the wrong options are deliberately tempting. Decide on your answer before revealing the explanation.

Question 1

A student writes: "the idèle group is the restricted product ${\prod_\mathfrak{p}}' K_\mathfrak{p}$ with respect to the rings of integers $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}$." Exactly one thing in that sentence is wrong. What is it?

  1. Nothing — that is precisely the definition of $I_K$.
  2. The restriction must be with respect to the unit groups $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$, and the local factors must be $K_\mathfrak{p}^*$, not $K_\mathfrak{p}$; as written it describes the adèle ring.
  3. The product should be unrestricted, since idèles are allowed to be non-integral at every place at once.
  4. The restriction is fine, but the local factors should be the rings of integers $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}$ themselves.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: B. The idèle group is multiplicative: $I_K = {\prod_\mathfrak{p}}' K_\mathfrak{p}^*$ restricted with respect to the unit groups $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$. The sentence as written — additive factors $K_\mathfrak{p}$ restricted by $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}$ — is the adèle ring $\mathbb{A}_K$, not the idèle group. A is the classic adèle/idèle confusion. C is false: an idèle must be a unit (lie in $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$) at almost all places, so the full product is wrong. D drops to non-invertible factors, which cannot form a group under multiplication.

Question 2

Which object does Artin reciprocity (Thm. 5.5) place in canonical correspondence with the abelianized Galois group $G(L|K)^{\mathrm{ab}}$, for a finite Galois $L|K$?

  1. The idèle group modulo norms, $I_K/N_{L|K}I_L$.
  2. The idèle class group modulo the norm group, $C_K/N_{L|K}C_L$.
  3. The full idèle class group $C_K$ itself.
  4. The multiplicative group $K^*/N_{L|K}L^*$ of principal idèles modulo norms.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: B. Reciprocity gives $G(L|K)^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong C_K/N_{L|K}C_L$ — the idèle class group quotiented by the norm group. A tempts because $I_K/N I_L$ does decompose into local pieces (the norm-everywhere principle of §3), but that idèle quotient is not the Galois group; one must first pass to classes by quotienting out $K^*$. C forgets the quotient by norms entirely — $C_K$ alone is infinite and maps onto every abelianized Galois group at once, not a single one. D uses principal idèles only, which is the cyclic Hasse-norm statement, not the reciprocity isomorphism.

Question 3

In the small Hilbert class field $H|K$, which statement correctly describes how a prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of $K$ behaves?

  1. $\mathfrak{p}$ splits completely in $H$ if and only if $\mathfrak{p}$ is principal; and $H|K$ is unramified at every place including infinity.
  2. $\mathfrak{p}$ splits completely if and only if $\mathfrak{p}$ is non-principal; $H|K$ is unramified.
  3. $\mathfrak{p}$ splits completely if and only if $\mathfrak{p}$ is principal; but $H|K$ is ramified exactly at the primes dividing the class number.
  4. Every $\mathfrak{p}$ splits completely, because $H|K$ is the maximal unramified abelian extension.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: A. By Cor. 7.4 a prime splits completely in the Hilbert class field iff its ideal class is trivial, i.e. iff it is principal — the Frobenius/Artin class being trivial is exactly the splitting condition. And $H$ is by definition the maximal abelian extension unramified everywhere, including the infinite places, with $G(H|K)\cong\mathrm{Cl}_K$. B reverses the decomposition law (the common slip: trivial class means split, not non-split). C contradicts the defining unramifiedness of the Hilbert class field — it is unramified at all places, never ramified at primes dividing $h_K$. D confuses "maximal unramified" with "everything splits"; only principal primes split completely.

Question 4

Let $p$ be a prime not dividing $m$. In which of these abelian extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$ does $p$ split completely exactly when $p\equiv 1\bmod m$?

  1. The Hilbert class field of $\mathbb{Q}$.
  2. The quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{m})$.
  3. The cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\mu_m)$, the ray class field mod $(m)$.
  4. The cyclotomic $\hat{\mathbb{Z}}$-extension $\tilde{\mathbb{Q}}$.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: C. For $\mathbb{Q}(\mu_m)|\mathbb{Q}$ the decomposition law reads off splitting from the class of $p$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^*$: $p$ splits completely iff its Frobenius is trivial, i.e. iff $p\equiv 1\bmod m$. A is empty content: $\mathbb{Q}$ has class number $1$, so its Hilbert class field is $\mathbb{Q}$ itself and every prime "splits." B gives a congruence modulo the discriminant via a quadratic-residue condition, not $p\equiv1\bmod m$. D is an infinite extension in which no finite prime splits completely.

Question 5

Which statement about the idèle class group $C_K$ is false?

  1. The norm-one subgroup $C_K^0$ is compact, a fact encoding both finiteness of the class number and the unit rank.
  2. For a Galois extension $L|K$ the Galois fixed points satisfy $C_L^G = C_K$.
  3. The closed finite-index subgroups of $C_K$ are exactly those containing some congruence subgroup $C_K^\mathfrak{m}$.
  4. Killing the unit and archimedean information turns $C_K$ into the ideal class group, and like the ideal class group $C_K$ is finite.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: D. The first clause is true (Prop. 1.3 — the quotient by $I_K^\infty K^*$ is $\mathrm{Cl}_K$), but the conclusion is false: $C_K$ is not finite — it has the continuous absolute-norm map onto $\mathbb{R}_+^*$ and splits as $C_K^0\times\mathbb{R}_+^*$. Only the norm-one part is compact and only the ideal-class quotient is finite. A is exactly Theorem 1.6. B is the genuine Galois descent (Prop. 2.5, via Hilbert 90) that the ideal class group famously lacks. C is Prop. 1.8, the cofinality of congruence subgroups. The tempting false statement smuggles a true premise in front of a wrong conclusion.

Question 6

In deriving quadratic reciprocity (Thm. 8.4) from the Hilbert symbol product formula $\prod_\mathfrak{p}\left(\frac{a,b}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)=1$ over $\mathbb{Q},\,n=2$ for odd coprime $a,b$, what exactly produces the answer?

  1. The odd-prime symbols reassemble into $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right),\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$, and only the explicit local symbols at $2$ and $\infty$ survive on the other side.
  2. One needs a separate deep input beyond the local Hilbert symbols — the analytic class number formula — to evaluate the surviving factors.
  3. Every local symbol including those at odd primes must be computed explicitly; there is no reassembly into residue symbols.
  4. The surviving bad places are $3$ and $\infty$, since $3$ is the smallest odd prime.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: A. The whole derivation is bookkeeping on the product formula: symbols at odd primes dividing $a$ collect into $\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)$ and those dividing $b$ into $\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)$, leaving only the two "bad" places $\mathfrak{p}\mid n\infty$ — here $2$ and $\infty$ — whose explicit symbols give $(-1)^{\frac{a-1}{2}\frac{b-1}{2}}$ and the sign factor. B is the misconception the chapter warns against: no extra ingredient is needed, just the explicit local symbols. C misses that residue symbols are precisely the reassembled product of odd-prime Hilbert symbols. D miscounts the bad places — for $n=2$ they are the primes dividing $n$ (namely $2$) together with $\infty$, not $3$.

Question 7

Why is the restricted product (rather than the full direct product or the direct sum) the right construction for $I_K$?

  1. The full product is too small and the direct sum too big; the restricted product is the unique one that is a group.
  2. The full product fails to be locally compact in the right topology, while the direct sum forgets the integral structure; the restricted product allows finitely many "large" components yet forces the rest into the compact $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$, making $I_K$ locally compact.
  3. The restricted product is just the full product with the subspace topology, chosen so that $K^*$ embeds continuously.
  4. Only the restricted product makes the diagonal embedding of $K^*$ dense, which is what discreteness of $K^*$ requires.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: B. "Restricted" means all but finitely many components lie in $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$. Because each $\mathfrak{o}_\mathfrak{p}^*$ is compact, this Goldilocks object is locally compact — the full product carries the wrong (non-locally-compact) topology and the direct sum throws away the integral structure. A inverts the sizing (the full product is too big, the sum too small) and all three are groups, so that is not the distinction. C is wrong on the key point: the restricted-product topology is strictly finer than the subspace topology from $\prod K_\mathfrak{p}$, which is exactly what makes inversion continuous. D inverts the truth: $K^*$ embeds discretely (and closed), not densely — by the product formula.
