Chapter 04

Abstract Class Field Theory

Build the reciprocity machine once, from nothing but a profinite group with a Frobenius and a henselian valuation — then bolt it onto local and global number theory and watch class field theory fall out twice.

Neukirch Chapter IV ~55 min reading time Prereqs: finite Galois theory, p-adic fields (ch. II), basic group cohomology vocabulary

Why an abstract theory at all?

Class field theory is the crown of algebraic number theory. Its central statement is a single, almost mystical isomorphism: for an abelian extension $L\mid K$,

$$G(L\mid K) \;\xrightarrow{\sim}\; A_K / N_{L\mid K}A_L.$$

Read aloud, this says: the symmetry group of the extension $L\mid K$ equals the group $A_K$ of the base field, divided out by the image of the norm map coming down from $A_L$. A few words first. An abelian extension is a Galois extension whose Galois group is commutative. The norm $N_{L\mid K}$ takes an element upstairs and multiplies together all of its Galois conjugates — a single number "averaged" down to the base; its image $N_{L\mid K}A_L$ is the norm group, and the quotient by it is the norm residue group. (We make the norm precise in §4.3.)

Here $A_K$ is a group attached to the base field $K$. In the local case it is the multiplicative group $K^\times$ (the nonzero elements of $K$ under multiplication); in the global case it is (a modification of) the idele class group — for now, treat this as a black box, a single global gadget that packages the arithmetic at every place of a number field at once. It is constructed in Chapter VI; you lose nothing by carrying it as a name. The remarkable thing Neukirch noticed — following Hazewinkel and Dwork — is that this isomorphism does not depend on fields at all. It can be proved for any profinite group equipped with two extra pieces of data. Prove it once in the abstract; instantiate it twice.

The whole chapter in one paragraph

Start with a profinite group $G$ (think: an absolute Galois group). Add a surjection $d\colon G \twoheadrightarrow \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ onto $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ (the profinite completion of $\mathbb{Z}$, built in §4.2 — picture it as "all congruence information about an integer at once") — this is the abstract "Frobenius / unramified" datum that mimics the residue-field arithmetic. Add a $G$-module $A$ (an abelian group on which $G$ acts — made precise below) with a "henselian valuation" $v\colon A_k \to \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ (a size-measuring map that survives up every extension — also made precise below). Impose one cohomological axiom (the class field axiom). Out comes a canonical reciprocity isomorphism for every finite Galois extension. That is the entire architecture, and it is genuinely elementary — no cohomological dimension theory required.

Before we can even state the abstract setup, we need three pieces of background machinery: how Galois theory works for infinite extensions (§4.1), the language of projective and inductive limits (§4.2), and the dictionary that lets us treat a bare profinite group as if it were a Galois group (§4.3). Then comes the payoff: valuations (§4.4), the reciprocity map (§4.5), the reciprocity law (§4.6), and the computational tool — the Herbrand quotient — that makes the axiom checkable in practice (§4.7).

4.1 Infinite Galois Theory

Every field $k$ comes with one distinguished Galois extension: its separable closure $\bar{k}\mid k$. The group $G_k = G(\bar{k}\mid k)$ is the absolute Galois group. It bundles together all finite Galois extensions of $k$ at once, which is exactly why we want to give it center stage. But there is a catch.

The main theorem of Galois theory fails for infinite extensions

Naively, subgroups of $G_k$ should correspond to intermediate fields. For infinite extensions this is false: there are far more subgroups than fields. The fix is topological — only the closed subgroups correspond to fields.

The motivating example — the absolute Galois group of $\mathbb{F}_p$

Let $G = G(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p \mid \mathbb{F}_p)$. It contains the Frobenius $\varphi$, defined by $x^{\varphi} = x^{p}$ for all $x \in \overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$. The cyclic group $\langle\varphi\rangle = \{\varphi^n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ has the same fixed field — namely $\mathbb{F}_p$ — as the whole group $G$. Yet $\langle\varphi\rangle \neq G$.

Why not? One constructs an automorphism $\psi$ that agrees with some power of Frobenius on each finite layer $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ but for which no single integer exponent works simultaneously for all $n$. Concretely, pick integers $a_n$ with $a_n \equiv a_m \ (\mathrm{mod}\ m)$ whenever $m \mid n$, but with no global integer $a$ satisfying $a_n \equiv a \ (\mathrm{mod}\ n)$ for all $n$. Setting $\psi|_{\mathbb{F}_{p^n}} = \varphi^{a_n}$ glues to a genuine automorphism of $\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$ that lives outside $\langle\varphi\rangle$. The coherent system $(a_n)$ is exactly an element of $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ that is not an ordinary integer — we will name this object in §4.2.

The picture — Frobenius to a "limit exponent"

Here is the honest mental image. The new automorphism $\psi$ behaves like "Frobenius raised to some exponent," except the exponent is not any whole number. On the layer $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ only the exponent modulo $n$ matters (because $\varphi^n$ is the identity on that layer). So we get to pick a residue mod 2, a compatible residue mod 3, mod 4, and so on — a tower of choices that agree wherever they overlap, yet correspond to no single integer. That coherent-but-rootless choice of exponent is precisely an element of $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ that lies outside the ordinary integers $\mathbb{Z}$. The Frobenius $\varphi$ is exponent $1$; $\psi$ is a "limit exponent" that no integer reaches — which is exactly why $\langle\varphi\rangle \subsetneq G$. Keep this in mind: in §4.2 we will see $G\cong\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ and these limit exponents become literal elements of $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$.

The repair is to put a topology on the Galois group. For a (finite or infinite) Galois extension $\Omega\mid k$ with group $G = G(\Omega\mid k)$, the Krull topology declares the cosets

$$\sigma\, G(\Omega\mid K)$$

to be a basis of neighbourhoods of $\sigma$, as $K\mid k$ ranges over the finite Galois subextensions. In words: two automorphisms are "close" if they agree on a large finite subextension. Multiplication and inversion are continuous, so $G$ is a topological group.

Proposition 4.1.1 — the Galois group is compact Hausdorff

For every Galois extension $\Omega\mid k$, the group $G = G(\Omega\mid k)$ is compact and Hausdorff in the Krull topology.

How the proof goes

Hausdorff is easy: if $\sigma \neq \tau$, they already disagree on some finite Galois $K\mid k$, and then their basic neighbourhoods are disjoint. Compactness comes from embedding $G$ as a closed subgroup of the product $\prod_K G(K\mid k)$ of the finite Galois groups (each given the discrete topology). The product is compact by Tychonoff's theorem; the embedding $\sigma \mapsto (\sigma|_K)_K$ is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset, namely the tuples that are compatible under restriction. That compatibility condition is what cuts the full product down to $G$.

Theorem 4.1.2 — main theorem, infinite version

Let $\Omega\mid k$ be Galois. The assignment $K \mapsto G(\Omega\mid K)$ is a bijection between intermediate fields $K$ and closed subgroups of $G$. Under it, the open subgroups correspond precisely to the finite subextensions.

Two facts power the proof. Every open subgroup is automatically closed (it is the complement of the union of its other cosets). And a closed subgroup $H$ equals $G(\Omega\mid K)$ for $K$ its fixed field, because the finite-Galois theorem makes $H$ dense in $G(\Omega\mid K)$, and a closed dense subgroup is everything. The "open $\Leftrightarrow$ finite" part uses compactness: only finitely many cosets can cover a compact group.

Definition 4.1.3 — profinite group

A profinite group is a topological group $G$ that is Hausdorff, compact, and has a basis of neighbourhoods of the identity $1$ consisting of normal subgroups.

The last condition is equivalent to $G$ being totally disconnected — meaning the only connected pieces of the space are single points; equivalently, the finite quotients chop $G$ into small clopen (closed-and-open) chunks, with no continuous "lumps" left to bridge them. Every Galois group is profinite, and a closed subgroup $H$ is open exactly when its index $(G:H)$ is finite. The slogan is: profinite groups are the compact, totally-disconnected limits of finite groups, and we can reconstruct them from their finite quotients. To make "reconstruct from finite quotients" precise we need limits — next section.

4.2 Projective and Inductive Limits

Projective ("inverse") and inductive ("direct") limits generalize intersection and union. If $\{X_i\}$ is a family of subsets closed under intersections, then $\varprojlim X_i = \bigcap_i X_i$; if it is closed under unions, the inductive limit is $\varinjlim X_i = \bigcup_i X_i$. The indexing set becomes a directed system: a partial order where any two indices have a common upper bound.

Definition 4.2.1 — projective / inductive system

Over a directed set $I$, a projective system is a family $\{X_i, f_{ij}\}$ of spaces with continuous maps $f_{ij}\colon X_j \to X_i$ (arrows point down) satisfying $f_{ii} = \mathrm{id}$ and the cocycle rule $f_{ik} = f_{ij}\circ f_{jk}$. An inductive system reverses the arrows: $f_{ij}\colon X_i \to X_j$.

Definition 4.2.2 — the limits

The projective limit is the subspace of the product consisting of compatible threads:

$$\varprojlim_{i} X_i = \Big\{ (x_i)_i \in \prod_i X_i \ \Big|\ f_{ij}(x_j) = x_i \text{ for } i \le j \Big\}.$$

The inductive limit is the disjoint union $\coprod_i X_i$ modulo the relation that identifies $x_i$ and $x_j$ once they become equal somewhere downstream: $\exists\, k\colon f_{ik}(x_i) = f_{jk}(x_j)$.

Mental picture

A projective limit is a "coherent infinite memory": an element is a choice at every stage that is consistent under the bonding maps — like a decimal expansion, where each truncation determines the shorter ones. An inductive limit is a "growing union": an element lives at some finite stage and you forget where it came from once two stages agree.

Pause — compactness is the key feature

Proposition 4.2.3. The projective limit of nonempty compact spaces is nonempty and compact. Nonemptiness is the subtle half: it is exactly the finite-intersection-property argument inside the compact product. This is the technical heart that makes profinite groups behave well, and it is why §4.5–§4.6 can solve infinitely many equations "at once" by solving them at each finite layer.

For systems of groups (or rings, modules) with continuous homomorphisms, both limits are again topological groups, and they satisfy the expected universal properties (Prop. 4.2.5): a compatible family of maps $H \to G_i$ factors uniquely through $\varprojlim G_i$, and dually for $\varinjlim$. So these are categorical limits — they are the universal solutions, not ad hoc constructions.

Exactness — and its one restriction

The inductive limit functor is always exact (Prop. 4.2.6): a short exact sequence at each level gives an exact sequence of limits, full stop. The projective limit is exact only for compact groups (Prop. 4.2.7). This asymmetry is not pedantry — it is precisely why the compactness baked into "profinite" matters, and why we keep insisting our groups be compact.

Proposition 4.2.8 — profinite = projective limit of finite

If $G$ is profinite and $N$ ranges over its open normal subgroups, then topologically and algebraically

$$G \;=\; \varprojlim_{N} G/N.$$

Conversely, every projective limit of finite (or profinite) groups is profinite.

This is the structural theorem promised in §4.1. It packages a profinite group as the totality of its finite quotients, stitched together compatibly. Now the examples — these are the objects you will meet again and again.

Example — the absolute Galois group as a limit

For Galois $\Omega\mid k$, the open normal subgroups are the $G(\Omega\mid K)$ for finite Galois $K\mid k$, and $G(K\mid k) = G(\Omega\mid k)/G(\Omega\mid K)$. Hence

$$G(\Omega\mid k) = \varprojlim_{K} G(K\mid k).$$
Example — the $p$-adic integers and $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$

The rings $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ form a projective system under reduction; their limit is the ring of $p$-adic integers,

$$\mathbb{Z}_p = \varprojlim_{n} \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}.$$

Taking instead all $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ ordered by divisibility gives the Prüfer ring $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$:

$$\widehat{\mathbb{Z}} = \varprojlim_{n} \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \;=\; \prod_{p} \mathbb{Z}_p,$$

the last equality by the Chinese remainder theorem passed to the limit. The ordinary integers $\mathbb{Z}$ sit inside $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ as a dense subring via the diagonal $a \mapsto (a,a,a,\dots)$ — dense but not equal, which is the abstract shadow of "$\langle\varphi\rangle \neq G$".

Example — Frobenius generates $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$

For $\mathbb{F}_q$ we have $G(\mathbb{F}_{q^n}\mid\mathbb{F}_q) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, sending $\varphi^n$... more precisely sending Frobenius to $1$. Passing to the limit,

$$G(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q\mid\mathbb{F}_q) \cong \widehat{\mathbb{Z}},$$

with Frobenius $\varphi$ mapping to $1 \in \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ and the cyclic subgroup $\langle\varphi\rangle$ onto the dense subgroup $\mathbb{Z}\subset\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$. The mysterious $\psi$ from §4.1 is now just some element of $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}\smallsetminus\mathbb{Z}$, e.g. $(\dots,0,1_p,0,\dots) \in \prod_\ell \mathbb{Z}_\ell$.

Example — cyclotomic $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$ and procyclic groups

Adjoining all roots of unity to $\mathbb{Q}$ gives $G(\mathbb{Q}(\mu_\infty)\mid\mathbb{Q}) \cong \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$, from the limit of $G(\mathbb{Q}(\mu_n)\mid\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$. A procyclic group is one topologically generated by a single element; $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ and $\mathbb{Z}_p$ are the basic examples, and every procyclic group is a quotient of $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$. Its open subgroups are exactly the $G^n$.

4.3 Abstract Galois Theory

Now the bold move. We discard fields and keep only a profinite group $G$, but we keep speaking the language of fields. Every profinite group really is some Galois group $G(\bar k\mid k)$, so the dictionary below is not a metaphor — it is a faithful translation that lets you reuse all your Galois intuition.

The dictionary
  • Closed subgroups $G_K$ are indexed by symbols $K$ we call "fields"; $K$ is the fixed field of $G_K$.
  • The base field $k$ has $G_k = G$; the top field $\bar k$ has $G_{\bar k} = \{1\}$.
  • We write $L\mid K$ (an extension) when $G_L \subseteq G_K$. It is finite if $G_L$ is open, with degree $[L:K] = (G_K : G_L)$.
  • It is Galois if $G_L$ is normal in $G_K$, and then $G(L\mid K) = G_K/G_L$.
A worked anchor — running the dictionary on $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$

Take the bare group $G = \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$. The dictionary says: pretend it is a Galois group — and indeed it is the genuine one $G(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p\mid\mathbb{F}_p)$, with base "field" $\mathbb{F}_p$. The open subgroups of $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ are the $n\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$; under the dictionary the subgroup $n\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is the "field" $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$. Its degree over the base is the index, and the two agree on the nose:

$$[\mathbb{F}_{p^n}:\mathbb{F}_p] = n = (\widehat{\mathbb{Z}} : n\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}).$$

So "treat $G$ as a Galois group" is not a figure of speech — every line of the dictionary checks out against a familiar finite-field tower.

Next we need objects for the Galois group to act on. A continuous (multiplicative) $G$-module $A$ is an abelian group on which $G$ acts (written exponentially, on the right: $a^\sigma$) satisfying the module axioms plus a continuity condition: every element is fixed by some open subgroup, i.e.

$$A = \bigcup_{[K:k]<\infty} A_K, \qquad A_K = \{ a \in A \mid a^\sigma = a \ \forall\, \sigma \in G_K\}.$$
Why right action and exponential notation

Writing $a^\sigma$ makes expressions like $a^{\sigma-1} := a^\sigma a^{-1}$ read like ordinary exponent arithmetic, which is enormously convenient in the cocycle computations to come. Mentally, $A_K$ is "the $K$-rational points" — when $A = \bar k^\times$, this is literally $K^\times$.

For a finite extension $L\mid K$ there is a norm map $N_{L\mid K}\colon A_L \to A_K$, defined by $a \mapsto \prod_{\sigma} a^\sigma$ — exactly the "multiply all the Galois conjugates together" operation promised in §4.1. Before naming the two stars of the theory we need one auxiliary gadget. The augmentation ideal action $I_{G}A_L$ (with $G = G(L\mid K)$) is the subgroup of $A_L$ generated by all the elements $a^{\sigma-1} = a^{\sigma}a^{-1}$ as $\sigma$ runs over $G$ and $a$ over $A_L$ — think "everything you can build out of conjugate-ratios." When $G(L\mid K)$ is cyclic with generator $\sigma$, it simplifies to $I_{G}A_L = A_L^{\sigma-1}$. With that in hand, the two stars are the norm residue group and its companion:

$$H^0\big(G(L\mid K), A_L\big) = A_K / N_{L\mid K}A_L,$$ $$H^{-1}\big(G(L\mid K), A_L\big) = {}_{N}A_L \big/ I_{G(L\mid K)}A_L.$$

Here ${}_{N}A_L = \{a \in A_L : N_{L\mid K}(a)=1\}$ is the group of norm-one elements.

What these two groups actually measure

Do not be put off by the superscripts. The slogan is simple. $H^0 = A_K/N_{L\mid K}A_L$ measures how far the norm map is from being surjective: it is trivial exactly when every element of the base is a norm from upstairs. $H^{-1} = {}_{N}A_L/I_G A_L$ measures how far the norm is from being injective in the relevant sense: it is trivial exactly when the only norm-one elements are the "obvious" conjugate-ratios $a^{\sigma-1}$. The strange superscripts $0$ and $-1$ come from Tate cohomology, a bookkeeping device that strings these groups into a doubly-infinite sequence — but for this entire chapter you only ever need these two. Treat $H^0$ and $H^{-1}$ as names for "norm-cokernel" and "norm-one-kernel" and you will not miss anything.

Worked example — Kummer theory (optional aside — safe to skip on a first pass)

Suppose $\wp\colon A \to A$ is a surjective $G$-homomorphism with finite cyclic kernel $\mu_\wp$ of order $n$ — the case to keep in mind is the $n$-th power map $a \mapsto a^n$ with kernel the $\mu_n$. Write $A_K^\wp$ for the image $\wp(A_K)$ of the base group under this operator (for the power map, the $n$-th powers in $A_K$). Assume $\mu_\wp \subseteq A_K$ and impose Axiom (3.1): $H^{-1}(G(L\mid K), A_L)=1$ for all finite cyclic $L\mid K$.

The Kummer correspondence (Thm. 4.3.3) is then a bijection

$$\Delta \ \longleftrightarrow\ L = K(\wp^{-1}(\Delta))$$

between groups $A_K^\wp \subseteq \Delta \subseteq A_K$ and abelian extensions $L\mid K$ of exponent $n$ (meaning every element of the Galois group satisfies $\sigma^n = 1$ — the group is killed by $n$), with the perfect pairing (a duality that is nondegenerate on both sides — neither factor has a nonzero element pairing trivially with everything)

$$\Delta / A_K^{\wp} \;\xrightarrow{\sim}\; \mathrm{Hom}\big(G(L\mid K), \mu_\wp\big), \qquad a \mapsto \chi_a,\ \ \chi_a(\sigma)=\alpha^{\sigma-1}.$$

With $A = \bar k^\times$ and $\mu_n\subseteq K$ this is the classical statement: abelian extensions of exponent $n$ are exactly the $K(\sqrt[n]{\Delta})$. The whole thing rests on Axiom (3.1), which in this case is Hilbert 90.

Theorem 4.3.5 — Hilbert 90

For a cyclic field extension $L\mid K$ one always has $H^{-1}(G(L\mid K), L^\times)=1$. Equivalently: every $a\in L^\times$ of norm $N_{L\mid K}(a)=1$ has the form $a = \beta^{\sigma-1}$, $\sigma$ a generator.

Hilbert 90 in one line, and Noether's upgrade

The proof is a clever averaging: linear independence of the automorphisms $1,\sigma,\dots,\sigma^{n-1}$ lets you build a $\beta$ that works. Emmy Noether generalized this to arbitrary finite Galois $L\mid K$ via cohomology: $H^1(G(L\mid K), L^\times)=1$ (Prop. 4.3.8). For cyclic groups, $H^1(G,A)\cong H^{-1}(G,A)$, which links the two formulations. In characteristic $p$ the additive analogue with operator $\wp(a)=a^p-a$ gives Artin–Schreier theory.

4.4 Abstract Valuation Theory

Here we add the first of the two extra data. Fix a surjective continuous homomorphism

$$d\colon G \twoheadrightarrow \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}.$$

This is the abstract version of "read off the residue field." In the model case where $G$ is the absolute Galois group of a $p$-adic field $k$ with residue field $\mathbb{F}_q$, the maximal unramified extension $\tilde k\mid k$ has $G(\tilde k\mid k)\cong G(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_q\mid\mathbb{F}_q)\cong\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$, and $d$ is "restrict to $\tilde k$, then apply that isomorphism." The element of $G(\tilde k\mid k)$ mapping to $1$ is the Frobenius.

Inertia, unramified extensions, Frobenius
  • The inertia group over $K$ is $I_K = \ker(d|_{G_K})$. Its fixed field $\tilde K$ is the maximal unramified extension of $K$, equal to the composite $K\tilde k$.
  • Set $f_K = (\widehat{\mathbb{Z}} : d(G_K))$ and $e_K = (I : I_K)$. When $f_K$ is finite, $d_K = \tfrac{1}{f_K} d$ induces $G(\tilde K\mid K)\xrightarrow{\sim}\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$.
  • Definition 4.4.1. The Frobenius over $K$ is the element $\varphi_K \in G(\tilde K\mid K)$ with $d_K(\varphi_K)=1$.

Inertia degree $f_{L\mid K}$ and ramification index $e_{L\mid K}$ are defined by the obvious index formulas, are multiplicative in towers, and obey the

Proposition 4.4.2 — the fundamental identity $$[L:K] = f_{L\mid K}\, e_{L\mid K}.$$

The extension is unramified if $e_{L\mid K}=1$ (so $L\subseteq\tilde K$), totally ramified if $f_{L\mid K}=1$. Every extension factors: the maximal unramified subextension is $L\cap\tilde K\mid K$, of degree $f_{L\mid K}$.

Pause — the Frobenius lift (this is the engine)

"The Frobenius governs the entire theory like a king." The crucial, non-obvious fact: in a finite Galois $L\mid K$, every element of $G(L\mid K)$ can be turned into a Frobenius by lifting it to the right enlarged extension. Concretely, work inside $\tilde L\mid K$ and consider the semigroup

$$\mathrm{Frob}(\tilde L\mid K) = \{\sigma \in G(\tilde L\mid K) \mid d_K(\sigma)\in\mathbb{N}\}.$$
A concrete instance — lifting in the $p$-adic model

Make this tangible in the model case $G(\tilde k\mid k)\cong\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ for a $p$-adic base $k$. Suppose $L\mid K$ is ramified, so an automorphism $\sigma\in G(L\mid K)$ need not be a Frobenius on its own — say $\sigma$ generates the inertia, with $d_K(\sigma)=0$. There is no Frobenius down at this level: $0$ is not a positive integer. The repair is to climb one notch into $\tilde L$ and pick the enlarged element $\tilde\sigma$ that restricts to $\sigma$ on $L$ and to the Frobenius on the unramified part $\tilde k$ — i.e. whose $d_K$ on the unramified coordinate is $1$. Now $d_K(\tilde\sigma)=1\in\mathbb{N}$, so $\tilde\sigma\in\mathrm{Frob}(\tilde L\mid K)$, and over its own fixed field $\Sigma$ it literally is the Frobenius. The point of the positive-integer condition is exactly this: $d_K(\tilde\sigma)$ counts how many genuine unramified layers the lift advances, and that count is a positive integer (here $1$) — never a fractional or limit exponent.

Propositions 4.4.4–4.4.5 — lifting to Frobenius

The restriction $\mathrm{Frob}(\tilde L\mid K)\to G(L\mid K)$, $\sigma\mapsto\sigma|_L$, is surjective (4.4.4). And if $\sigma\in\mathrm{Frob}(\tilde L\mid K)$ has fixed field $\Sigma$, then (4.4.5):

  • $f_{\Sigma\mid K} = d_K(\sigma)$, and $[\Sigma:K]<\infty$;
  • $\tilde\Sigma = \tilde L$ — lifting one notch up makes $\tilde L$ the unramified closure of $\Sigma$;
  • $\sigma = \varphi_\Sigma$ — over its own fixed field, the lift literally is the Frobenius.
Why this matters so much

This is the secret behind the whole reciprocity construction. The reciprocity map will be defined on Frobenius elements (where there is an obvious right answer: Frobenius corresponds to a prime element). Proposition 4.4.4 says every Galois automorphism is the restriction of some Frobenius, so the definition reaches everyone; Proposition 4.4.5 says the construction is forced and consistent. Hold the diagram in your head: a tower $K \subseteq \Sigma,\ L \subseteq \tilde L = \tilde\Sigma$ with $\sigma=\varphi_\Sigma$ on top.

The second new datum is a henselian valuation, the abstract version of a discrete valuation that "reproduces itself" up every finite extension.

Definition 4.4.6 — henselian valuation

A homomorphism $v\colon A_k \to \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is a henselian valuation with respect to $d$ if:

  • $v(A_k) = Z \supseteq \mathbb{Z}$ with $Z/nZ = \widehat{\mathbb{Z}}/n\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ for all $n$;
  • $v(N_{K\mid k}A_K) = f_K\, Z$ for all finite $K\mid k$.
In the model case — this is just the $p$-adic valuation

The definition reads abstractly, but it is modelled on one familiar object. Let $k$ be a $p$-adic field with $A = \bar k^\times$, so $A_k = k^\times$. Then $v$ is the ordinary normalized valuation $v\colon k^\times \twoheadrightarrow \mathbb{Z}$ that counts the power of the prime — "how many times $p$ divides." A prime element is a uniformizer $\pi$ with $v(\pi)=1$; the units $U_k = \ker v = \mathcal{O}_k^\times$ are exactly the invertible elements of the ring of integers. And the second axiom is concrete: passing to a finite extension $K\mid k$, the value group of the norms scales by the residue degree — $v(N_{K\mid k}A_K) = f_K\,\mathbb{Z}$ — which is the classical fact that taking a norm down to $k$ multiplies valuations by $f_K$. Everything below is this picture, stripped of the field.

Just like $d$, the valuation propagates: over any finite $K\mid k$ one gets $v_K = \tfrac{1}{f_K}\, v\circ N_{K\mid k}$, surjective onto $Z$, compatible with conjugation and with norms in towers (Prop. 4.4.7). An element $\pi_K$ with $v_K(\pi_K)=1$ is a prime element, and $U_K = \ker v_K$ the units. In the unramified case prime elements survive upward; in the totally ramified case norms of prime elements are prime elements.

4.5 The Reciprocity Map

We now assemble the pieces. The data: a profinite $G$, a continuous $G$-module $A$, a surjection $d\colon G\to\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$, and a henselian valuation $v$. One axiom is in force throughout:

Axiom 5.1 — the unramified cohomology vanishes

For every unramified finite extension $L\mid K$,

$$H^i\big(G(L\mid K), U_L\big) = 1 \quad\text{for } i = 0, -1.$$

In plain words (recall the slogan from §4.3): the $i=0$ half says every unit downstairs is the norm of some unit upstairs (the norm map on units is onto), and the $i=-1$ half says the only units of norm one are the obvious conjugate-ratios — together an unramified analogue of Hilbert 90. This is the weak axiom: it only constrains the unramified extensions and is just enough to build the reciprocity map. The strong version, Axiom 6.1 in §4.6, will constrain all cyclic extensions and is what upgrades that map to an isomorphism.

Goal: a canonical homomorphism $r_{L\mid K}\colon G(L\mid K)\to A_K/N_{L\mid K}A_L$ for every finite Galois $L\mid K$. The construction follows the §4.4 strategy exactly.

Definition 5.2 — reciprocity on Frobenius elements

First define a map on the semigroup $\mathrm{Frob}(\tilde L\mid K)$. For $\sigma\in\mathrm{Frob}(\tilde L\mid K)$ with fixed field $\Sigma$ and prime element $\pi_\Sigma\in A_\Sigma$, set

$$r_{\tilde L\mid K}(\sigma) = N_{\Sigma\mid K}(\pi_\Sigma) \ \bmod\ N_{L\mid K}A_{\tilde L}.$$
Read the definition slowly — it is forced

The guiding principle of all of class field theory: Frobenius automorphisms correspond to prime elements. Over its fixed field $\Sigma$, the element $\sigma$ is the Frobenius $\varphi_\Sigma$ (Prop. 4.4.5), so it should map to a prime element $\pi_\Sigma$. To push that down to the base $K$, functoriality demands the norm $N_{\Sigma\mid K}$. Here is why it has to be the norm and not some other map. On the group side, enlarging the base from $K$ to $\Sigma$ is an inclusion of Galois groups $G_\Sigma \hookrightarrow G_K$. When you dualize that inclusion over to the module side, an inclusion of subgroups turns into the operation "multiply over all the cosets" — and multiplying an element by all its conjugates is precisely the norm. So the map down to the base is forced to be $N_{\Sigma\mid K}$; the formula writes itself. Sanity check: for an unramified extension of finite fields, $G(L\mid K)$ is cyclic generated by Frobenius, and the norm $a\mapsto a\cdot a^{q}\cdots a^{q^{n-1}}$ is exactly the classical field norm — the recipe above reproduces what you already know. Independence of $\pi_\Sigma$: a different prime differs by a unit, and Axiom 5.1 ensures the unit's norm lands in the norm group, killing it modulo $N_{L\mid K}A_{\tilde L}$.

The hard part is that this map is multiplicative (Prop. 5.5). The proof manipulates the endomorphisms $\sigma-1$ and $\sigma_n = \tfrac{\sigma^n-1}{\sigma-1}$ of $A_{\tilde L}$ and rests on two lemmas about the norm $N = N_{\tilde L\mid \tilde k}$: a computation of $N_{\Sigma\mid K}(a) = (N\circ\varphi^n)(a)$ (Lemma 5.3), and a criterion (Lemma 5.4) that an element fixed by Frobenius has norm in $N_{L\mid K}U_L$. Both lemmas are pure §4.2 compactness plus Axiom 5.1.

Proposition 5.6 — the reciprocity homomorphism

Because $\mathrm{Frob}(\tilde L\mid K)\to G(L\mid K)$ is surjective and the map kills the ambiguity, it descends to a canonical homomorphism

$$r_{L\mid K}\colon G(L\mid K) \longrightarrow A_K / N_{L\mid K}A_L,$$

the reciprocity homomorphism: $r_{L\mid K}(\sigma) = N_{\Sigma\mid K}(\pi_\Sigma) \bmod N_{L\mid K}A_L$ for any Frobenius lift.

The cleanest case — Proposition 5.7, unramified extensions

If $L\mid K$ is unramified, then $\varphi_K$ lifts the Frobenius automorphism, its fixed field is just $K$, and

$$r_{L\mid K}(\varphi_{L\mid K}) = \pi_K \ \bmod\ N_{L\mid K}A_L.$$

Moreover $r_{L\mid K}$ is an isomorphism here. The check uses the valuation: $v_K$ induces $A_K/N_{L\mid K}A_L \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ (with $n=[L:K]$), matching $G(L\mid K)\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and Frobenius $\mapsto 1$ on both sides.

The whole story in one concrete case — unramified $p$-adic fields

Let $K$ be a $p$-adic field and $L\mid K$ the unramified extension of degree $n$, with $A = \bar K^\times$ so that $A_K = K^\times$. The Galois group is cyclic, generated by Frobenius: $G(L\mid K)\cong\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. The reciprocity map $r$ sends Frobenius to a prime element $\pi$ of $K$, read modulo the norm group $N_{L\mid K}L^\times$. Trace the three groups around:

$$G(L\mid K) \;\xrightarrow[\ \pi\,\mathrm{mod}\,N\ ]{\ \varphi\,\mapsto\,}\; K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times \;\xrightarrow[\ \sim\ ]{\ v_K\ }\; \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}.$$

The right-hand isomorphism is just the valuation $v_K$: an element's class in $K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times$ is pinned down entirely by its valuation modulo $n$, because norms from the unramified $L$ have valuation divisible by $n$. So the valuation is the isomorphism, and the chain $\varphi \mapsto \pi \mapsto 1$ reads: "Frobenius corresponds to a prime element, whose valuation is $1$." This single line is local class field theory for unramified extensions, and it is the seed that the abstract machine waters into the general statement.

Functoriality — the two diagrams you will reuse

Proposition 5.8 records how $r_{L\mid K}$ behaves under enlarging the base (left diagram: restriction $\leftrightarrow$ norm $N_{K'\mid K}$) and under conjugation by $\sigma\in G$ (right diagram). Proposition 5.9 adds a subtler one via the group-theoretic transfer (Verlagerung) $\mathrm{Ver}\colon G^{\mathrm{ab}}\to H^{\mathrm{ab}}$: enlarging the base to an intermediate field corresponds to the transfer on the Galois side. These compatibilities are what let the abstract map specialize correctly to both local and global theory.

4.6 The General Reciprocity Law

Everything so far used only the weak Axiom 5.1 (unramified vanishing). To upgrade $r_{L\mid K}$ from a homomorphism to an isomorphism we strengthen it to a statement about all cyclic extensions.

Axiom 6.1 — the Class Field Axiom

For every cyclic extension $L\mid K$,

$$\#H^i\big(G(L\mid K), A_L\big) = \begin{cases} [L:K], & i = 0,\\ 1, & i = -1.\end{cases}$$
What the axiom buys you

The $i=-1$ half is Hilbert-90-style (norm-one elements are "trivial"); the $i=0$ half pins the size of the norm residue group at exactly $[L:K]$. Since the unramified extensions are among the cyclic ones, Axiom 6.1 implies Axiom 5.1 (Prop. 6.2), so the reciprocity map of §4.5 already exists. A class field theory is precisely a pair $(d\colon G\to\widehat{\mathbb{Z}},\ v\colon A\to\widehat{\mathbb{Z}})$ with $A$ satisfying Axiom 6.1.

Theorem 6.3 — the General Reciprocity Law

For every finite Galois extension $L\mid K$, the reciprocity homomorphism

$$r_{L\mid K}\colon G(L\mid K)^{\mathrm{ab}} \;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\; A_K / N_{L\mid K}A_L$$

is an isomorphism. This is the main theorem of the chapter — proved once, abstractly.

The proof in three reductions

The strategy is a beautiful cascade of reductions, all driven by the exact commutative diagram (from Prop. 5.8) relating $r_{L\mid M}, r_{L\mid K}, r_{M\mid K}$ for a Galois subextension $M\mid K$:

  • Reduce to abelian: the commutator subgroup is exactly the kernel, and a Sylow argument — which lets you verify surjectivity one prime at a time, on each $p$-Sylow piece separately — handles surjectivity, so it suffices to treat $G(L\mid K)$ abelian.
  • Reduce to cyclic: an abelian group injects into the product of its cyclic quotients — so to prove an isomorphism it is enough to test it on each cyclic piece — and thus injectivity (and, by induction on degree, surjectivity) reduces to cyclic $L\mid K$.
  • Reduce to totally ramified: peel off the maximal unramified part, where Prop. 5.7 already gave an isomorphism; the remaining $f_{L\mid K}=1$ case is settled by a direct computation with prime elements that uses Axiom 6.1 to force $k=0$ in $r_{L\mid K}(\sigma^k)=1$.

Inverting $r_{L\mid K}$ gives the norm residue symbol

$$(\,\cdot\,, L\mid K)\colon A_K \twoheadrightarrow G(L\mid K)^{\mathrm{ab}}, \qquad \ker = N_{L\mid K}A_L.$$

For the special extension $\tilde K\mid K$ it reads off the valuation cleanly (Prop. 6.5): $(a, \tilde K\mid K) = \varphi_K^{\,v_K(a)}$, so $d_K\circ(\,\cdot\,,\tilde K\mid K)=v_K$. This is the precise sense in which "the valuation tells you which power of Frobenius you get."

Theorem 6.7 — the existence theorem / class field correspondence

Give $A_K$ the norm topology (basic neighbourhoods $a\,N_{L\mid K}A_L$). Then

$$L \ \longmapsto\ \mathcal{N}_L = N_{L\mid K}A_L$$

is an inclusion-reversing bijection between finite abelian extensions $L\mid K$ and open subgroups $\mathcal{N}$ of $A_K$, with

$$\mathcal{N}_{L_1 L_2} = \mathcal{N}_{L_1}\cap\mathcal{N}_{L_2}, \qquad \mathcal{N}_{L_1\cap L_2} = \mathcal{N}_{L_1}\mathcal{N}_{L_2}.$$

The field $L$ matching $\mathcal{N}$ is the class field of $\mathcal{N}$, and $G(L\mid K)\cong A_K/\mathcal{N}$.

The punchline — abelian extensions live inside the base field

This is the deepest dividend: the abelian extensions of $K$ are catalogued by subgroups of $A_K$, an object built purely from $K$ itself. You never have to leave the base field to know its abelian world. In the local case $A_K = K^\times$, this becomes $G(L\mid K)\cong K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times$ — exactly the local class field theory of chapter V. The same abstract theorem, fed the global module, yields global class field theory in chapter VI.

4.7 The Herbrand Quotient

The abstract theory is complete — but it is useless until you can verify Axiom 6.1 in a concrete situation. Checking $\#H^0 = [L:K]$ and $\#H^{-1}=1$ directly is painful. The Herbrand quotient is the tool that makes it tractable, by trading the two orders for a single, beautifully multiplicative invariant.

Definition 7.2 — the Herbrand quotient

Let $G$ be finite cyclic and $A$ a $G$-module with both $H^0(G,A), H^{-1}(G,A)$ finite. The Herbrand quotient is

$$h(G,A) = \frac{\#H^0(G,A)}{\#H^{-1}(G,A)}.$$
Proposition 7.1 — the exact hexagon

A short exact sequence $1\to A\to B\to C\to 1$ of $G$-modules induces a cyclic six-term exact sequence

$$H^0(A)\to H^0(B)\to H^0(C)\to H^{-1}(A)\to H^{-1}(B)\to H^{-1}(C)\to H^0(A)$$

that closes up into a hexagon, using the cyclicity of $G$ (which makes the cohomology 2-periodic).

Proposition 7.3 — multiplicativity, the whole point

From the hexagon, for an exact sequence $1\to A\to B\to C\to 1$,

$$h(G,B) = h(G,A)\, h(G,C),$$

whenever any two of the three are defined (then so is the third). And for any finite $G$-module $A$, $h(G,A)=1$.

Why these two properties are exactly what you want

The Herbrand quotient is insensitive to finite pieces and multiplicative across exact sequences. So to compute it for a complicated module — say the units $\mathcal{O}_L^\times$ of a number field — you may freely replace the module by any finite-index sub- or super-module, and break it apart along exact sequences, because all the finite "noise" cancels. This is precisely how the global class field axiom gets verified in chapter VI: compute $h$ of the idele class group by dismantling it into manageable lattice pieces. The quotient sees only the "rank," never the torsion.

The other workhorse is the behaviour of induced modules. For a subgroup $g\subseteq G$ and a $g$-module $B$, the induced module $A = \mathrm{Ind}_g^G(B)$ consists of functions $f\colon G\to B$ with $f(x\tau)=f(x)^\tau$; when $g$ has finite index it splits as $A = \prod_{\sigma\in G/g} B^\sigma$.

Proposition 7.4 — Shapiro's lemma

For $G$ finite cyclic, $g\subseteq G$, and a $g$-module $B$,

$$H^i\big(G, \mathrm{Ind}_g^G(B)\big) \;\cong\; H^i(g, B), \qquad i = 0, -1.$$
What Shapiro does for you

First, the one-line picture of an induced module $\mathrm{Ind}_g^G(B)$: it is assembled from a smaller $g$-module $B$ by laying down one copy of $B$ over each coset of $g$ in $G$ and letting $G$ shuffle the copies. Shapiro's lemma says the slogan exactly: the $G$-cohomology of the inflated module equals the $g$-cohomology of the original seed $B$. So induced modules compute cohomology of the subgroup. In number theory the units or ideles of $L$, viewed as a module over $G(L\mid K)$, decompose locally as induced modules from decomposition groups (the subgroup of automorphisms fixing a chosen prime above — the "local Galois group at that place") — and Shapiro reduces a global cohomology computation to a sum of local ones. Combined with multiplicativity and the finite-module triviality, this is the entire toolkit needed to land the class field axiom in practice. Hilbert 90 itself (Prop. 4.3.4, additive case) is "$L$ is induced, by the normal basis theorem."

Summary — the machine, and why it runs twice

This chapter built an axiomatic engine and proved its central theorem once and for all. Here is the assembly order:

  • §4.1 Infinite Galois theory. Galois groups are profinite — compact, Hausdorff, totally disconnected. The main theorem survives if you restrict to closed subgroups. The $\mathbb{F}_p$ example shows $\langle\varphi\rangle \subsetneq G$.
  • §4.2 Limits. Projective limits generalize intersection (compactness gives nonemptiness and exactness for compact groups); inductive limits generalize union (always exact). Profinite $=$ projective limit of finite. Meet $\mathbb{Z}_p$, $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$, $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^\times$.
  • §4.3 Abstract Galois theory. Treat a bare profinite $G$ as a Galois group; act on a continuous $G$-module $A$. The groups $H^0, H^{-1}$, Kummer theory, Hilbert 90.
  • §4.4 Abstract valuation theory. Add $d\colon G\to\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ and a henselian valuation $v$. The Frobenius lift: every Galois element restricts from a Frobenius — the structural engine.
  • §4.5 The reciprocity map. Under Axiom 5.1, define $r_{L\mid K}$ by sending Frobenius to a prime element, then norming down. It is a canonical homomorphism, an isomorphism in the unramified case.
  • §4.6 The reciprocity law. Under the Class Field Axiom 6.1, $r_{L\mid K}$ is an isomorphism; the norm residue symbol and the class field correspondence classify abelian extensions by subgroups of $A_K$.
  • §4.7 The Herbrand quotient. The multiplicative, finite-blind invariant $h(G,A)$ that makes Axiom 6.1 verifiable; Shapiro's lemma localizes the computation.
The dividend

The abstraction pays off exactly as promised. Chapter V instantiates $A = \bar k^\times$ over a $p$-adic base and recovers local class field theory: $G(L\mid K)^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times$. Chapter VI instantiates the idele class module and recovers global class field theory. Same theorem, two faces. Verifying Axiom 6.1 in each — the only real work left — is what the Herbrand quotient was built for.

Knowledge check

These are designed to make you think — the wrong options are deliberately tempting. Decide on your answer before revealing the explanation.

Question 1

In the absolute Galois group $G = G(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p\mid\mathbb{F}_p)$, what is the precise relationship between the cyclic group $\langle\varphi\rangle$ generated by the Frobenius and the whole group $G$?

  1. $\langle\varphi\rangle = G$, since Frobenius already realizes every automorphism of $\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p$.
  2. $\langle\varphi\rangle$ is a proper but dense subgroup; it is the copy of $\mathbb{Z}$ sitting densely inside $G\cong\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$.
  3. $\langle\varphi\rangle$ is a proper closed subgroup whose fixed field strictly contains $\mathbb{F}_p$.
  4. $\langle\varphi\rangle$ is open of finite index, with $G/\langle\varphi\rangle$ a nontrivial finite group.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: B. Under $G\cong\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$, Frobenius maps to $1$ and $\langle\varphi\rangle$ to the dense subring $\mathbb{Z}\subset\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ — dense (it has the same fixed field, namely $\mathbb{F}_p$) but not all of $G$. A is the central misconception the §4.1 example is built to demolish: the automorphism $\psi$ with a "limit exponent" lies outside $\langle\varphi\rangle$. C is wrong because $\langle\varphi\rangle$ is not closed — its closure is all of $G$, and a closed subgroup having the same fixed field as $G$ would have to equal $G$. D fails because density forces the closure to be everything, so no open subgroup of finite index can contain it as a dense piece.

Question 2

Which description correctly distinguishes the projective limit from the inductive limit, and correctly classifies $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$?

  1. $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is the inductive limit of the $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, generalizing union; its elements live at one finite stage.
  2. Both limits coincide for the system $\{\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\}$, so the distinction is immaterial for $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$.
  3. $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is the projective limit of the $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$: an element is a compatible thread of residues, generalizing intersection.
  4. $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ is the projective limit, and projective limits are always exact, which is why $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ behaves well.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: C. $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}} = \varprojlim_n \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ consists of coherent threads of residues — the "coherent infinite memory" picture — and projective limits generalize intersection. A swaps the two limits: an inductive limit generalizes union and its elements live at a single finite stage, which is exactly what threads in $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$ do not do. B is false: for this non-trivial directed system the two limits are genuinely different objects. D states the correct limit but tacks on a false reason — projective limit is exact only for compact groups (Prop. 4.2.7); it is the inductive limit that is always exact.

Question 3

For a finite cyclic $L\mid K$, which statement about the cohomology groups $H^0$ and $H^{-1}$ is correct?

  1. $H^0 = A_K/N_{L\mid K}A_L$ measures failure of injectivity of the norm; $H^{-1}$ measures failure of surjectivity.
  2. $H^0 = A_K/N_{L\mid K}A_L$ measures failure of surjectivity of the norm; $H^{-1} = {}_N A_L/I_G A_L$ measures how far norm-one elements stray from conjugate-ratios.
  3. Both $H^0$ and $H^{-1}$ measure surjectivity of the norm, differing only by a sign convention from Tate cohomology.
  4. $H^0$ is the kernel of the norm and $H^{-1}$ its cokernel, so Hilbert 90 is the statement $H^0 = 1$.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: B. $H^0 = A_K/N_{L\mid K}A_L$ is the norm cokernel: it vanishes exactly when the norm is surjective. $H^{-1} = {}_N A_L/I_G A_L$ vanishes exactly when every norm-one element is an obvious conjugate-ratio $a^{\sigma-1}$. A inverts the roles — the easy slip of reading "$H^0$" as an injectivity (kernel) measure when it is really about surjectivity (cokernel). C collapses the two into one notion, erasing the distinct content of the $i=-1$ term. D mislabels Hilbert 90, which is the vanishing of $H^{-1}$ (norm-one elements are conjugate-ratios), not of $H^0$.

Question 4

Which statement about the Herbrand quotient $h(G,A) = \#H^0(G,A)/\#H^{-1}(G,A)$ is FALSE?

  1. For a short exact sequence $1\to A\to B\to C\to 1$, $h(G,B) = h(G,A)\,h(G,C)$ whenever two of the three are defined.
  2. For any finite $G$-module $A$, $h(G,A) = 1$.
  3. It is computed for finite cyclic $G$, using the 2-periodicity that closes the cohomology into a hexagon.
  4. It directly outputs the two orders $\#H^0$ and $\#H^{-1}$ separately, which is what makes Axiom 6.1 checkable.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: D. The Herbrand quotient outputs only the ratio of the two orders, not each separately — that lossiness is the price of its good behaviour. To finish verifying Axiom 6.1 you still pair it with $H^{-1}=1$ (often Hilbert 90) to recover $\#H^0 = [L:K]$. A is true and is the whole point — multiplicativity across exact sequences. B is true and crucial: finite modules contribute trivially, letting you discard torsion "noise." C is true: $h$ is defined for finite cyclic $G$ where 2-periodicity gives the exact hexagon of Prop. 7.1.

Question 5

In the unramified model — $K$ a p-adic field, $L\mid K$ unramified of degree $n$, $A_K = K^\times$ — what does the reciprocity map $r$ do, and why is it an isomorphism?

  1. It sends Frobenius to a prime element $\pi$; the valuation $v_K$ identifies $K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times$ with $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, matching $G(L\mid K)$.
  2. It sends Frobenius to a unit of $K$; the units generate $K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times$ because prime elements are already norms.
  3. It sends Frobenius to $0\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, since unramified means the valuation contributes nothing to the class.
  4. It is only a homomorphism here, not an isomorphism; bijectivity needs the totally ramified case where Axiom 6.1 does the work.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: A. The chain $\varphi\mapsto\pi\mapsto 1$ runs $G(L\mid K)\cong K^\times/N_{L\mid K}L^\times\xrightarrow{v_K}\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; norms from the unramified $L$ have valuation divisible by $n$, so the valuation modulo $n$ pins down the class and the map is an isomorphism (Prop. 5.7). B is backwards: Frobenius corresponds to a prime element of valuation $1$, not a unit; units form the kernel of $v_K$. C confuses the unramified case with the ramified one — here the valuation is precisely the nonzero datum (Frobenius has $d_K=1$). D is false: the unramified case is exactly where the isomorphism already holds under the weak Axiom 5.1, before any totally-ramified work.

Question 6

What is the actual difference between the weak axiom (5.1) and the Class Field Axiom (6.1), and what does each one deliver?

  1. Axiom 5.1 constrains all cyclic extensions and gives the isomorphism; Axiom 6.1 is the weaker unramified-only version used to define the map.
  2. The two axioms are equivalent restatements; the numbering merely separates the local and global cases.
  3. Axiom 5.1 vanishes cohomology only for unramified extensions and suffices to build the reciprocity homomorphism; Axiom 6.1 fixes $\#H^0=[L:K]$, $\#H^{-1}=1$ for all cyclic extensions, upgrading the map to an isomorphism.
  4. Axiom 6.1 only constrains unramified extensions but with an exact count, whereas Axiom 5.1 covers all Galois extensions qualitatively.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: C. The weak Axiom 5.1 only kills $H^0,H^{-1}$ on the units for unramified extensions — just enough to define $r_{L\mid K}$ and get the unramified isomorphism. The strong Axiom 6.1 pins the orders for all cyclic extensions, and that is what makes $r_{L\mid K}$ an isomorphism in general (and implies 5.1, Prop. 6.2). A swaps which axiom is which. B is false: they are genuinely different in strength — 6.1 is strictly stronger and implies 5.1, not vice versa. D mis-scopes 6.1 (it covers all cyclic, not just unramified) and overstates 5.1 (it is restricted to unramified, not all Galois).

Question 7

Neukirch builds class field theory abstractly — from a profinite $G$ with a Frobenius datum and a henselian valuation — rather than directly over number fields. What is the genuine payoff of this abstraction?

  1. It avoids group cohomology entirely, replacing it with elementary commutative algebra.
  2. The reciprocity theorem is proved once for the abstract setup, then instantiated to yield both local and global class field theory from the same machine.
  3. It proves a strictly stronger theorem than the classical one, valid for non-abelian extensions where the classical reciprocity fails.
  4. It removes the need to verify any axioms in concrete cases, since the abstract group already encodes the arithmetic.
Reveal answer & explanation

Answer: B. The architecture is "prove once, instantiate twice": the General Reciprocity Law (Thm. 6.3) holds for any data satisfying the Class Field Axiom, so feeding it $A=\bar k^\times$ over a p-adic base gives local class field theory (ch. V) and feeding it the idele class module gives global (ch. VI). A overstates the case: the theory still uses $H^0,H^{-1}$ and the Herbrand quotient — it avoids heavy cohomological dimension theory, not cohomology itself. C confuses the scope: reciprocity is intrinsically about the abelian quotient $G(L\mid K)^{\mathrm{ab}}$, and abstraction does not extend it to non-abelian extensions. D is the opposite of the truth: the abstraction relocates the real work into verifying Axiom 6.1 in each concrete case, which is exactly what the Herbrand quotient (§4.7) is built to do.
