Chapter 01

Algebraic Integers

A single question — which primes are sums of two squares — pulls us out of the integers and into a whole landscape of rings of integers, ideals, lattices, and class numbers that the rest of the book inhabits.

Neukirch Chapter I ~90 min reading time Prereqs: basic ring & field theory, Galois theory, linear algebra

Orientation: what this chapter is doing

This is the foundational chapter, and it is built around a single guiding idea: to understand the ordinary integers $\mathbb{Z}$, it pays to enlarge them. Diophantine questions about $\mathbb{Z}$ — like "which primes are $a^2+b^2$?" — become transparent once we work in a bigger ring of integers where the arithmetic factors more finely. Pursuing this systematically forces us to build a whole vocabulary, and that vocabulary is the real content of this chapter.

Here is the arc. Sections 1.1–1.2 introduce algebraic integers and the notion of integrality. Section 1.3 confronts the failure of unique factorization and Kummer/Dedekind's repair: ideals, which factor uniquely in a Dedekind domain. Sections 1.4–1.7 deploy geometry (lattices, Minkowski's theorem) to prove the two great finiteness theorems: the class number is finite (1.6) and the unit group is finitely generated (1.7, Dirichlet). Sections 1.8–1.10 study how primes factor in extensions, sharpened by Galois symmetry (Hilbert) and made wonderfully explicit in cyclotomic fields. Sections 1.11–1.12 develop the local tools (localization, orders) needed when the ring is not quite a Dedekind domain, and 1.13–1.14 reinterpret everything geometrically as schemes and draw the analogy with function fields.

Intuition

The whole chapter is one long answer to the question: "What replaces the prime factorization of $\mathbb{Z}$ when we leave $\mathbb{Z}$?" The short answer is: numbers may stop factoring uniquely, but ideals always do — and two finite invariants, the class number and the regulator, measure exactly how far the new world departs from the old.

1.1 The Gaussian Integers

We begin with the most famous example. Look at the primes that are sums of two squares:

$$2=1+1,\quad 5=1+4,\quad 13=4+9,\quad 17=1+16,\quad 29=4+25.$$

Apart from $2$, every prime in this list is congruent to $1 \bmod 4$. One direction is easy: a square is $0$ or $1 \bmod 4$, so a sum of two squares is never $3 \bmod 4$. The striking fact is the converse.

Theorem (1.1)

For an odd prime $p$, $$p=a^2+b^2\ (a,b\in\mathbb{Z}) \iff p\equiv 1\bmod 4.$$

The natural explanation lives in a larger ring. The Gaussian integers are $$\mathbb{Z}[i]=\{a+bi \mid a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\},\qquad i=\sqrt{-1}.$$ In this ring the equation $p=x^2+y^2$ becomes a factorization $p=(x+iy)(x-iy)$. So the real question is: how does a rational prime $p$ factor in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$?

Proposition (1.2)

The ring $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Euclidean with respect to $\alpha\mapsto|\alpha|^2$, and therefore is a unique factorization domain.

Intuition — the vocabulary in one breath

Four words are doing the work here. A Euclidean domain is a ring where you can divide with remainder, the remainder kept "small" by a size function (a norm) — exactly like dividing integers. Two elements are associates if they differ only by a unit (an invertible factor), so $3$ and $-3$ are associate in $\mathbb{Z}$. An element is irreducible if it cannot be split into two non-unit factors, and prime if whenever it divides a product it divides one of the factors. The logical chain is Euclidean $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD: division-with-remainder forces every ideal to be principal (a PID), and that in turn forces factorization into irreducibles to be unique (a UFD, a unique factorization domain). So once we know $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Euclidean, unique factorization comes for free.

The proof is a one-line picture: the Gaussian integers form a unit square lattice in the complex plane. Given $\alpha/\beta\in\mathbb{C}$, it lies in some unit square, and the nearest lattice point is at distance at most half the diagonal, $\tfrac12\sqrt2 < 1$. So there is a Gaussian integer $\gamma$ with $|\alpha/\beta-\gamma|<1$, which is exactly the Euclidean condition.

Intuition

This is the seed of the entire chapter: a divisibility question was answered by viewing integers as lattice points. Minkowski theory (§1.4–1.5) is nothing but this idea, scaled up to arbitrary number fields.

The norm $N(x+iy)=x^2+y^2=|z|^2$ is multiplicative: $N(\alpha\beta)=N(\alpha)N(\beta)$. This single fact does most of the work. Units are exactly the elements of norm 1.

Proposition (1.3)

The unit group of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the fourth roots of unity: $$\mathbb{Z}[i]^{*}=\{1,-1,i,-i\}.$$

Now to why (1.1) holds. It suffices to show a prime $p\equiv 1\bmod 4$ is not prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. The congruence $-1\equiv x^2\bmod p$ has a solution (take $x=(2n)!$; this follows from Wilson's theorem). Here is why: writing $p=4n+1$, Wilson's theorem says $(p-1)!\equiv-1\bmod p$. Now pair each factor $k$ in $(p-1)!$ with $p-k\equiv-k$; the upper half rewrites as the lower half times signs, collapsing $(p-1)!$ into $\pm\big((2n)!\big)^2$. So $\big((2n)!\big)^2\equiv-1\bmod p$, i.e. $x=(2n)!$ works. Then $p\mid x^2+1=(x+i)(x-i)$, yet $p\nmid(x\pm i)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: if it did, then $(x\pm i)/p$ would be a Gaussian integer, but its imaginary coordinate is $\pm 1/p$, not a whole number. So $p$ is reducible, $p=\alpha\beta$ with non-units, and taking norms gives $p=N(\alpha)=a^2+b^2$.

Theorem (1.4): the primes of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$

Up to associates, the prime elements $\pi$ of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ are:

  • $\pi=1+i$ (lying over $2$, since $2\sim(1+i)^2$ — the prime 2 ramifies);
  • $\pi=a+bi$ with $a^2+b^2=p,\ p\equiv 1\bmod 4$ (here $p$ splits into two conjugate primes);
  • $\pi=p$ with $p\equiv 3\bmod 4$ (such $p$ stays prime — it is inert).
Intuition — what split, inert, ramified mean here

These three words (the general definitions wait until §1.8) describe the three things a rational prime can do when we carry it into the bigger ring $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. A prime splits if it breaks into two different primes, like $5=(2+i)(2-i)$ — two genuinely distinct conjugate factors. It is inert if it refuses to break at all and stays prime, like $7$ (here $7\equiv3\bmod4$). It ramifies if it breaks but the factors coincide — a repeated prime — like $2=-i(1+i)^2$, where the single prime $1+i$ appears squared. Split = two distinct, inert = stays whole, ramified = a square. The whole of §1.8–1.10 is the general theory of exactly this trichotomy.

Notice the three words — ramified, split, inert — already appearing. The whole of §1.8–1.10 generalizes exactly this trichotomy.

Finally, the Gaussian integers play the role in $\mathbb{Q}(i)$ that $\mathbb{Z}$ plays in $\mathbb{Q}$. Crucially, there is a basis-free characterization (Proposition 1.5): $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ consists exactly of the $\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}(i)$ satisfying a monic equation $x^2+ax+b=0$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$. That intrinsic description is the launching point for the next section.

1.2 Integrality

An algebraic number field $K$ is a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$. We want, inside each such $K$, the right notion of "the integers." The basis-free definition from (1.5) generalizes verbatim.

Definition (2.1)

Let $A\subseteq B$ be a ring extension. An element $b\in B$ is integral over $A$ if it satisfies a monic equation $$x^{n}+a_{1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n}=0,\qquad a_i\in A.$$ An element of a number field $K$ is an algebraic integer if it is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$.

It is not obvious that the integral elements form a ring — that $b_1+b_2$ and $b_1b_2$ are integral if $b_1,b_2$ are. The clean fix is to recast integrality as a finiteness statement.

Proposition (2.2)

Elements $b_1,\dots,b_n\in B$ are integral over $A$ if and only if the ring $A[b_1,\dots,b_n]$ is finitely generated as an $A$-module.

(An $A$-module is just a "vector space over a ring": you can add its elements and scale them by elements of $A$. "Finitely generated" means every element is an $A$-combination of finitely many fixed generators — the algebraic version of "lives in a bounded box.")

The mechanism is a determinant trick (the "row–column expansion," Proposition 2.3). Suppose $b$ sends a finitely generated module into itself, with generators $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_r$. Then each product $b\,\omega_i$ is again a combination of the generators, so we can write $b\,\omega_i=\sum_j a_{ij}\,\omega_j$ with coefficients $a_{ij}\in A$. Collecting these into a matrix equation says that $\big(bE-(a_{ij})\big)$ kills the nonzero vector $(\omega_1,\dots,\omega_r)$. A matrix that annihilates a nonzero vector must have determinant zero, so $\det(bE-(a_{ij}))=0$ — and expanding that determinant gives precisely a monic polynomial equation in $b$ with coefficients in $A$. That is integrality. Since sums and products live inside one finitely generated module, integrality is closed under them.

Intuition

"Integral" means "lives in a finitely generated module." This is the algebraic shadow of "bounded," and it is why integrality behaves so well: finitely generated modules compose. Proposition 2.4 makes this precise — integrality is transitive.

The set $\bar A=\{b\in B \mid b \text{ integral over } A\}$ is a ring, the integral closure of $A$ in $B$. When $A$ is a domain with fraction field $K$ and $\bar A=A$ inside $K$, we call $A$ integrally closed. A useful baseline: every UFD is integrally closed.

The ring of integers

For a number field $K$, the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$ is the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$. It is the central object of the whole book.

To study $\mathcal{O}_K$ we import two tools from field theory: the trace and norm. For $x\in L$, multiplication-by-$x$ is a $K$-linear map $T_x:L\to L$, and we set $$\mathrm{Tr}_{L|K}(x)=\mathrm{Tr}(T_x),\qquad N_{L|K}(x)=\det(T_x).$$

For a separable extension these have a Galois-theoretic reading (2.6): summing/multiplying over the embeddings $\sigma:L\hookrightarrow\bar K$, $$\mathrm{Tr}_{L|K}(x)=\sum_\sigma \sigma x,\qquad N_{L|K}(x)=\prod_\sigma \sigma x.$$ Both are transitive in towers (2.7). And when $x$ is integral, so are all its conjugates, hence $\mathrm{Tr}(x),N(x)\in A$ — a fact we lean on constantly. Units are detected by the norm: $x\in\mathcal{O}_K^{*}\iff N_{K|\mathbb{Q}}(x)=\pm1$.

Example — trace and norm in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$

Let $x=a+b\sqrt2$ and use the basis $\{1,\sqrt2\}$. Multiplying by $x$ sends $1\mapsto a+b\sqrt2$ and $\sqrt2\mapsto 2b+a\sqrt2$ (since $\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2=2$). So in this basis the multiplication map is the matrix $$T_x=\begin{pmatrix}a & 2b\\ b & a\end{pmatrix}.$$ Reading off the trace and determinant, $$\mathrm{Tr}(x)=2a,\qquad N(x)=a^2-2b^2.$$ The Galois-theoretic reading gives the same answer instantly: there are two embeddings, the identity $\sqrt2\mapsto\sqrt2$ and the conjugation $\sqrt2\mapsto-\sqrt2$. Adding the two images of $x$ gives $(a+b\sqrt2)+(a-b\sqrt2)=2a$; multiplying them gives $(a+b\sqrt2)(a-b\sqrt2)=a^2-2b^2$. Matrix and embeddings agree, exactly as the two formulas above promise.

The trace also gives a bilinear form $(x,y)=\mathrm{Tr}_{L|K}(xy)$, which is nondegenerate when $L|K$ is separable (2.8). This nondegeneracy is what guarantees integral bases exist. The associated number is the discriminant of a basis: $$d(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)=\det\big(\sigma_i(\alpha_j)\big)^2=\det\big(\mathrm{Tr}_{L|K}(\alpha_i\alpha_j)\big).$$

Integral basis & field discriminant

An integral basis is an $A$-basis $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_n$ of $B$; it exists whenever $A$ is a PID (Proposition 2.10), so in particular $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $n=[K:\mathbb{Q}]$. Its discriminant $d_K$ is the discriminant of $K$, independent of the chosen basis (a base change has determinant $\pm1$).

Intuition — why the discriminant matters

The discriminant is one number that quietly does three jobs. First, it is a genuine-basis detector: $d(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ is nonzero exactly when the $\alpha_i$ are linearly independent — if they secretly collapse, the determinant vanishes. Second, it is a volume: in the next section we will see $\sqrt{|d_K|}$ is literally the volume of the fundamental box of the lattice $\mathcal{O}_K$, so it measures how "spread out" the ring of integers sits. Third, it is a ramification gauge: a prime $p$ ramifies if and only if it divides $d_K$ (§1.8). For $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt D)$ with $D$ squarefree this is concrete: $d_K=D$ if $D\equiv1\bmod4$, else $4D$ — so for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$ we get $d_K=-20$, whose prime divisors $2$ and $5$ are exactly the ramified primes.

Example — quadratic fields

Let $K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt D)$, $D$ squarefree. Then $$\mathcal{O}_K=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt D], & D\equiv 2,3\bmod 4,\\[2pt] \mathbb{Z}\!\left[\tfrac{1+\sqrt D}{2}\right], & D\equiv 1\bmod 4,\end{cases}$$ with discriminant $d_K=4D$ or $D$ respectively. The half-integer case (e.g. the golden ratio in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 5)$) is the classic trap: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt D]$ is the wrong ring.

Why is $\tfrac{1+\sqrt D}{2}$ an algebraic integer at all? Set $\alpha=\tfrac{1+\sqrt D}{2}$. Then $2\alpha-1=\sqrt D$, and squaring gives $(2\alpha-1)^2=D$, which rearranges to $$\alpha^2-\alpha+\tfrac{1-D}{4}=0.$$ This monic equation has integer coefficients exactly when $\tfrac{1-D}{4}\in\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. when $D\equiv1\bmod4$. That single divisibility is the whole reason the ring of integers grows in this case — and why it does not when $D\equiv2,3\bmod4$.

Finally, the discriminant tracks index: if $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}'$ are full $\mathcal{O}_K$-modules, then $d(\mathfrak{a})=(\mathfrak{a}':\mathfrak{a})^2\,d(\mathfrak{a}')$ (2.12). This index–discriminant relation will reappear as the volume formula in Minkowski theory.

1.3 Ideals

In $\mathcal{O}_K$ every nonzero non-unit factors into irreducibles (the norm decreases, so induction terminates). But — and this is the crisis that built the subject — factorization need not be unique.

The fundamental counterexample

In $\mathcal{O}_K=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $$21=3\cdot 7=(1+2\sqrt{-5})(1-2\sqrt{-5}).$$ All four factors are irreducible (a norm argument: $x^2+5y^2=\pm3$ has no integer solutions), and they are pairwise non-associate. Two genuinely different factorizations.

Kummer's escape was to imagine "ideal numbers" $\mathfrak{p}_1,\dots,\mathfrak{p}_4$ with $3=\mathfrak{p}_1\mathfrak{p}_2,\ 7=\mathfrak{p}_3\mathfrak{p}_4,\ 1+2\sqrt{-5}=\mathfrak{p}_1\mathfrak{p}_3,\ 1-2\sqrt{-5}=\mathfrak{p}_2\mathfrak{p}_4$. Then both factorizations of 21 are just two regroupings of $\mathfrak{p}_1\mathfrak{p}_2\mathfrak{p}_3\mathfrak{p}_4$. Dedekind made this rigorous by replacing "ideal numbers" with honest ideals, defining divisibility by reverse inclusion: $\mathfrak{a}\mid\mathfrak{b}\iff\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}$ ("to contain is to divide").

Theorem (3.1) + Definition (3.2)

$\mathcal{O}_K$ is noetherian (every ideal is generated by finitely many elements — no infinitely complicated ideals), integrally closed, and every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. A domain with these three properties is a Dedekind domain.

Why those three? Noetherian (every ideal finitely generated) comes from $\mathcal{O}_K$ being a finite $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Integrally closed is by construction. "Primes are maximal" holds because $\mathcal{O}_K/\mathfrak{p}$ is a finite domain, hence a field — in a finite integral domain, multiplying by a fixed nonzero element is an injective map of a finite set to itself, so it is also surjective, which means every nonzero element has an inverse. These three axioms are exactly what unique factorization of ideals requires.

Theorem (3.3): unique factorization of ideals

In a Dedekind domain, every ideal $\mathfrak{a}\neq(0),(1)$ factors $$\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{p}_1\cdots\mathfrak{p}_r$$ into nonzero primes, uniquely up to order.

The proof hinges on a clever auxiliary: for a prime $\mathfrak{p}$, define $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}=\{x\in K \mid x\mathfrak{p}\subseteq\mathcal{O}\}$. Lemma 3.5 shows multiplying by $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}$ strictly enlarges any ideal, which both produces the factorization (existence) and, via cancellation $\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}^{-1}=\mathcal{O}$, gives uniqueness. Concretely: for existence, repeatedly pull a prime factor out of $\mathfrak{a}$; each step enlarges the surrounding module, and because the ring is noetherian the enlargement cannot go on forever, so the process terminates in a finite product of primes. For uniqueness, if two prime products agree, multiply both sides by the relevant $\mathfrak{p}^{-1}$ to cancel a common prime, and induct on the number of factors.

Two operations make the ideal language smooth. Sum $\mathfrak{a}+\mathfrak{b}$ is the gcd; intersection $\mathfrak{a}\cap\mathfrak{b}$ is the lcm; and for coprime ideals ($\mathfrak{a}+\mathfrak{b}=\mathcal{O}$) we get the Chinese Remainder Theorem (3.6): $$\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{a}\;\cong\;\bigoplus_{i}\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{a}_i.$$

Fractional ideals & the class group

A fractional ideal is a finitely generated $\mathcal{O}$-submodule $\mathfrak{a}\neq 0$ of $K$. Under multiplication these form an abelian group $J_K$ — the ideal group — free on the prime ideals (3.8–3.9), with inverse $\mathfrak{a}^{-1}=\{x\in K\mid x\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathcal{O}\}$. The principal fractional ideals $P_K$ form a subgroup, and the quotient $$Cl_K=J_K/P_K$$ is the ideal class group.

Everything fits into one exact sequence that organizes the chapter: $$1\to\mathcal{O}^{*}\to K^{*}\to J_K\to Cl_K\to 1.$$ Read it left to right. The middle map $K^{*}\to J_K$ sends a nonzero number $a$ to the principal ideal $(a)$ it generates. Its kernel — the numbers that generate the trivial ideal $(1)=\mathcal{O}$ — is exactly the units $\mathcal{O}^{*}$. Its cokernel — all ideals modulo the principal ones — is exactly the class group $Cl_K$. So on the left, $\mathcal{O}^{*}$ measures the contraction (distinct numbers giving the same ideal); on the right, $Cl_K$ measures the expansion (ideals that are not principal). For instance in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the failure of unique factorization from the warning box is captured by $Cl_K\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: there is exactly one nontrivial ideal class, and the two factorizations of $21$ differ precisely by it. The next four sections prove both ends are tame (finite / finitely generated) for number fields.

1.4 Lattices

We now make precise the lattice picture that solved the Gaussian-integer problem.

Definition (4.1)

A lattice in an $n$-dimensional real vector space $V$ is a subgroup $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}v_1+\cdots+\mathbb{Z}v_m$ with $v_1,\dots,v_m$ linearly independent. It is complete (a full $\mathbb{Z}$-structure) if $m=n$. The fundamental mesh is $\Phi=\{\,\sum x_iv_i \mid 0\le x_i<1\,\}$.

The key structural fact is intrinsic — it doesn't mention a basis:

Proposition (4.2)

A subgroup $\Gamma\subseteq V$ is a lattice if and only if it is discrete.

A subtlety

Not every finitely generated subgroup is a lattice: $\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\sqrt2\subset\mathbb{R}$ is finitely generated but dense, hence not discrete. Discreteness is the real hypothesis.

On a Euclidean $V$ we have a volume. For a complete lattice the covolume is $$\mathrm{vol}(\Gamma)=\left|\det\big((v_i,v_j)\big)\right|^{1/2},$$ independent of basis. Completeness is equivalent (Lemma 4.3) to: some bounded set's translates cover $V$. With this we reach the engine of the next two sections.

Theorem (4.4): Minkowski's Lattice Point Theorem

Let $\Gamma$ be a complete lattice in Euclidean $V$ and $X$ a centrally symmetric convex set with $$\mathrm{vol}(X)>2^{n}\,\mathrm{vol}(\Gamma).$$ Then $X$ contains a nonzero lattice point.

Intuition — the pigeonhole behind it

Shrink $X$ by half. If the translates $\tfrac12X+\gamma$ were all disjoint, their total volume couldn't exceed one mesh — contradicting $\mathrm{vol}(\tfrac12X)>\mathrm{vol}(\Gamma)$. So two overlap; their difference is a nonzero lattice point in $X$ (using symmetry and convexity). "Enough volume forces a lattice point" — that one sentence proves the finiteness of the class number.

1.5 Minkowski Theory

To use lattices we must embed a number field $K$ into a real Euclidean space. The $n$ embeddings $\tau:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}$ give the canonical embedding $$j:K\longrightarrow K_{\mathbb{C}}=\textstyle\prod_\tau\mathbb{C},\qquad a\mapsto(\tau a)_\tau.$$ Equip $K_{\mathbb{C}}$ with the Hermitian product $\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_\tau x_\tau\overline{y_\tau}$.

Embeddings come in $r$ real ones $\rho$ and $s$ conjugate pairs $\sigma,\bar\sigma$, with $n=r+2s$. Taking the conjugation-invariant points gives the real Minkowski space $K_{\mathbb{R}}$, isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{r+2s}$ via $z_\rho,\ \mathrm{Re}(z_\sigma),\ \mathrm{Im}(z_\sigma)$ (Proposition 5.1). Under $j$, the field's trace becomes the coordinate-sum, so the geometry "knows" the arithmetic.

Proposition (5.2): ideals are lattices

For a nonzero ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $\mathcal{O}_K$, the image $j\mathfrak{a}$ is a complete lattice in $K_{\mathbb{R}}$, with covolume $$\mathrm{vol}(j\mathfrak{a})=\sqrt{|d_K|}\,\cdot(\mathcal{O}_K:\mathfrak{a}).$$

This is the bridge: the discriminant is literally the covolume of the lattice $\mathcal{O}_K$. Combining (5.2) with Minkowski (4.4) yields the workhorse:

Theorem (5.3)

In plain words: every nonzero ideal contains a nonzero element all of whose conjugates are simultaneously small — as small as we like in each direction, provided the chosen size bounds together leave enough room. Here is the precise statement. Given bounds $c_\tau>0$ ($c_\tau=c_{\bar\tau}$) with $\prod_\tau c_\tau>A\,(\mathcal{O}_K:\mathfrak{a})$, where $A=(\tfrac{2}{\pi})^{s}\sqrt{|d_K|}$, there exists $a\in\mathfrak{a}$, $a\neq0$, with $|\tau a|<c_\tau$ for all $\tau$.

Intuition — why this is just Minkowski in disguise

The bounds $c_\tau$ carve out a box in Minkowski space (a centrally symmetric convex region), and the product $\prod_\tau c_\tau$ controls its volume. The hypothesis "the product exceeds $A\,(\mathcal{O}_K:\mathfrak{a})$" is precisely "the box is bigger than $2^n$ times the covolume of the ideal lattice" — the exact trigger of Minkowski's lattice-point theorem (4.4). So the theorem is the bald statement "enough volume forces a lattice point," translated into the language of conjugates: a lattice point inside the box is an ideal element whose conjugates are all small. Everything downstream — the class number bound, Dirichlet's theorem — is this one idea, retuned.

In words: every ideal contains a nonzero element whose conjugates are all small. There is also a multiplicative version, passing through $z\mapsto\log|z|$ to turn the multiplicative group into an additive one — the setup that will give Dirichlet's theorem in §1.7. Optimizing (5.3) with the AM–GM inequality yields the Minkowski bound (Exercise 5.3): a nonzero $a\in\mathfrak{a}$ with $$|N_{K|\mathbb{Q}}(a)|\le M\,(\mathcal{O}_K:\mathfrak{a}),\quad M=\tfrac{n!}{n^{n}}\Big(\tfrac{4}{\pi}\Big)^{\!s}\sqrt{|d_K|}.$$

1.6 The Class Number

Now we cash in. To count ideals we use the absolute norm $\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a})=(\mathcal{O}_K:\mathfrak{a})$, a finite index. It is multiplicative (6.1), $\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{ab})=\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a})\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{b})$, and on principal ideals it recovers the field norm: $\mathfrak{N}((a))=|N_{K|\mathbb{Q}}(a)|$.

Lemma (6.2)

Every nonzero ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ contains a nonzero $a$ with $$|N_{K|\mathbb{Q}}(a)|\le\Big(\tfrac{2}{\pi}\Big)^{\!s}\sqrt{|d_K|}\;\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{a}).$$

Theorem (6.3): finiteness of the class number

The ideal class group $Cl_K$ is finite. Its order $h_K=(J_K:P_K)$ is the class number of $K$.

The proof is a two-step counting argument. First, there are only finitely many ideals of bounded norm (each prime $\mathfrak{p}$ sits over one rational prime $p$, and $\mathfrak{N}(\mathfrak{p})=p^f$). Second, lemma (6.2) shows every ideal class contains an integral representative with norm below the Minkowski bound $M$. Finitely many small-norm ideals, so finitely many classes.

Intuition

$h_K=1$ means $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a PID — classical unique factorization of elements survives. The class number measures precisely how badly that fails. The finiteness theorem says: passing from numbers to ideals does not throw us into an infinite wilderness.

Example — known class-number-one fields

The only imaginary quadratic fields $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ ($d<0$ squarefree) with $h_K=1$ are the nine Heegner numbers $$d=-1,-2,-3,-7,-11,-19,-43,-67,-163.$$ Real quadratic fields with $h_K=1$ are far more common, but it is still unknown whether there are infinitely many.

Pause — the Fermat connection

Historically the divisibility of the class number $h_p$ of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ (here $\zeta_p$ is a primitive $p$-th root of unity, $\zeta_p=e^{2\pi i/p}$; these cyclotomic fields are studied properly in §1.10) by $p$ was central to Fermat's Last Theorem. Just as $x^2+y^2$ led to $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, the sum $x^p+y^p$ factors via $\zeta_p$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$. Kummer called $p$ regular when $p\nmid h_p$, and proved FLT for regular primes — a triumph of class-group thinking, even though the final proof (Wiles) came from elsewhere.

1.7 Dirichlet's Unit Theorem

Now the left end of the exact sequence: the unit group $\mathcal{O}_K^{*}$. It contains the finite group $\mu(K)$ of roots of unity in $K$, but is usually infinite (think of $1+\sqrt2$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, all of whose powers are units). The genius is to linearize via logarithm.

Apply the map $\lambda=\ell\circ j$: $$\lambda(a)=\big(\log|\rho_1 a|,\dots,\log|\rho_r a|,\ \log|\sigma_1 a|^2,\dots,\log|\sigma_s a|^2\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{r+s}.$$ A unit has norm $\pm1$, so the coordinates of $\lambda(\varepsilon)$ sum to zero: the image lands in the trace-zero hyperplane $H\cong\mathbb{R}^{r+s-1}$.

Proposition (7.1)

The sequence $$1\to\mu(K)\to\mathcal{O}_K^{*}\xrightarrow{\ \lambda\ }\Gamma\to 0$$ is exact, where $\Gamma=\lambda(\mathcal{O}_K^{*})$. The kernel is exactly the roots of unity.

Theorem (7.3 + 7.4): Dirichlet's Unit Theorem

$\Gamma$ is a complete lattice in the $(r+s-1)$-dimensional space $H$. Hence $$\mathcal{O}_K^{*}\ \cong\ \mu(K)\times\mathbb{Z}^{\,r+s-1}.$$ There exist fundamental units $\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_t$ ($t=r+s-1$) so that every unit is uniquely $\zeta\,\varepsilon_1^{v_1}\cdots\varepsilon_t^{v_t}$.

The hard part is completeness of the lattice $\Gamma$ — that the rank is exactly $r+s-1$ and not less. This is shown with Minkowski's theorem again: one builds a bounded set in the norm-one surface whose unit-translates cover it (using Lemma 7.2: only finitely many elements of a given norm, up to units).

Example — the rank in pictures
  • Imaginary quadratic: $r=0,s=1$, so rank $r+s-1=0$ — units are just roots of unity (finite).
  • Real quadratic: $r=2,s=0$, rank $1$ — one fundamental unit, found by solving Pell's equation $x^2-dy^2=\pm4$. E.g. $1+\sqrt2$ for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$.
The regulator

The covolume of the unit lattice $\lambda(\mathcal{O}_K^{*})$ in $H$ equals $\sqrt{r+s}\,R$, where $R$ — the regulator — is the absolute value of any $t\times t$ minor of the matrix $(\lambda_i(\varepsilon_j))$. It is the unit-group analogue of the discriminant, and surfaces later in the class number formula.

1.8 Extensions of Dedekind Domains

Having understood $\mathcal{O}_K$ globally, we ask the local question: how does a prime $p$ factor in $\mathcal{O}_K$? We do this in general: $\mathfrak{o}$ a Dedekind domain, $L|K$ finite, $\mathcal{O}$ the integral closure of $\mathfrak{o}$ in $L$.

Proposition (8.1)

$\mathcal{O}$ is again a Dedekind domain. So a prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of $\mathfrak{o}$ factors uniquely $$\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}=\mathfrak{P}_1^{e_1}\cdots\mathfrak{P}_r^{e_r}.$$

The primes $\mathfrak{P}_i$ are exactly those lying over $\mathfrak{p}$ ($\mathfrak{P}_i\cap\mathfrak{o}=\mathfrak{p}$, written $\mathfrak{P}_i\mid\mathfrak{p}$). Two invariants describe each:

  • the ramification index $e_i$, the exponent;
  • the inertia (residue) degree $f_i=[\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{P}_i:\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}]$, the size of the residue field extension.
Proposition (8.2): the fundamental identity

If $L|K$ is separable, $$\sum_{i=1}^{r} e_i f_i = n=[L:K].$$

Intuition

Think of $n$ "slots" over each prime. They distribute among $r$ primes, each consuming $e_if_i$. The proof is the Chinese Remainder Theorem plus dimension counting of $\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}$ as a $k=\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}$-vector space.

Computing the factorization. If $\mathcal{O}=\mathfrak{o}[\theta]$ with minimal polynomial $p(X)$, then for $\mathfrak{p}$ not dividing the conductor (Dedekind's theorem, 8.3 — the conductor is a fixed ideal measuring how far $\mathfrak{o}[\theta]$ falls short of the full ring of integers, defined precisely in §1.12; the upshot is simply "for all but finitely many primes"): factor $\bar p(X)$ mod $\mathfrak{p}$ into irreducibles $\bar p(X)=\prod\bar p_i(X)^{e_i}$, and then $$\mathfrak{P}_i=\mathfrak{p}\mathcal{O}+p_i(\theta)\mathcal{O},\qquad f_i=\deg p_i.$$ The polynomial factors the same way the prime does. This is the single most useful computational tool in the chapter.

Example — recovering the $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ trichotomy by factoring $X^2+1$

Take $\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{Z}[i]=\mathbb{Z}[\theta]$ with $\theta=i$ and minimal polynomial $p(X)=X^2+1$ (here the conductor is trivial, so every prime is allowed). The recipe says: factor $X^2+1$ modulo $p$ and read off how $p$ splits.

  • $p\equiv1\bmod4$: then $-1$ is a square mod $p$, so $X^2+1\equiv(X-a)(X+a)$ with two distinct roots — two distinct prime factors, so $p$ splits (e.g. $X^2+1\equiv(X-2)(X+2)\bmod5$, matching $5=(2+i)(2-i)$).
  • $p\equiv3\bmod4$: then $-1$ is not a square mod $p$, so $X^2+1$ stays irreducible — one prime factor of degree 2, so $p$ is inert (e.g. $X^2+1$ mod $7$).
  • $p=2$: then $X^2+1\equiv(X+1)^2\bmod2$ — a repeated factor, so $2$ ramifies, matching $2\sim(1+i)^2$.

The split / inert / ramified trichotomy of Theorem 1.4 falls straight out of one polynomial factored mod $p$.

Vocabulary
  • Totally split: $r=n$ (all $e_i=f_i=1$).
  • Inert / nonsplit: $r=1$.
  • Ramified: some $e_i>1$ (or inseparable residue extension).

Only finitely many primes ramify (8.4): exactly the divisors of the discriminant $\mathfrak{d}$ of $\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{o}$.

Example — quadratic fields & the Legendre symbol

For $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt a)$ ($a$ squarefree, $p\nmid 2a$): factoring $X^2-a$ mod $p$ shows $$p \text{ totally split} \iff \left(\tfrac{a}{p}\right)=1.$$ So prime splitting is governed by quadratic residues (8.5). Pushing this through $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ reproves Gauss's quadratic reciprocity (8.6) — the splitting law is the reciprocity law. §1.10 reveals the real reason.

1.9 Hilbert's Ramification Theory

When $L|K$ is Galois with group $G$, the factorization acquires symmetry: $G$ permutes the primes over $\mathfrak{p}$.

Proposition (9.1)

$G$ acts transitively on the primes $\mathfrak{P}$ above $\mathfrak{p}$. Consequently all $e_i$ are equal and all $f_i$ are equal, and the fundamental identity collapses to $efr=n$.

The symmetry is encoded in subgroups. For a prime $\mathfrak{P}$:

Definition (9.2, 9.5)
  • Decomposition group $G_{\mathfrak{P}}=\{\sigma\in G \mid \sigma\mathfrak{P}=\mathfrak{P}\}$ — its index counts the primes: $r=(G:G_{\mathfrak{P}})$.
  • Inertia group $I_{\mathfrak{P}}$ — the kernel of $G_{\mathfrak{P}}\twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{Gal}(\kappa(\mathfrak{P})|\kappa(\mathfrak{p}))$.

(Here $\kappa(\mathfrak{P})$ denotes the residue field $\mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{P}$ — what is left after collapsing the prime $\mathfrak{P}$ to zero — and likewise $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})=\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}$. These are finite fields, and $\kappa(\mathfrak{P})|\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ is the residue field extension whose degree is $f$.)

Intuition — what these two groups are watching

Picture the Galois group $G$ shuffling the primes above $\mathfrak{p}$ among themselves. The decomposition group $G_{\mathfrak{P}}$ is the subgroup of symmetries that leave one chosen prime $\mathfrak{P}$ exactly where it is — the "stabilizer" of $\mathfrak{P}$. Among those, the inertia group $I_{\mathfrak{P}}$ is the still-smaller set that not only fixes $\mathfrak{P}$ but also acts trivially on the residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak{P})$ — symmetries so gentle they are invisible after reducing mod $\mathfrak{P}$. So $G_{\mathfrak{P}}$ sees the splitting and the residue-field action together, while quotienting out $I_{\mathfrak{P}}$ isolates just the residue-field part — which is exactly the next exact sequence.

The residue field extension is normal, and there is an exact sequence $$1\to I_{\mathfrak{P}}\to G_{\mathfrak{P}}\to \mathrm{Gal}(\kappa(\mathfrak{P})|\kappa(\mathfrak{p}))\to 1$$ (Proposition 9.4). When the residue extension is separable, the orders are exactly $\#I_{\mathfrak{P}}=e,\ (G_{\mathfrak{P}}:I_{\mathfrak{P}})=f$.

Intuition — a tower that reads off the numbers

The fixed fields give a tower $K\subseteq Z_{\mathfrak{P}}\subseteq T_{\mathfrak{P}}\subseteq L$ in which the three numbers separate cleanly:

  • $K$ to $Z_{\mathfrak{P}}$: the splitting happens (degree $r$);
  • $Z_{\mathfrak{P}}$ to $T_{\mathfrak{P}}$: the residue degree $f$ grows;
  • $T_{\mathfrak{P}}$ to $L$: pure ramification (degree $e$).

When $\mathfrak{p}$ is unramified, $I_{\mathfrak{P}}=1$ and $G_{\mathfrak{P}}$ is canonically the Galois group of the residue extension — a cyclic group with a distinguished generator, the Frobenius $\varphi_{\mathfrak{P}}$ (Exercise 9.2), characterized by $\varphi(\alpha)\equiv\alpha^{q}\bmod\mathfrak{P}$. This element is the heart of class field theory in later chapters. Hilbert theory itself is the global shadow of the valuation-theoretic ramification of Chapter II.

1.10 Cyclotomic Fields

Time for a concrete masterpiece. Let $\zeta$ be a primitive $n$-th root of unity and $K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$ the cyclotomic field. Everything we have built becomes explicit here.

Proposition (10.2): the ring of integers

For every $n$, $$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)}=\mathbb{Z}[\zeta],$$ with integral basis $1,\zeta,\dots,\zeta^{\varphi(n)-1}$ ($\varphi$ = Euler's function).

The prime-power case rests on a lovely fact (Lemma 10.1): for $n=\ell^v$, the element $\lambda=1-\zeta$ generates the unique prime above $\ell$, and $\ell=(\lambda)^{\varphi(\ell^v)}$ — totally ramified. The general case glues prime powers using the relatively-prime-discriminants trick (2.11).

Proposition (10.3): the decomposition law

Write $n=\prod p^{v_p}$. For a prime $p$, let $f_p$ be the smallest positive integer with $p^{f_p}\equiv 1 \bmod n/p^{v_p}$. Then $$p=(\mathfrak{p}_1\cdots\mathfrak{p}_r)^{\varphi(p^{v_p})},$$ with the $\mathfrak{p}_i$ distinct of common degree $f_p$.

Intuition

The splitting of $p$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ is dictated by the order of $p$ modulo $n$. Beautifully clean (Corollary 10.4): $p$ ramifies $\iff p\mid n$, and $p$ totally split $\iff p\equiv 1\bmod n$. The abstract $e,f,r$ become pure congruence conditions.

Example — reciprocity, finally explained

Since $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\ell^{*}})\subset\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_\ell)$ (with $\ell^{*}=(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}\ell$), comparing the two splitting laws (Proposition 10.5) gives quadratic reciprocity conceptually: $p$ splits in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\ell^{*}})\iff p$ splits into an even number of primes in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_\ell)$. The contrived Gauss-sum computation of §1.8 is replaced by the structure of $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_\ell)|\mathbb{Q})$. This is the prototype of Artin reciprocity.

1.11 Localization

To analyze one prime at a time, we localize: form fractions with denominators outside a prime. For a multiplicative set $S\subseteq A\setminus\{0\}$, $$AS^{-1}=\Big\{\tfrac{a}{s}\;\Big|\;a\in A,\ s\in S\Big\}.$$ With $S=A\setminus\mathfrak{p}$ we get the localization $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.

Proposition (11.1) + Corollary (11.2)

Primes of $AS^{-1}$ correspond bijectively to primes of $A$ disjoint from $S$. In particular $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is a local ring: it has a unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_{\mathfrak{p}}=\mathfrak{p}A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.

Intuition

Localization "forgets everything not about $\mathfrak{p}$." All other primes vanish, leaving a clean one-prime ring where structure is transparent. Many global statements reduce to checking them locally at every prime.

Definition (11.3): discrete valuation ring

A DVR is a PID with a unique nonzero maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}=(\pi)$. Every nonzero element of its fraction field is uniquely $\varepsilon\pi^{n}$, defining a valuation $v:K^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}$ with $v(ab)=v(a)+v(b),\ v(a+b)\ge\min\{v(a),v(b)\}$.

Propositions (11.4–11.5): the local–global picture

A localization of a Dedekind domain is Dedekind, and a noetherian domain is Dedekind iff every localization $\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is a DVR. So Dedekind = "locally a DVR everywhere."

These local valuations $v_{\mathfrak{p}}$ recover the global factorization: $v_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)$ is exactly the exponent of $\mathfrak{p}$ in $(x)$. The model case is $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, giving the $p$-adic valuation $v_p$ — the doorway to Chapter II. Proposition 11.6 packages units and class groups of a localized ring into an exact sequence, which for $\mathcal{O}_K$ yields the $S$-units and finite $S$-class group: $K^{S}\cong\mu(K)\times\mathbb{Z}^{\#S+r+s-1}$ (Corollary 11.7), a direct generalization of Dirichlet.

1.12 Orders

Not every natural ring is the full $\mathcal{O}_K$. Rings like $\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\sqrt5\subset\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$ are integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ but not integrally closed. These are orders.

Definition (12.1)

An order in $K$ is a subring $\mathfrak{o}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_K$ containing a full integral basis (so $\mathfrak{o}$ has rank $n$). $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the maximal order.

An order is a one-dimensional noetherian domain (12.2) — primes are maximal, but it may fail to be integrally closed. The right framework is exactly such rings. Two repairs are needed because unique factorization of ideals breaks.

Picard group

Restrict to invertible fractional ideals ($\mathfrak{ab}=\mathfrak{o}$) — equivalently, those that are locally principal (12.4). These form a group, and $$\mathrm{Pic}(\mathfrak{o})=J(\mathfrak{o})/P(\mathfrak{o}).$$ For a Dedekind domain, $\mathrm{Pic}(\mathfrak{o})=Cl_K$.

Comparing an order $\mathfrak{o}$ with its normalization $\bar{\mathfrak{o}}$ (a Dedekind domain — the Krull–Akizuki theorem 12.8 guarantees this even non-separably) gives a clean exact sequence (12.9, 12.11) controlled by the conductor $\mathfrak{f}=\{a\in\bar{\mathfrak{o}} \mid a\bar{\mathfrak{o}}\subseteq\mathfrak{o}\}$. The non-regular ("singular") primes are exactly the divisors of $\mathfrak{f}$.

Theorem (12.12): units & class number of an order

$\mathcal{O}_K^{*}/\mathfrak{o}^{*}$ and $\mathrm{Pic}(\mathfrak{o})$ are finite, with $$\#\mathrm{Pic}(\mathfrak{o})=\frac{h_K\cdot\#(\bar{\mathfrak{o}}/\mathfrak{f})^{*}}{(\mathcal{O}_K^{*}:\mathfrak{o}^{*})\cdot\#(\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{f})^{*}}.$$ In particular $\mathrm{rank}(\mathfrak{o}^{*})=r+s-1$ — Dirichlet's theorem survives for orders.

An even finer invariant keeps all primes: the Chow group $CH^{1}(\mathfrak{o})=\mathrm{Div}(\mathfrak{o})/\mathcal{P}(\mathfrak{o})$, built from the free group on all maximal ideals using length-based $\mathrm{ord}_{\mathfrak{p}}$ functions. For a Dedekind domain $\mathrm{Pic}\cong CH^{1}$ (12.14) — the divisor language and the ideal language coincide, foreshadowing the geometric viewpoint next.

1.13 One-dimensional Schemes

Heads-up — a change of language

The next two sections are a geometric reinterpretation of everything already proved: nothing new is being assumed about number fields, we are only re-describing rings as spaces. If the words scheme, sheaf, and generic point are unfamiliar, it is completely safe to skim — the arithmetic in §1.1–1.12 stands on its own. Read this as a preview of why later chapters speak geometrically.

The same algebra can be read geometrically. The key move: view ring elements as functions on a space of prime ideals.

The spectrum

For a ring $\mathfrak{o}$, the spectrum $X=\mathrm{Spec}(\mathfrak{o})$ is the set of prime ideals, topologized by declaring $V(\mathfrak{a})=\{\mathfrak{p}\supseteq\mathfrak{a}\}$ closed (the Zariski topology). An element $f$ has a "value" $f(\mathfrak{p})=f\bmod\mathfrak{p}$ in the residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$.

Example — $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ as a line

Picture $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})$ as a curve with one closed point per prime $2,3,5,7,\dots$ plus a generic point $(0)$ whose closure is everything (a "generic point" is one that is dense — it is near every other point at once, the zero ideal playing the role of "the whole curve smeared out"). An integer $a$ is a "function" whose value at $(p)$ is $a\bmod p\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, and at the generic point is $a\in\mathbb{Q}$. Every prime field appears once.

Enriching $X$ with the structure sheaf $\mathcal{O}_X$ (regular functions, with stalk $\mathcal{O}_{X,\mathfrak{p}}=\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}$) gives an affine scheme $(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$ (13.3). Three of these words deserve a gloss. A regular function on an open piece of $X$ is just a ratio of ring elements with no pole there — the algebraic version of a smooth function. A sheaf is the bookkeeping device that records which functions are defined on which open sets and how they glue. And the stalk at a prime $\mathfrak{p}$ is the collection of functions defined "right near $\mathfrak{p}$" — which is exactly the localization $\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}}$ from §1.11, so the geometry literally hands us back the local rings we already built. Ring maps induce scheme morphisms, contravariantly.

Intuition — the dictionary
  • DVR = a point with an infinitesimal neighbourhood (closed point + generic point).
  • Dedekind domain = a smooth curve; each $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathfrak{o}_{\mathfrak{p}})$ is an infinitesimal neighbourhood.
  • Order / non-maximal ring = a curve with singularities exactly at the non-regular primes (e.g. the cusp $y^2=x^3$ at the origin).
  • Normalization = resolving those singularities into a smooth curve.

An extension $\mathcal{O}|\mathfrak{o}$ becomes a ramified covering of curves $\mathrm{Spec}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathrm{Spec}(\mathfrak{o})$: generically $n$ sheets, with branch points exactly where primes ramify. For Galois $L|K$, the Galois group is the group of deck transformations, $G(L|K)=\mathrm{Aut}_Y(X)$. Taking the maximal unramified extension gives a "universal cover," and one defines the fundamental group $\pi_1(Y)=\mathrm{Gal}(\tilde K|K)$ — Galois theory reincarnated as topology, the seed of étale cohomology.

1.14 Function Fields

The geometric picture is confirmed by a striking analogy: replace $\mathbb{Z}$ by the polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}_p[t]$.

The dictionary
  • $\mathbb{Z}$ with fraction field $\mathbb{Q}$ $\leftrightarrow$ $\mathbb{F}_p[t]$ with fraction field $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$.
  • Prime numbers $\leftrightarrow$ monic irreducible polynomials $p(t)$, with residue fields $\mathbb{F}_{p^{d}}$.
  • Number fields $\leftrightarrow$ function fields: finite extensions $K|\mathbb{F}_p(t)$.
The crucial difference — hidden primes

The choice of $t$ is arbitrary; $t'=1/t$ gives a different ring $\mathbb{F}_p[1/t]$ with different primes. So $\mathbb{F}_p[t]$ misses some valuations — notably the degree valuation at infinity $v_\infty(f/g)=\deg g-\deg f$. The fix is to use all normalized valuations.

Working with all valuations, one forms the divisor group and divisor class group $$Cl(K)=\mathrm{Div}(K)/\mathcal{P}(K).$$ Unlike the number-field case this is infinite, but the degree map $\deg:Cl(K)\to\mathbb{Z}$ has finite kernel $Cl^{0}(K)$ — the genuine analogue of finiteness of the class number.

Intuition — the missing point at infinity

The proper home for a function field is a scheme (not just an affine one), which "sees all primes at once." For $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ this is the projective line $\mathbb{P}^1_{\mathbb{F}_p}$, glued from $\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{F}_p[u]$ and $\mathrm{Spec}\,\mathbb{F}_p[v]$ via $u\mapsto 1/v$. Adding the point at infinity completes the affine line into a compact curve — which is exactly why function fields behave so geometrically, and why they serve as a testing ground for conjectures about number fields.

Summary

This chapter assembled the permanent vocabulary of the subject. The throughline: leave $\mathbb{Z}$, lose unique factorization of numbers, but regain unique factorization of ideals — then measure the discrepancy geometrically.

  • Algebraic integers & $\mathcal{O}_K$ (1.1–1.2): integrality = finite-module condition; trace, norm, discriminant, integral bases.
  • Dedekind domains (1.3): noetherian + integrally closed + primes maximal $\Rightarrow$ unique ideal factorization; the class group $Cl_K$.
  • Geometry of numbers (1.4–1.7): lattices + Minkowski's theorem $\Rightarrow$ finiteness of $h_K$ and Dirichlet's unit theorem (rank $r+s-1$, regulator $R$).
  • Splitting of primes (1.8–1.10): $\sum e_if_i=n$; Dedekind's factorization-via-polynomial; Hilbert's $G_{\mathfrak{P}}, I_{\mathfrak{P}}$ and Frobenius; cyclotomic fields making it all explicit and explaining reciprocity.
  • Local & singular tools (1.11–1.12): localization, DVRs, the local–global criterion; orders, Picard and Chow groups.
  • Geometric language (1.13–1.14): $\mathrm{Spec}$, schemes, ramified coverings, and the number-field / function-field analogy.
Looking ahead
