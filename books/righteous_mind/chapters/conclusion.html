Conclusion

This book has been a long journey through human nature — through the evolution of our moral emotions, the architecture of our political identities, and the deep reasons why people who share a country can feel like strangers to one another. Moral psychology, Haidt argues, turns out to be the master key. It unlocks not only why we fight about politics and religion, but also how civilizations cohered in the first place, why humans became so extraordinarily cooperative compared with other primates, and why that cooperation so reliably curdles into tribalism. The tour covered a lot of ground, and Haidt closes by pausing at the three most important landmarks.

Three Principles Revisited

The first principle — intuitions come first, strategic reasoning second — upends the story most of us tell about our own minds. We like to think that we reason our way to moral conclusions, weighing evidence and following arguments wherever they lead. The social intuitionist model says almost the opposite: the emotional, intuitive parts of the mind reach a verdict almost instantly, and the deliberate reasoning that follows is largely devoted to constructing a justification for what the gut already decided. Hume saw this clearly centuries ago, and Haidt's research confirmed it experimentally. The practical implication is disarming: if you find it effortless to refute someone else's argument, that says little about whether their underlying moral concern is mistaken. The rider can always generate counterarguments; the elephant moved first. Keeping that image in mind — yourself and everyone around you as a small rider atop a large, opinion-having elephant — is an antidote to the smug certainty that tends to poison political conversation.

The second principle — there is more to morality than harm and fairness — pushes back against the WEIRD tendency to treat ethics as a two-foundation system. WEIRD cultures (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) have narrowed the moral domain to questions of who gets hurt and who gets treated unjustly. Moral Foundations Theory maps out six taste receptors of the moral palate: Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, Sanctity, and Liberty. Every human society uses some combination of these, though the recipes differ. Richard Shweder's cross-cultural fieldwork, Durkheim's sociology of religion, and Haidt's own time in India all point in the same direction: the binding foundations — loyalty to the group, deference to legitimate authority, and a sense of the sacred — are not moral mistakes waiting to be corrected by Enlightenment reason. They are functional systems that help communities cohere. The lesson Haidt wants readers to carry is a suspicion of moral monists: anyone who insists that one foundation, or one configuration of foundations, should govern every society in every era is doing a kind of moral fundamentalism, even when the creed sounds progressive.

Isaiah Berlin spent his career wrestling with the fact of moral diversity, and his conclusion offers a useful compass. He rejected pure relativism — the view that every moral preference is as valid as every other, the way coffee-with-milk is as valid as coffee-without. But he also rejected the idea that reason would eventually converge on a single correct moral code. What he endorsed was pluralism: the recognition that there are multiple genuine human values, perhaps a finite but sizable number of them, and that a person sincerely pursuing one value I do not personally prioritize is still someone I can understand. That possibility of understanding — the imaginative act of seeing why someone else's moral world makes sense from inside it — is the foundation of both good political philosophy and ordinary civil life.

The third principle — morality binds and blinds — rests on the claim that we are products of multilevel selection. Natural selection worked not only on individual survival and reproduction but also, at key moments in prehistory, on the competition between groups. The result is a creature Durkheim called Homo duplex: we are simultaneously selfish individuals and deeply groupish social animals. Haidt estimates the ratio at roughly ninety percent chimp and ten percent bee — mostly self-interested, but with a genuine capacity for hive-minded selflessness when the right conditions activate it. Religion, he argues, was the primary cultural technology that activated that capacity at scale, building the moral communities large enough to sustain agriculture, cities, and eventually civilization. The evolutionary lens also explains political orientation: people differ in the degree to which the binding foundations register as morally compelling, and that variation maps with striking consistency onto left-right political identity.

The image Haidt offers as a parting gift from this third section is the hive switch — a figurative toggle, dormant most of the time, that can briefly dissolve the boundary between self and group. Fifty years of pop-psychology messaging has told us that human beings are fundamentally selfish, and reality television has been happy to furnish supporting evidence. But that picture is incomplete. Most people, under the right circumstances — a shared crisis, a transcendent ritual, a moment of genuine collective purpose — can step outside their private interests entirely and feel themselves part of something larger. That experience is not a malfunction or a manipulation; it is one of the most reliably reported sources of meaning in human life. The capacity for self-transcendence is real, even if it is not the default state.

Figure 13.1 illustrates why political antagonists believe they are divided by good versus evil, when the actual structure of their conflict is far more symmetrical and far less flattering to either side. Both camps inhabit coherent moral matrices; both are convinced that the other's positions are obviously wrong or even wicked; and both reach those convictions through the same basic process of gut-led, reason-supported moral judgment. The Manichaean framing — we are the forces of light, they are the forces of darkness — is not a description of the political landscape. It is a predictable output of minds built for group loyalty and in-group justification.

What to Do With This

Understanding the mechanics of moral psychology does not automatically make anyone more virtuous or more tolerant. Knowledge of the elephant does not give the rider control over the elephant. But it does offer something: it makes the behavior of other riders legible. When you know that the person across the political divide is not operating from cynicism or stupidity but from a moral intuition as vivid and self-evident to them as your own is to you — shaped by the same evolutionary pressures, the same basic set of moral taste receptors, just weighted differently — the conversation becomes at least potentially possible.

Haidt's practical suggestion for that conversation is modest and sequential. When you find yourself next to someone from a different moral matrix, resist the temptation to open with a policy disagreement. Instead, look first for genuine common ground — not manufactured agreement, but actual shared concerns. Establish a degree of trust. When moral topics do arise, approach them with curiosity rather than prosecution. The goal is not to win the argument; the rider's arguments rarely move the other person's elephant anyway. The goal is to make contact with the other person's moral world so that understanding, if not agreement, becomes possible.

Closing insight: Human nature is simultaneously the source of the problem and the resource for addressing it. The same tribal psychology that makes us righteous and blind to our own biases also makes us capable of genuine self-transcendence, of moments when group solidarity feels more real than individual interest. The answer to moral polarization is not to pretend the elephant doesn't exist, or to demand that people reason their way out of their gut convictions. It is to recognize that every moral conviction — yours included — feels self-evidently true from the inside, and that intellectual humility about that fact is both the most honest and the most politically productive stance available to us.
