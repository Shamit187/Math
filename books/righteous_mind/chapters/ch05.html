Part II · There's More to Morality than Harm and Fairness

Beyond WEIRD Morality

For part of his dissertation research, Haidt spent hours outside a West Philadelphia McDonald's trying to recruit working-class adults to participate in his study. When someone agreed, they would settle into the outdoor seating and Haidt would walk them through his collection of moral dilemmas — the family that cooked and ate its own dog, the woman who cut up her country's flag to use as a cleaning rag, the man who did something intimate with a chicken carcass. The interviews often produced laughter, but what genuinely surprised Haidt was the frustration his follow-up question provoked. Whenever a subject judged something as wrong, he would ask them to explain why. Among college students at Penn, that question flowed naturally into reasoned justifications. But a few blocks west, among working-class respondents, the same question drew long silences and incredulous stares — expressions that seemed to say: you seriously need me to explain this? Haidt came to realize that he was the strange one in the room. He had been formed by a very particular moral world — the University of Pennsylvania — and that world had trained him to think about morality in a way that was, by global standards, deeply unusual.

Penn students stood out among all twelve groups in the study. Their moral compass was almost entirely set by what John Stuart Mill called the harm principle — the idea that the only legitimate reason to restrict someone's behavior is to prevent harm to others. This led Penn students to shrug off disgust and declare acts permissible as long as nobody was being hurt, even when those acts made them visibly uncomfortable. They were the only group whose majority could bring themselves to accept the chicken story on the grounds that it was a private act between consenting parties. This peculiarity turns out to be part of a much larger pattern. In a landmark 2010 paper, psychologists Joe Henrich, Steve Heine, and Ara Norenzayan pointed out that nearly all psychological research is conducted on people from societies that are Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic — giving them the acronym WEIRD. The startling finding was that WEIRD people are statistical outliers on measure after measure. They are not a useful baseline for understanding human nature; they are closer to an anomaly. Even within the West, Americans score as more extreme than Europeans, and within America, the educated upper-middle class sits at the furthest edge of the distribution. One way to summarize what makes WEIRD people unusual is this: the WEIRDer you are, the more you see the world as populated by discrete, independent objects rather than by relationships and contexts. Americans tend to describe themselves in terms of internal traits — "I am outgoing," "I love jazz" — whereas East Asians are more likely to define themselves through their connections to others — "I am a husband," "I work at Fujitsu."

This difference in self-conception runs all the way down to basic visual perception. In a classic experimental task, participants are shown a square frame containing a line, then asked to reproduce that line in a new frame of a different size. The task can be done in two ways: you can try to match the line's absolute length, ignoring the frame, or you can try to match its length relative to the new frame. Westerners, and Americans especially, tend to excel at the absolute version, because they encoded the line as a standalone object in the first place. East Asians tend to outperform Westerners on the relative version, because they automatically encoded the relationship between the line and its frame. This reflects a broader contrast in thinking style. WEIRD people tend toward analytic cognition — they pull objects out of their context, sort them into categories, and reason about the categories. Most of the rest of humanity thinks more holistically, attending to the whole situation and the web of relations within it. It follows naturally that the great moral philosophers of the Western tradition — Kant, Mill, Kohlberg — produced systems that are individualistic, rule-based, and meant to apply universally across all persons. That kind of morality is exactly what you need if you take the individual as your fundamental unit. But when thinkers embedded in non-WEIRD cultures write about ethics, the result looks more like Confucius: a collection of context-sensitive observations about how people in different roles and relationships should treat one another. There is no single master rule because the moral world is not made of interchangeable units.

If you start with a picture of the world composed of separate individuals, you will naturally build a morality centered on protecting those individuals — their rights, their freedom from harm, their fair treatment. But if you start with a picture of the world composed of families, lineages, communities, and institutions, your moral concerns will be fundamentally different. You will care about whether people are honoring their obligations, respecting the appropriate hierarchies, and holding up their end of the social structures that make collective life possible. Haidt calls this a sociocentric morality, and it is the dominant mode in most human societies across history. Part II of the book is built around the claim that this richer moral universe is not just a curiosity — it is the normal condition of human moral life, and WEIRD morality is the outlier. The task Haidt sets himself is descriptive before it is evaluative: to map what morality actually looks like across cultures before passing judgment on which version is correct. Moral intuitions are like an elephant — they move first, and reasons follow. If we let our immediate partisan reactions decide which moral frameworks deserve attention, we will never understand the ones that feel foreign to us.

What "WEIRD" really means. The acronym WEIRD — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic — is not just a label for demography. It marks a cognitive style: analytic rather than holistic, individualist rather than relational, harm-focused rather than duty-focused. The finding that upends decades of moral psychology research is that the populations psychologists have studied almost exclusively are the most statistically extreme humans on earth. Generalizing from WEIRD samples to "human nature" is a bit like generalizing from marathon runners to human locomotion.

Three Ethics Are More Descriptive Than One

The University of Chicago has long embraced its reputation as a place where intellectual seriousness crowds out social life — proudly ranked by Playboy as the worst party school in America, its students wearing T-shirts that read "Where Fun Goes to Die." It was into this atmosphere that Haidt arrived in the fall of 1992, fresh off his Penn dissertation. He had won a fellowship to spend two years working with Richard Shweder, the founding figure of cultural psychology and one of the most original moral thinkers of his generation. On his very first night in Chicago, Haidt sat in a bar and watched a man slam his hands on a neighboring table and shout about Marx. He had clearly found his tribe.

Cultural psychology, as Shweder and his colleagues practiced it, rests on a simple but radical premise: culture and mind are not separate things that occasionally influence each other. They co-create each other. You cannot study cognition in isolation from the cultural context that gives it content and shape, any more than you can understand a word without knowing the language it belongs to. And you cannot understand cultural practices in isolation from the mental architecture that makes those practices feel natural and compelling. Shweder had spent years studying morality in the Indian state of Orissa, collecting hundreds of interview transcripts and searching them for pattern. What emerged from that analysis was a framework of three distinct moral clusters, which he called the ethics of autonomy, community, and divinity. Each one rests on a different foundational picture of what a human being is. The ethic of autonomy treats persons primarily as sovereign agents — beings with their own desires, goals, and preferences who deserve space to pursue them without interference. Moral thinking from within this framework generates concepts like rights, consent, and fairness, and it leads naturally to moral systems like Mill's utilitarianism or Kant's deontology. This is the home morality of WEIRD cultures, and in purely secular Western societies it tends to be the only morality that is taken seriously in public debate.

The ethic of community rests on a very different premise: that people are, first and foremost, members of collectives — families, clans, villages, nations, religious traditions. These groups are not mere conveniences that individuals join for personal benefit; they are real entities with histories, purposes, and vulnerabilities of their own. From this view, the highest moral virtues are not rights or autonomy but duty, loyalty, and respect for the roles and hierarchies through which collective life is organized. Many societies that operate within this ethic look at Western individualism and see something troubling — a culture of selfishness that erodes the institutions everyone depends on. The ethic of divinity begins from the premise that human beings carry within them something sacred — a soul, a divine spark, a fundamental dignity that transcends biology. Bodies, behaviors, and social practices can either honor or degrade that sacred quality. From this perspective, morality is not just about preventing harm; it is about maintaining a kind of cosmic order, keeping pure things pure and preventing the sacred from being profaned. Cultures shaped by this ethic generate concepts of sanctity and pollution, elevation and degradation, that have no direct counterpart in autonomy-based thinking. When Haidt encountered Shweder's framework in 1991, while still writing his dissertation, he suddenly recognized what his best stories had been doing. The scenarios that generated the most moral emotion without producing any identifiable victim — the flag used as a mop, the private acts that disgusted people for no articulable reason — were violations of community and divinity ethics, not autonomy ethics. Shweder's three-part scheme unlocked the data. Penn students spoke almost exclusively in the language of autonomy, while working-class respondents and Brazilian participants drew freely on all three.

Having spent a year in Chicago immersed in cultural psychology, Haidt applied for a Fulbright fellowship to go to India. He wanted firsthand exposure to the ethic of divinity, which had appeared least frequently in his dissertation data. With the help of Shweder's network of contacts in Bhubaneswar, the capital of Orissa, he assembled a research proposal, secured funding, and flew to India in September 1993.

Shweder's three ethics at a glance.

Autonomy — The individual is the basic moral unit. Harm, rights, and fairness are the central concerns. "Does this hurt anyone?"

Community — Groups, roles, and institutions are the basic moral units. Duty, loyalty, and honor are the central concerns. "Is this person living up to their obligations?"

Divinity — The soul or sacred order is the basic moral unit. Purity, sanctity, and spiritual elevation are the central concerns. "Does this degrade or honor what is sacred?"

WEIRD cultures operate almost entirely within the first ethic. Most human societies across history have operated within all three simultaneously.

How I Became A Pluralist

Haidt arrived in Bhubaneswar in a position of considerable material comfort — a furnished apartment with a cook, a rented car with a driver, and an office arranged by a local professor who had long known Shweder. Within a week he had assembled a student research team and was ready to begin. The formal experiment plan was to run studies on moral judgment, with a focus on purity and divinity violations. But the experiments, he would later admit, taught him relatively little compared to what he absorbed simply by moving through the city, making mistakes, noticing his own confusion, and asking his Indian hosts to explain things he didn't understand. He had arrived carrying two identities that did not fit comfortably together: a 29-year-old liberal atheist with firm moral convictions, and an aspiring anthropologist who had read deeply in the tradition of respectful cross-cultural inquiry.

The early weeks were jarring. He shared meals with men while their wives served in silence and never addressed him directly. He was gently instructed to stop thanking his household servants, because gratitude in that context felt inappropriate — it implied a kind of equality that disrupted the expected social order. He watched people draw water from visibly polluted rivers for cooking and bathing, treating those same waters as sacred. Everything around him was organized by hierarchies of gender, caste, age, and religious status that his upbringing had taught him to see as oppressive. And yet he had committed himself to understanding rather than condemning. What dissolved his initial dissonance was not intellectual effort but something simpler: he genuinely liked the people around him. They were generous, warm, and patient with his stumbling attempts to understand their world. Gratitude, Haidt noticed, has a way of opening the mind. When you feel warmly toward someone, your intuitive elephant naturally leans toward their perspective, and your reasoning rider follows. Rather than defaulting to a story about oppressors and victims, he began to perceive an alternative moral architecture — one in which the family, not the individual, was the fundamental unit of moral concern, and in which roles, duties, and interdependence were the scaffolding of a coherent social world. Equality and personal freedom were not sacred here; honoring elders, protecting subordinates, and fulfilling one's place in the larger web of relationships were. He had understood the ethic of community as an abstraction before arriving. Now, for the first time, he felt it from the inside.

The contrast became vivid the moment he boarded his flight home. As he settled into his seat, he heard a loud American voice across the aisle insisting, at some length, on his right to the overhead compartment above his own seat. Haidt cringed in a way he would not have three months earlier. The ethic of divinity underwent the same arc. Haidt had spent his graduate school years researching the emotion of disgust alongside Paul Rozin and Clark McCauley. Their working theory was that disgust, which originated as a biological alarm against contamination, had been co-opted by moral psychology to police something else entirely: a person's position on an implicit vertical scale that runs from the divine or pure at the top down through ordinary humanity to the degraded or animal at the bottom. A person who commits a mundane crime — say, robbing a store — does something bad, and we want them punished. But someone who betrays their own parents, or who treats children as objects, seems to fall off that scale entirely. We don't just want them punished; we feel revulsed, as though encountering something outside the human category. Haidt and his colleagues believed this vertical moral dimension to be nearly universal, though it is articulated more explicitly in some cultures than others. Hindu reincarnation doctrine could not make it more literal: the virtue of your current life determines whether your soul rises or falls in the next.

India made the ethic of divinity tangible in everyday practice. The constant presence of animals, outdoor defecation, and refuse in Bhubaneswar's streets made the Indian convention of removing shoes before entering a home feel not like a cultural quirk but like a natural and sensible boundary between profane and clean space. Visiting temples, he became attuned to their graded topography: the street was least pure, the outer courtyard a step above, the inner antechamber purer still, and the innermost sanctum accessible only to Brahmin priests who had observed strict rules of personal purity. Private homes had their own spatial hierarchy, and Haidt had to remember never to enter the kitchen or the puja room — spaces reserved for food preparation and divine offering. The body itself was mapped onto this scheme: the right hand for eating (after washing), the left for cleaning after defecation. You did not hand things to others with your left hand. These were not arbitrary rules; they were part of an integrated sensory and moral grammar that said: some things are higher, some are lower, and how you treat them expresses what you believe about the order of the universe.

When Haidt returned to Chicago, he found that some of this sensibility had stayed with him. He noticed a new reluctance to leave certain books on the floor or carry them into the bathroom. Funerals and formal burial began to make emotional sense in a way they hadn't before — not because he had become religious, but because he had internalized the idea that a human body is not simply an object that becomes inert at death. There are fitting and unfitting ways to treat it, even when there is no longer anyone inside to experience the treatment. He also began to understand with fresh clarity why debates in the American culture wars so often revolve around acts of symbolic desecration. Is a national flag simply fabric, or does it carry something non-material that is damaged when it is burned in protest? When a work of art places a sacred religious object in something profane — a crucifix in a jar of urine, an image of the Virgin Mary defaced with animal waste — can the offense be dismissed by saying "don't go to the museum if you don't want to see it"? Or does the mere existence of the work introduce a kind of pollution into the shared world? To test your own reactions, Haidt suggests reversing the political valence: imagine a conservative artist using the same technique with images of Martin Luther King Jr. or Nelson Mandela, intending to satirize their veneration by the left. Would progressive museums in New York or Paris feel contaminated even after removing the paintings? The discomfort most people feel in that scenario is evidence that the ethic of divinity is not the exclusive property of the religious right — it operates wherever people treat certain figures, symbols, or values as sacred and feel violated by their profanation.

Having now felt the ethics of both community and divinity from the inside, Haidt could see what each one illuminates that autonomy-based thinking misses. The ethic of community reveals why rampant individualism can feel threatening — not because individuals are doing harm to each other in the conventional sense, but because they are neglecting the collective structures that make a livable society possible. The ethic of divinity offers a vocabulary for the widespread sense that something has gone wrong in a culture that treats the body as a playground, that commodifies everything, that allows the coarsening of public life without any moral vocabulary to name the loss. Haidt also acknowledged the dark side of each ethic — community ethics can legitimate the oppression of women and lower castes; divinity ethics can license cruelty toward anyone whose body or behavior triggers disgust in a majority. But seeing the dark side of something does not exhaust its meaning. Both ethics respond to genuine human concerns that autonomy-based morality simply cannot address.

Stepping Out Of The Matrix

Across many cultures and centuries, wise traditions have converged on a disturbing idea: the world we take to be real is something closer to an elaborate shared dream, and genuine understanding requires waking from it. The film The Matrix gave that ancient idea a vivid modern shape. When the protagonist Neo is offered a choice between a red pill that will shatter the illusion and a blue pill that will let him remain inside it, he chooses to wake up — and the familiar world dissolves around him. The experience of encountering Shweder's ideas worked on Haidt something like that red pill. He began to see that the moral world he had grown up in was not simply the moral world — it was one matrix among several, each internally coherent, each supported by evidence and argument that made perfect sense to people living inside it. Haidt had grown up in suburban New York in a Jewish family whose grandparents had fled Russia and found solidarity in the labor movement and the Democratic Party. Roosevelt was a hero; liberalism was the obvious moral position. This was not ideology in the pejorative sense — it was the texture of life, absorbed before it could be questioned.

Yale reinforced the matrix. In classrooms and dining halls throughout the 1980s, liberalism was the assumed baseline. Jokes at Reagan's expense landed without friction. The causes of the moment — opposing apartheid, supporting labor unions, advancing civil rights — seemed not just morally correct but self-evidently so. Conservative positions appeared not as alternative moral visions but as either ignorance or naked self-interest — tax cuts for the wealthy, resentment of minorities dressed up in policy language. What never occurred to Haidt or his peers was that they were embedded in a moral framework just as surely as the people they criticized, and that other frameworks might organize genuine moral concerns rather than merely rationalize selfishness. Moving from Yale to Penn to Chicago did nothing to shake him loose — all three institutions were variations on the same matrix. Only India forced him to stand outside it entirely, because there were no fellow travelers to reinforce his habitual moral assumptions.

When he returned from India, his perceptions of American political life had shifted noticeably. Listening to religious conservative leaders who called for more religious expression in schools and stricter limits on sexual content no longer automatically triggered contempt. He could now see what they were trying to do: resist what they experienced as the relentless thinning of the moral and spiritual atmosphere of public life. He might disagree with their prescriptions, but the underlying concern — that a culture shaped purely by individual desire and market logic leaves something important out — was now recognizable to him as a moral concern, not just a prejudice. The same was true of conservative arguments about welfare, family structure, and social obligations. These arguments made more sense to someone who had spent time inhabiting a worldview organized around collective obligations rather than individual rights. Haidt had not become a conservative; he had become a pluralist. He stopped looking for the single correct moral system and started asking what each system was tracking and what it was missing. The feeling that followed was, he writes, something like liberation — not because all moral views are equally valid, but because the compulsive certainty of partisan thinking had loosened its grip.

In a 1991 essay, Shweder offered a formulation that Haidt would return to again and again: when we truly understand another culture's moral conception, we recognize possibilities that were always latent within our own rationality — possibilities that had simply never been activated. We are not, at bottom, creatures with a single fixed moral nature. We are multiple from the start. The human mind carries the potential to become morally exercised about many different kinds of concerns, but any given upbringing activates only a subset of those potentials. Growing up WEIRD, you develop extraordinary sensitivity to oppression and inequality — you can detect a harm or a rights violation even when no one else in the room sees it. But you may also find yourself unable to feel what is morally at stake for someone raised in a tradition organized around sanctity, duty, or honor. Conversely, someone raised in a traditionalist community may be extraordinarily alert to disrespect and degradation, but struggle to perceive the autonomy violations that WEIRD people find most salient. The point is not relativism — not all moral matrices are equally good — but it is a call for a kind of moral imagination that goes beyond the walls of the matrix you were born into.

In Sum

Part II opens with a second foundational principle of moral psychology: there is more to morality than harm and fairness. The case for this begins with the finding that people from WEIRD societies — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic — are statistical outliers across a wide range of psychological measures, including moral ones. They are the last people you should generalize from if you want to understand human moral nature as a whole.

The WEIRDer you are, the more likely you are to perceive a world of separate objects and individuals rather than a world of relationships, roles, and contexts — and this perceptual difference has direct consequences for what you consider to be a moral concern in the first place.

Moral pluralism is a descriptive fact, not merely a philosophical position. The range of things that different human cultures treat as moral matters is genuinely wider than any single framework captures, and this variation is not random — it maps onto the three ethics of autonomy, community, and divinity.

WEIRD cultures have an unusually narrow moral domain, one largely confined to the ethic of autonomy — harm, rights, and fairness between individuals. Most other societies, and many religious and traditional communities even within WEIRD nations, operate across all three ethics simultaneously. Each moral matrix is internally coherent and emotionally compelling to those living inside it, which is precisely what makes it so difficult to perceive or take seriously the moral concerns of people living in a different matrix.

The chapters that follow will build a detailed map of the moral intuitions that make up these broader matrices — what Haidt will call the moral foundations. The goal is to give readers the tools to comprehend moral arguments that originate outside their own native framework, not necessarily to agree with them, but at least to understand what genuine human concerns they are tracking.
