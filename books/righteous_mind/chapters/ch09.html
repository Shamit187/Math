Part III · Morality Binds and Blinds

Why Are We So Groupish?

In the days following the September 11 attacks, Haidt felt a powerful and unfamiliar impulse: he wanted to put an American flag sticker on his car. The urge came out of nowhere, as if some dormant circuit in the back of his brain had suddenly switched on. For the first time in his adult life, he felt viscerally, overwhelmingly American — and he wanted to show it. Like many people, he gave blood and donated to the Red Cross, and he found himself more generous with strangers on the street. But there was a problem. He was a professor, and professors — liberal, cosmopolitan, universalist by professional temperament — do not wave flags. After three days of internal conflict, he settled on a compromise: an American flag in one corner of his rear window, and a United Nations flag in the other. He could signal love of country without looking like a nationalist.

Up to this point in the book, Haidt has offered a somewhat unflattering picture of human nature. Drawing on Glaucon's challenge from Plato's Republic, he has argued that people care more about appearing virtuous than about actually being virtuous. Intuitions fire first; reasons come later, dressed up as justifications. We fudge, deceive, and rationalize — and then believe our own rationalizations so completely that we become self-righteous about them. Most of moral psychology, Haidt concedes, can be accounted for through a lens of enlightened self-interest: selfishness shaped by kin selection and reciprocal altruism, policed by gossip and reputation. That story is largely correct, but it is incomplete. Part III introduces a new piece: human beings are not only selfish, they are also deeply groupish. We form teams, clubs, fraternities, and movements. We adopt collective identities and labor alongside strangers toward shared goals with an enthusiasm that suggests our minds were built for exactly this kind of cooperation.

The distinction Haidt draws is precise. To say humans are selfish is to say we have mental mechanisms that help us advance our own interests in competition with peers. To say humans are also groupish is to say we have a separate layer of mental mechanisms that help us advance our group's interests in competition with other groups. We are imperfect individuals but sometimes excellent team members. This groupishness raises an evolutionary puzzle. Did it arise because groupish individuals outcompeted less groupish ones within the same community — in which case it is still essentially individual-level selection in disguise? Or did it arise because whole groups that cooperated effectively outcompeted groups that could not hold together — which would require group selection, a concept that was declared scientifically heretical in the 1970s? Haidt's argument in this chapter is that group selection was wrongly convicted. He will present four exhibits in its defense, and in doing so make the case for multilevel selection: the idea that natural selection operates simultaneously at the level of individuals and the level of groups, pulling human nature in both directions at once.

These two pressures — competition within groups and competition between groups — gave rise to the peculiar mixture of selfishness and genuine selflessness that defines human moral psychology. Understanding that mixture is what Part III is about.

Key insight: 90% chimp, 10% bee. Haidt's central metaphor for human nature is that we are overwhelmingly like other primates — competitive, self-interested, strategically cooperative at best — but we also carry a thin but real overlay of hive-like psychology. Like worker bees, humans can subordinate personal gain to the group under the right conditions. This "bee" layer is not the dominant force in human nature, but it is genuinely present, and it cannot be explained away as disguised self-interest. Understanding morality, politics, and religion requires taking it seriously.

Victorious Tribes?

Darwin himself was the first to articulate the logic of group selection clearly, and also the first to identify its central problem. In The Descent of Man, he observed that when two tribes compete, the tribe with more courageous, cooperative, and loyal members will tend to prevail. Disciplined, cohesive groups function almost like unified organisms, and natural selection can operate on them just as it operates on individuals. A tribe rich in such virtues would spread and displace its rivals. But Darwin immediately saw the difficulty: within any given tribe, selfish individuals would still tend to do better than selfless ones. The person willing to sacrifice his life for the group might leave no descendants at all. The free rider — the one who shares the group's gains while avoiding its costs — would reproduce more, not less. How could group-level virtues ever persist if they are constantly undermined from within?

Darwin grasped what is now called multilevel selection. Life is organized in nested layers — genes within cells, cells within organisms, organisms within groups — and selection can operate at any of these levels. At the group level, cohesive and cooperative groups tend to win. But within any group, free riders enjoy an advantage: they collect the benefits of collective effort while shirking its costs. The bravest army may defeat its opponents, but inside that army the soldiers who hold back and avoid the thick of battle are most likely to survive and go home. Multilevel selection is the formal study of how strong each of these pressures is — how powerfully group-level competition favors cooperation versus how powerfully individual-level competition favors defection. A trait like suicidal self-sacrifice would be strongly favored by group selection but equally strongly opposed by individual selection. It can therefore only evolve in species like bees, where competition among nestmates has been nearly eliminated and nearly all selection happens at the colony level. Humans can be trained or pressured into self-sacrifice, but it does not come naturally — it requires enormous cultural scaffolding.

Given even a minimal capacity for banding together, early human groups would have entered the terrain of group-level competition, giving an edge to the most cohesive ones. But what gave those early groups their groupish tendencies in the first place? Darwin proposed a sequence of building blocks: first, social instincts that made loners more vulnerable to predation; second, reciprocity, which rewarded those who helped others; and third — most critically — the passionate concern humans feel about the praise and blame of their peers. Reputation, Darwin argued, was the engine that drove the social virtues. He also added a final ingredient: the capacity to treat moral duties as sacred, which he connected to religious sentiment. Taken together, these steps trace a path from solitary primate to social human, in which free riding becomes less and less attractive. In a real army, cowardice is not merely ineffective — it is a violation of something sacred. The coward who survives the battle may return home to find that his reputation has cost him partners, mates, and standing. Real groups suppress selfishness in many ways, and every mechanism for suppression shifts the balance in a multilevel analysis: individual selection becomes weaker, group selection becomes stronger.

Darwin summarized his view in what Haidt calls one of the most prescient sentences in the history of moral psychology: morality originates in social instincts, is shaped by the approval of our peers, is guided by reason and self-interest, and is ultimately reinforced by religion and habit. That synthesis satisfied readers for nearly a century, and group selection became a standard tool in evolutionary thinking. Trouble arrived when writers began applying it carelessly — claiming that animals restrain their feeding or reproduction "for the good of the species" without explaining how free riders would be kept in check. By the mid-twentieth century, sloppy group-selectionism had become widespread enough to provoke a sharp backlash.

A Fast Herd Of Deer?

The backlash came primarily from the biologist George Williams. Attending a lecture in 1955 in which a termite specialist praised cooperation throughout the animal kingdom and described death as nature's way of making room for the young, Williams found himself repulsed by the intellectual sloppiness. He spent the next decade writing what became Adaptation and Natural Selection (1966), a book whose mission was to purge biology of loose group-selectionist reasoning. Williams argued that natural selection is a design process, and that adaptations at a given level of organization imply a selection process operating at that level. To invoke higher-level selection when lower-level selection suffices is bad science. His paradigm case was the running deer. A herd of deer moves and reacts as a unit; one might be tempted to say that faster herds survived better than slower ones. But Williams pointed out that the real selection happened at the individual level: within every herd, slower deer were eaten while faster ones escaped. A fast herd of deer is nothing more than a herd of fast deer. No group-level explanation is needed.

Williams was not saying group selection is logically impossible. He acknowledged it could happen in principle. He set out a clear criterion for when it would be required: if you found behavioral traits that were oriented toward protecting the group rather than the individual — sentinel deer guarding the herd while the fastest runners drew predators away — that would be evidence of group-related adaptation, and only group selection could explain it. He then spent most of the book arguing that no such traits actually exist in nature. Every apparent case of animal altruism, he contended, turned out to be individual selfishness or kin selection on closer inspection. Richard Dawkins extended this argument to a mass audience with The Selfish Gene, and by the late 1970s the scientific consensus was clear: anyone who invoked group selection to explain animal behavior was not to be taken seriously. The social sciences absorbed a similar spirit. The leading model of fairness in social psychology rested on the axiom that individuals maximize their own outcomes — a premise the authors described as so obvious that not even the most contentious scientist could dispute it. All apparent altruism had to be reframed as covert self-interest.

Real life, of course, keeps producing inconvenient examples. People leave tips in restaurants they will never visit again. They give anonymously to strangers. They sometimes die saving children who are not related to them. The standard response was to call these misfirings: ancient circuits tuned to Pleistocene conditions, where most companions were kin, now misdirecting behavior in the vast anonymity of modern societies. Morality, on this view, is not an adaptation — it is a mistake. Williams went so far as to call it "an accidental capability produced, in its boundless stupidity, by a biological process that is normally opposed to the expression of such a capability." Dawkins urged readers to resist their selfish genes and teach themselves altruism, since it does not come naturally. Haidt rejects this dismissal. Humans, he argues, are the giraffes of altruism — strange outliers among primates, capable of genuinely selfless behavior in ways that ordinary evolutionary accounting cannot explain away. If Williams's own criterion holds — that group-related adaptations require a theory of group selection — then human cooperative behavior in every known society might be precisely the evidence he was asking for.

The September 11 attacks crystallized this point for Haidt personally. The attacks switched something on in millions of people simultaneously: a groupish reflex that produced flag displays, blood drives, volunteerism, and military enlistment. Hundreds of strangers drove to New York hoping to dig survivors from the rubble. Thousands enlisted in the weeks that followed. These responses fit the template of a group-related adaptation far better than they fit a model of disguised self-interest. Whether that reflex evolved specifically through group selection remains contested — most evolutionary theorists still follow Williams — but Haidt argues the time has come to reopen the case. He offers four exhibits in defense of multilevel selection, with the aim of showing not just that group selection is theoretically possible, but that it actually shaped the psychology that underlies our morality, politics, and religion.

These exhibits also tell a broader story: that our groupishness, for all the tribalism and conflict it generates, was one of the essential ingredients that made large-scale civilization possible. The capacity to form moral communities — to bind individuals into cooperating groups — did not just prepare us for war. It prepared us for the extraordinary peaceable coordination that characterizes everyday life in every human society.

Exhibit A: Major Transitions In Evolution

Consider an allegorical boat race. A hundred solo rowers set off across a wide river. Halfway through, a two-person rowboat glides past them. Then a longer craft — three rowboats lashed together, rowed by six identical siblings while a seventh steers — overtakes the pair. And just before the finish line, a team of twenty-four sisters in a motorboat sweeps by everyone. There are no rules about what kind of vessel is allowed. This fable captures the history of life on Earth. For the first billion years, all organisms were single cells competing individually. Then, around two billion years ago, two bacteria merged inside a single membrane — the origin of the eukaryotic cell, which is why mitochondria still carry their own separate DNA. These newly composite cells could reap the advantages of cooperation and internal division of labor. Competition between organelles within the cell was suppressed: they could only reproduce when the whole cell reproduced. A new kind of biological vehicle had arrived.

Biologists call these pivotal events "major transitions." Each one involves some mechanism that suppresses competition at a lower level, allowing a higher-level collective to emerge as a new kind of organism. After single-celled eukaryotes dominated the oceans, some of them began staying attached after cell division, forming multicellular organisms in which every cell carried identical genes. Once again, competition within the collective was suppressed: individual cells could only pass on genes through the organism's reproductive cells. Organs and limbs became possible through specialization, and the world filled rapidly with plants, animals, and fungi. The biologists John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry identified just eight clear major transitions in the last four billion years, the most recent of which is the emergence of human societies. Each transition follows the same pattern: suppress free riding at one level, and a new, more powerful superorganism crystallizes at the next level up. These superorganisms then begin competing with each other, and that competition between superorganisms is itself a form of group selection.

Key insight: multilevel selection. The history of life is a history of cooperation breaking out at successively higher levels of organization. Every time a mechanism appears that suppresses free riding within a collective, the collective starts behaving like a single organism. Individual-level selection becomes less important; group-level selection becomes more powerful; and the most cohesive groups come to dominate. This is not a fringe idea — it is the template for some of the most consequential events in biological history, from the origin of the eukaryotic cell to the rise of colonial insects to the emergence of human civilization.

The most striking example is the colonial insects. More than a hundred million years ago, some wasps evolved a division of labor between a single egg-laying queen and specialized worker castes. This innovation was discovered independently dozens of times — by the ancestors of termites, bees, ants, and several other lineages. In each case, the free rider problem was contained: workers could not reproduce independently, so their genes advanced only when the colony thrived. The result was a new kind of vehicle in which individual selfishness gave way to collective efficiency. Colonial insects make up only about two percent of insect species, but they claimed the richest feeding and nesting territories, transformed terrestrial ecosystems, enabled the evolution of flowering plants by providing pollinators, and now account for the majority of insect biomass on Earth. The lesson is stark: when a group figures out how to suppress internal defection, it can become overwhelmingly dominant.

Did something similar happen in human evolution? Many animals are social — they live in herds, flocks, or loose aggregations — but very few are ultrasocial, meaning they live in large, internally structured groups with a clear division of labor. Beehives and ant colonies are the classic examples, and human societies are another. What seems to have enabled the transition to ultrasociality in every non-human case is the need to defend a shared, productive nest against competitors and predators. Early human ancestors fit the same profile: they were territorial, they occupied defensible sites, they produced offspring requiring prolonged care, and they faced constant pressure from neighboring groups. These conditions, sustained over hundreds of thousands of years, pulled human evolution in the direction of greater groupishness. We are the only ultrasocial primate, and the route we took mirrors the route taken by the bees. Just as bee colonies eventually became "factories inside fortresses," human groups built granaries, walls, and armies. City-states and empires arose within a few thousand years of the invention of agriculture, spreading rapidly and transforming the Earth's ecosystems just as the colonial insects did before them. The parallel is not superficial — both are products of the same underlying evolutionary logic: major transitions driven by the suppression of free riding and the emergence of cooperative superorganisms.

Exhibit A, then, is the pattern of major transitions itself. Group selection does not require special pleading; it is the mechanism responsible for some of the most momentous events in the history of life. The question is not whether it ever happens, but whether it shaped human nature — and the evidence from our ultrasociality suggests that it did.

Exhibit B: Shared Intentionality

Every major transition requires some mechanism that suppresses internal competition. For the colonial insects it was genetics — workers share so much DNA with the queen that their own reproduction becomes evolutionarily irrelevant. For humans, the key mechanism was cognitive. The developmental psychologist Michael Tomasello, one of the world's leading experts on chimpanzee cognition, pinpointed it with a single sentence that stopped Haidt cold: "It is inconceivable that you would ever see two chimpanzees carrying a log together."

The sentence is startling because chimpanzees are by almost any measure the second most intelligent species on Earth. They make tools, deceive rivals, and predict each other's intentions with impressive accuracy. Yet they cannot carry a log together. To investigate this gap, Tomasello devised a battery of tasks that could be administered to both chimpanzees and two-year-old human children in nearly identical form. Tasks that involved reasoning about physical objects — choosing the larger of two food quantities, using a stick to retrieve an out-of-reach reward — produced identical scores: chimps and toddlers both solved them correctly about sixty percent of the time. But tasks that required reading and responding to another agent's intentions told a completely different story. Children solved these social tasks seventy percent of the time; chimps managed only thirty percent, no better than chance on many items. The gap between chimps and humans is not general intelligence — it is specifically social cognition. Something in our lineage evolved that chimps lack entirely.

Tomasello calls this capacity shared intentionality: the ability to form and hold a joint mental representation of a task that two or more individuals are pursuing together. When two people forage as a team — one bending a branch while the other picks fruit, with both anticipating the other's next move — they are doing something qualitatively different from what chimps do. Chimps sometimes appear to cooperate during monkey hunts, but Tomasello argues that each chimp is simply responding to the situation as it reads it in the moment; they are not jointly coordinating a shared plan. They fail to communicate during the hunt, and when it ends they each compete for the meat using force rather than distributing it according to any agreed-upon share. They are hunting the same prey at the same time, but they are not really working together. When early humans developed shared intentionality, everything changed. Everyone in the group could now hold a common model of the task, monitor whether partners were doing their part, notice violations of the shared plan, and react with disapproval when someone shirked or hogged the rewards. From that foundation, the first moral matrix grew: a consensual set of norms about how things are supposed to be done and what happens when they are not.

Tomasello sees the emergence of full human ultrasociality as a two-step process. The first step was shared intentionality itself — the capacity to jointly plan a hunt or a foraging expedition with two or three companions. That was the evolutionary Rubicon. Over subsequent hundreds of thousands of years, selection favored groups that could scale this capacity upward, from three people sharing a task to hundreds or thousands sharing a moral community. This second step involved what Tomasello calls "group-mindedness": learning social norms, feeling group-related emotions such as pride and shame, and obeying the social institutions that encode collective expectations. Language, often cited as humanity's decisive innovation, was itself made possible by shared intentionality — a word is not merely a sound paired with an object, it is an agreed-upon convention within a community that shares a joint representation of the world. Shared intentionality is therefore Exhibit B in the defense of group selection: it is the cognitive mechanism that allowed humans to build moral communities — weightless, portable, yet defended as fiercely as any physical nest.

Exhibit C: Genes And Cultures Coevolve

The human lineage split from the common ancestor we share with chimpanzees and bonobos somewhere between five and seven million years ago. For most of that span, the fossil record shows creatures better described as bipedal apes than as early humans: australopithecines like "Lucy," with modest brain sizes and simple tool use. Around 2.4 million years ago, hominids with significantly larger brains began to appear — the first members of genus Homo. They left behind the Oldowan toolkit: simple stone flakes used mainly to scrape meat off carcasses left by other predators. Homo habilis was a scavenger, not a hunter.

The timeline of human evolution maps the slow accumulation of cognitive and cultural capacity before the burst that made us recognizably human.

Figure

Around 1.8 million years ago a new toolkit appeared — the Acheulean tradition, centered on carefully crafted, teardrop-shaped hand axes whose symmetry signals a qualitatively different kind of mind. Yet these tools remained almost completely uniform across Africa, Europe, and Asia for more than a million years, with virtually no regional variation. This suggests the knowledge of how to make them may have been encoded genetically rather than transmitted culturally, the way a beaver's dam-building instinct is innate. True cumulative culture — where each generation builds on what the previous one learned — seems to emerge only around 600,000 to 700,000 years ago, with the first hominids whose brain size matched our own.

Figure

Among these hominids, known collectively as Homo heidelbergensis, we find the first unambiguous hearths and the first true projectile weapons: initially sharpened wooden sticks, later carefully balanced spear points hafted to wooden shafts. These people hunted large game cooperatively and brought their kills back to shared campsites to be cooked and divided.

Homo heidelbergensis is the best candidate for our Rubicon-crossing ancestor. They had cumulative culture, coordinated teamwork, and a division of labor — which implies shared intentionality and at least a rudimentary moral matrix governing how work was assigned and rewards distributed. By crossing that threshold, they changed not just the course of human evolution but its very mechanism. The anthropologists Pete Richerson and Rob Boyd have argued that cultural innovations — spear designs, cooking techniques, religious rituals — evolve by processes closely analogous to biological evolution, and that the two streams are so intertwined that neither can be studied in isolation. The clearest evidence for gene-culture coevolution comes from the history of cattle domestication. All mammals lose the ability to digest lactose after weaning; the gene for lactase production shuts off in childhood because there is no milk to drink. When some human populations began herding cattle, fresh milk became available year-round. Any individual whose genes kept the lactase gene active into adulthood could drink that milk and gain a substantial nutritional advantage. Over time, adult lactose tolerance became common in northern European and some African populations. That genetic change then fed back into culture: groups with the new gene kept larger herds and developed more elaborate milk-processing practices such as cheese-making. Cultural innovation drove genetic change; genetic change enabled further cultural innovation.

Could the same feedback loop have operated for morality? Richerson and Boyd argue that it did. Their "tribal instincts hypothesis" holds that human groups have always competed with neighbors, and that groups which stumbled upon cultural innovations enabling larger-scale cooperation tended to prevail. One of the most powerful such innovations was the use of symbolic markers — body paint, tattoos, scarification, circumcision, distinctive clothing — to signal group membership. Such markers forge a sense of shared identity that extends beyond kinship, and they are costly enough to be hard to fake. Groups that developed strong in-group markers became more cohesive, more trusting, and more capable of coordinated action than groups that did not.

Once proto-tribal cultures appeared, they altered the selection environment for genes. Individuals who struggled to internalize group norms, control antisocial impulses, or conform to collective expectations became less attractive as partners, allies, and mates. Extreme violators — especially the violent and the chronically disruptive — were shunned, expelled, or killed. This is what researchers call "self-domestication." Just as the ancestors of dogs and cats were shaped into cooperative companions by living near humans, early humans domesticated themselves by selectively excluding those who could not function within the group's moral matrix. The physical evidence supports this: modern humans have smaller teeth, smaller bodies, and reduced aggression compared to our ancestors, along with greater playfulness extending into adulthood — precisely the pattern found in domesticated animals. These tribal instincts form an overlay atop our older, more selfish primate nature: a layer of groupish emotions and social intuitions that makes large-scale cooperation possible. It is a somewhat humbling picture, but consider the alternative: without this tribal overlay, we would still be small, fragmented bands of foragers, barely more cooperative than chimpanzees, decimated by every prolonged drought. The coevolution of tribal minds and tribal cultures prepared us not just for warfare, but for the remarkable peaceful coordination that characterizes human social life at every scale. Gene-culture coevolution is Exhibit C.

Once our ancestors crossed the Rubicon into cumulative culture, their genes began tracking their cultural innovations. The most important innovations were precisely those that solved the free rider problem: markers of group membership, institutions that enforced norms, and practices that fostered collective identity. These are the kinds of changes that trigger major transitions. Even if group selection played little role in other species, human evolution since the advent of shared intentionality has been so distinctive — so shaped by culturally constructed environments — that humans may represent a genuinely special case. The broad dismissal of group selection in the 1960s and 1970s was based largely on evidence from other species and may not apply to us.

Exhibit D: Evolution Can Be Fast

If group selection shaped human nature, when did it do so? Given that humans everywhere share the same fundamental groupishness, most of the relevant genetic changes must predate our ancestors' dispersal from Africa roughly 50,000 years ago. But did genetic evolution effectively stop at that point, leaving all subsequent adaptation to culture alone? For decades, many anthropologists said yes. In 2000, the paleontologist Stephen Jay Gould asserted that natural selection had become "almost irrelevant" in modern human evolution because cultural change operates orders of magnitude faster than genetic change, and that there had been no meaningful biological change in humans for 40,000 to 50,000 years. If Gould was right, the Holocene era — the last 12,000 years — is simply irrelevant to understanding human nature. But Darwin himself was skeptical of the claim that evolution is always glacially slow. He wrote frequently about the dramatic changes that animal breeders achieved in just a few generations.

The most vivid demonstration of how fast evolution can be comes from a Soviet experiment. In the 1950s, the geneticist Dmitri Belyaev began selectively breeding silver foxes — not for fur quality, as commercial breeders did, but solely for tameness. Each generation, only the least fearful and most human-tolerant pups were allowed to breed. Within a few generations, the foxes were measurably tamer. Within nine generations, something striking happened: traits that were never selected for began appearing spontaneously. Patches of white fur emerged on heads and chests. Jaws and teeth shrank. Tails curled. These were precisely the traits that distinguish domesticated dogs from wolves. Within thirty generations the foxes were so docile they could be kept as household pets. The experiment demonstrated that selecting for one behavioral trait — tameness — could rapidly reorganize a broad package of physical and behavioral characteristics through coordinated genetic changes.

A parallel experiment with chickens illustrated that group selection can produce equally rapid change. In commercial egg farming, selecting the most productive individual hens actually reduces overall output, because the best layers tend to be the most aggressive ones. Breeding for individual productivity intensifies aggression throughout the population, leading to widespread injury and death. Mortality rates in such flocks can reach sixty percent.

Figure

In the 1980s the geneticist William Muir tried a different approach: instead of selecting the best individual hens, he simply selected the most productive cages each generation and bred all the hens in those cages together. Within three generations, aggression plummeted. By the sixth generation, mortality had fallen from sixty percent to just eight percent, and eggs produced per hen jumped from ninety-one to two hundred thirty-seven. The group-selected hens were calmer, healthier, and more productive than their individually-selected counterparts. Group selection had worked faster and more effectively than individual selection in this controlled setting. Humans were never subject to selection pressures as intense or consistent as those acting on Belyaev's foxes or Muir's hens, so the human case would take more time. But how much more? Genomic data now let us answer this empirically. Teams analyzing the sequenced genomes of thousands of people from every continent can distinguish random genetic drift from the signature of natural selection pulling specific variants through a population. The results directly contradict Gould's claim: genetic evolution in humans accelerated sharply in the last 50,000 years, with the rate of change climbing steeply after 20,000 years ago and reaching its peak during the Holocene. Far from being an evolutionary non-event, the era of agriculture and civilization was a period of intense genetic change.

This makes sense when you consider how dramatically the human environment changed. Population densities soared during the Holocene, putting more genetic variants in play. People moved into new climates, adopted new diets, built new social structures, and encountered new pathogens. Genes that were neutral under one set of conditions became strongly advantageous or harmful under another. The lactose tolerance gene is one example; another is the cluster of genetic changes that altered the hemoglobin of Tibetans to enable survival at high altitude. Dozens of such recent genetic adaptations have now been identified. If evolution could reshape bones, skin, metabolism, and blood chemistry in just a few thousand years as physical environments changed, there is no principled reason it could not simultaneously reshape social behavior and emotion as the social environment underwent equally radical transformation. Haidt is not claiming that new mental modules can be created from scratch in 12,000 years — that is probably too fast. But existing psychological tendencies, such as the moral foundations described in earlier chapters, could be recalibrated if conditions changed and then remained stable for a thousand years or more. The emergence of agricultural societies, with their hierarchies, trade networks, and long-range cooperation, would have created precisely such new and sustained selection pressures. Fast evolution is Exhibit D: if the human genome responds rapidly to cultural innovation, then group selection could have shaped meaningful aspects of human nature in timescales far shorter than the 1970s consensus assumed.

The mechanism might have been intensified during catastrophic episodes. Between 70,000 and 140,000 years ago the African climate swung violently between wet and dry periods, and a massive volcanic eruption around 74,000 years ago from the Toba volcano in Indonesia may have dramatically destabilized global climate within a single year. Genetic evidence suggests the human population was reduced to just a few thousand individuals at some point during this interval — a severe bottleneck. Those who survived almost certainly did so in groups, not as isolated individuals. Whatever combination of traits allowed those groups to hold together under extreme resource scarcity was likely selected with exceptional intensity. Even if group selection was active for only a brief portion of human evolutionary history, episodes like this could have installed the groupish overlay that subsequently enabled our species to spread rapidly out of Africa and build the first civilizations.

It's Not All About War

It is easy to describe group selection in militaristic terms — cohesive tribes defeating less cohesive ones in battle — because that is roughly how Darwin first framed it. But competition between groups does not require combat. Groups also compete in the more fundamental biological sense: which group turns available resources most efficiently into surviving offspring. Any trait that improves a group's ability to feed itself, care for its young, coordinate labor, or resolve internal disputes makes that group more productive and therefore more likely to grow and persist. Group selection favors cooperation, suppression of antisocial behavior, and motivation to act on behalf of the collective. When group-serving behavior harms outsiders, it can produce terrible violence. But the primary evolutionary logic of groupishness is about in-group welfare — building something together — not about out-group destruction. The two often go together historically, but they are conceptually separate, and it matters for how we think about the psychology that group selection produced.

In Sum

Darwin proposed that morality evolved through natural selection operating at both individual and group levels: virtuous tribes displaced selfish ones. This idea was effectively banished from science in the 1960s and 1970s when Williams and Dawkins argued that the free rider problem makes group-level selection unworkable. For three decades the sciences focused almost exclusively on competition within groups, and any seemingly altruistic behavior had to be explained as a disguised form of individual self-interest. Recent scholarship has challenged that consensus by showing that natural selection regularly operates at multiple levels simultaneously, and that groups of organisms can sometimes function as genuine units of selection.

Haidt cannot claim certainty that human nature was shaped by group selection — serious scientists disagree — but as a psychologist studying morality, he finds that multilevel selection offers the best available explanation for why human beings are simultaneously so selfish and so groupish. The four exhibits he has presented collectively amount to a defense of that position. Exhibit A, major transitions in evolution, shows that group-level selection has been responsible for some of the most transformative events in biological history; suppressing free riding at one level consistently produces more powerful and cooperative superorganisms at the next. Exhibit B, shared intentionality, identifies the cognitive mechanism that enabled humans specifically to cross that threshold: the uniquely human ability to form joint mental representations of shared tasks, which allowed early humans to cooperate, divide labor, and construct the first moral norms. Exhibit C, gene-culture coevolution, shows that once cumulative culture appeared, biological and cultural evolution became inseparably entangled. Cultural innovations — especially those that marked group membership, enforced norms, and suppressed internal conflict — altered the selection environment for genes, which in turn enabled further cultural complexity in a self-amplifying cycle. Exhibit D, the speed of evolution, demolishes the assumption that genetic human nature was frozen 50,000 years ago. Genomic evidence shows that genetic change accelerated during the Holocene; selection can produce dramatic behavioral change in dozens of generations, not millions of years.

The overall picture is of a human nature that is mostly shaped by individual-level selection — competitive, self-interested, strategically cooperative at best — but with a genuine groupish overlay that arose from the simultaneous operation of group-level selection. Most of us discovered this overlay in the days after September 11, 2001, when the rally-round-the-flag reflex switched on and made strangers feel like teammates. That reflex is not a quirk or a misfiring; it is the "bee" in us. Haidt's summary formulation is that we are ninety percent chimp and ten percent bee. The chimp part is ancient and dominant; the bee part is recent, thin, and contingent on the right conditions — but it is real. When those conditions are met, the hive switch flips, and we become capable of the kind of selfless coordination that built every civilization on Earth.
