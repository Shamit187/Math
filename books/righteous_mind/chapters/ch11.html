Part III · Morality Binds and Blinds

Religion Is a Team Sport

Every autumn Saturday, tens of thousands of people pour into college football stadiums across the United States and behave in ways that look, from the outside, unmistakably tribal. At the University of Virginia, the ritual starts hours before kickoff. Students dress in particular outfits — ties and dress shirts for men, skirts and dresses for women — and many paint team insignia on their faces. They gather at pregame parties, then migrate toward the stadium, stopping along the way to join alumni who have driven hours to set up tailgates in every nearby parking lot. By the time the game begins, a significant portion of the crowd is drunk enough to shed self-consciousness and throw themselves fully into the synchronized chants and songs that fill the next several hours. When the Cavaliers score, tens of thousands of students lock arms and sway together, singing a song that UVA students have sung in exactly that moment for over a century — a song addressed not to a player or a coach but to the community itself.

After the arm-swaying comes something more martial: coordinated fist-pumping to a battle chant, everyone moving in unison, the kind of muscular synchrony that, as the historian William McNeill observed, has long primed human groups for collective action. The game itself is almost beside the point. What matters is the shared emotional arc — outrage at bad referee calls, euphoria at touchdowns, communal grief at losses — all of it experienced together, all of it deepening the bonds among people who may have been strangers before they walked through the gate.

From a narrow, outcomes-focused perspective, college football looks extravagant and irrational. It consumes enormous resources, injures its participants, and regularly impairs the judgment of its fans. But from a sociological perspective, Haidt argues, it is doing exactly what it is designed to do: it is a ritual that lifts people from the ordinary, individual plane of daily life into something larger and shared. The sociologist Émile Durkheim called this the movement from the profane to the sacred. A football game flips what Haidt has been calling the "hive switch" — for a few hours, tens of thousands of individuals stop feeling like isolated selves and start feeling like parts of a whole. The result is real: stronger school spirit, more alumni giving, a richer community for everyone, including people who never set foot in the stadium.

This is why Durkheim insisted that religion cannot be studied by examining lone individuals. A religion, in his definition, is a unified system of beliefs and practices centered on sacred things — things set apart from ordinary life — that binds those who share them into a single moral community. Notice that the binding is not incidental to religion; it is the point. Many scientists who study religion miss this entirely. They focus on what is most visible: the individual believer and the supernatural claims that believer holds. They conclude that religion is a costly, irrational, harmful institution and cannot understand why it has persisted in every known human culture. To render a fair verdict, Haidt argues, you first have to describe religion accurately — and accurate description requires looking at groups, not just minds.

Intuition: Religion as Social Glue

Think of religion less like a textbook of metaphysical claims and more like a team uniform, a fight song, and a shared calendar of practices all rolled into one. The uniform signals membership; the song creates synchronized emotion; the calendar ensures that members repeatedly gather and renew their bonds. None of these functions require the metaphysical claims to be literally true in order to produce real social effects. Durkheim's core insight is that the social effects — cohesion, trust, shared identity — are the primary product. The theology is closer to the fight song: it matters enormously to participants, but its causal work is largely emotional and social rather than propositional.

The Lone Believer

The September 11 attacks forced a question that had been simmering since the 1980s into the open: is there something about religion — or at least Islam — that makes it uniquely prone to violence? Commentators on the right blamed the religion itself. Many on the left defended Islam as a religion of peace and pointed instead to extremism as the culprit. But a separate fracture opened within the left. A number of scientists whose politics were otherwise liberal began attacking not just radical Islam but all religion. After decades of fighting creationism in public schools, they saw little meaningful difference between Islam and Christianity — both, in their view, were systems of irrational belief that obstructed science, secularism, and progress.

Between 2004 and 2007, this discontent crystallized into a publishing phenomenon: the New Atheism. Sam Harris wrote The End of Faith, Richard Dawkins published The God Delusion, Daniel Dennett offered Breaking the Spell, and Christopher Hitchens contributed God Is Not Great. The first three — Harris, Dawkins, and Dennett — are scientists and philosophers who presented their critiques as grounded in reason and empirical evidence. Despite their differences, all three share a central assumption: religion is fundamentally about belief in supernatural agents. Harris argues that a religious belief functions like a lever — pull it, and it moves nearly everything else in a person's behavior. Dawkins frames his entire argument around what he calls the "God Hypothesis," the proposition that a supernatural intelligence designed the universe. Dennett takes a similarly belief-centered approach. For all three, the path to understanding religion runs through the propositions a believer holds about invisible beings, and the damage religion causes flows from the falseness of those propositions.

Haidt finds this framework too narrow. Supernatural agents do occupy a central place in most religions, just as a football occupies the center of a game-day crowd. But you cannot understand why college football produces such powerful collective emotion by studying the trajectory of the ball. You have to look at the whole social system: the rituals, the costumes, the synchronized chants, the shared history, the community that forms around the game. The same is true of religion. Belief, practice, and belonging are three distinct but interlocking dimensions of religious life. The New Atheists focus almost entirely on belief while underweighting practice and belonging almost entirely. Haidt calls the richer alternative the Durkheimian model: beliefs and practices exist primarily to create and sustain moral communities, and understanding that function is the key to understanding religion as a psychological and social phenomenon.

The contrast between the two models also maps onto the contrast between rationalist and social-intuitionist views of the mind. The New Atheist model implicitly assumes that people are guided primarily by their explicit beliefs — get the beliefs right, and behavior will follow. But the evidence reviewed throughout this book suggests the opposite: moral behavior is largely driven by automatic, intuitive processes embedded in social contexts, with conscious reasoning playing a secondary, post-hoc role. If that is true of morality in general, it is very likely true of religious morality in particular. The question is not just whether God beliefs are true, but what psychological and social work those beliefs are doing — and that question requires a much broader inquiry.

The New Atheist Story: By-Products, Then Parasites

From an evolutionary standpoint, religion is a puzzle. Natural selection is ruthless about eliminating costly, wasteful behaviors over time, yet religion — with its fasting, its tithing, its elaborate rituals, its time-consuming prayers — appears in every known human culture throughout recorded history. Either religiosity conferred real benefits on individuals or groups, or there must be some complicated explanation for why humans everywhere invest so heavily in something that provides no adaptive return. The New Atheists choose the latter option. Their evolutionary accounts have two stages: first, religion arose as an accidental by-product of cognitive systems that evolved for other purposes; second, it spread and persisted because it functions like a parasite.

The by-product stage begins with what researchers call the hypersensitive agency detection device. Human minds, like those of many animals, are built to distinguish events caused by intentional agents from events caused by impersonal forces. Because failing to detect a predator is far more costly than falsely detecting one that isn't there, this system is calibrated to produce many false positives. We hear a rustle in the bushes at night and assume something is watching us even when nothing is. This hair-trigger response, useful for survival, means that humans readily perceive agency in the weather, in disease, in random misfortune. Thunder sounds like someone is angry up above. A run of bad luck feels like punishment. Given this cognitive tendency, plus the human capacity for shared storytelling, it is not hard to see how early human groups would begin populating the world with invisible agents — gods, spirits, ancestors — even if no such agents actually exist.

Other cognitive modules, New Atheists argue, contribute additional raw material. Children's brains are calibrated to trust and absorb whatever their caregivers tell them — an adaptive shortcut that also makes children prone to accepting religious instruction uncritically. The circuitry for romantic attachment, which makes people fall profoundly in love, may be co-opted by some religions to produce devotion to God. Our intuitive dualism — the sense that minds and bodies are fundamentally different kinds of things — primes us to believe in immortal souls inhabiting temporary bodies. Each of these tendencies evolved for reasons unrelated to religion, but taken together they produce minds highly susceptible to religious belief. At no point in this account did religion itself provide a survival advantage. The genes that built these modules were already fixed before modern humans left Africa, and they have not changed in response to selection pressures for or against religiosity in the fifty thousand years since.

The second stage of the New Atheist story involves cultural evolution. Once supernatural beliefs existed and were being transmitted across generations, they competed with one another — not for the benefit of the people who held them, but purely on their own terms as self-replicating ideas. Dennett describes this as a kind of mental tournament: countless supernatural concepts are generated, most are too forgettable to spread, and only the most memorable and emotionally resonant survive. Dawkins uses the language of viruses: religions spread by making their hosts expend resources — time, money, emotional energy — in ways that propagate the faith, just as a cold virus makes its host sneeze to scatter copies of itself. Dennett's opening image is of a parasitic worm that commandeers an ant's nervous system and drives the ant to climb to the top of a grass blade, where it can be eaten by a grazing animal — a death that kills the ant but helps the parasite reproduce. In this view, suicide bombing is the religious equivalent: catastrophic for the individual, but adaptive for the ideology that inspired it. The practical upshot is clear: if religion is a parasite that exploits our cognitive vulnerabilities for its own benefit rather than ours, then enlightened people should work to eliminate it.

A Better Story: By-Products, Then Cultural Group Selection

Scientists who are not committed to the New Atheist program have been far more willing to consider that religion might be genuinely adaptive — that it spread and persisted partly because it made groups more successful. The anthropologists Scott Atran and Joe Henrich offer a two-step account that shares the first step with the New Atheists: diverse cognitive modules, including the hypersensitive agency detector, evolved for reasons unrelated to religion but produced supernatural beliefs as by-products. Those modules were in place before humans left Africa. But the second step is radically different. Instead of treating religions as parasitic memes competing for brain space, Atran and Henrich treat them as cultural innovations competing for group success. Religions that made groups more cohesive and cooperative spread; religions that failed to do so declined or disappeared. The selection pressure was competition between groups, not between individuals or memes.

Among the most useful things a group can do with its by-product gods, according to this account, is build a moral community around them. The gods of small-scale hunter-gatherer societies tend to be capricious — they can punish bad behavior, but they inflict suffering on the virtuous just as readily. As societies grow larger and more complex, however, their gods tend to become more moralistic. The gods of agricultural civilizations are typically preoccupied with precisely the behaviors that threaten social cohesion: murder, adultery, false testimony, broken oaths. Gods who monitor and punish cheating serve a powerful function in a world where people regularly interact with strangers they will never meet again. Research bears this out. People cheat more in dimly lit rooms and less when reminded of watchful eyes — even a cartoon image of eyes nearby reduces dishonest behavior. Activating the concept of God in someone's memory, simply by having them unscramble sentences containing God-related words, measurably reduces cheating. Creating gods who see everything and despise deceivers turns out to be an effective social technology for increasing honesty. Gods who administer collective punishment are even more powerful: when people believe that an entire village might suffer drought or disease because of one couple's adultery, the community becomes intensely vigilant about one another's behavior.

The clearest evidence that religion genuinely helps groups cohere comes from a natural experiment conducted by the anthropologist Richard Sosis, who studied two hundred communes founded in the United States during the nineteenth century. Communes are a nearly perfect test case for cooperation outside of kinship: they survive only if their members can suppress selfishness and solve the free-rider problem together. Some of these communes were religiously organized; others were secular, mostly socialist. The survival gap was dramatic: only six percent of secular communes were still functioning after twenty years, compared to thirty percent of religious ones. Sosis then searched for the variable that explained the difference. He found it: the number of costly demands each commune placed on its members. Giving up alcohol or tobacco, fasting regularly, adopting a communal dress code, cutting contact with outsiders — the more of these a commune required, the longer it lasted. But the relationship held only for religious communes. For secular communes, demanding more sacrifice provided no survival advantage at all, and most collapsed within eight years regardless of what they asked of members.

Sosis's explanation draws on the anthropologist Roy Rappaport's observation that investing social conventions with sacredness hides their arbitrariness behind a sense of necessity. When a secular organization demands sacrifice, any member is entitled to ask for a cost-benefit justification, and many will refuse practices that seem logically pointless. But when a religious community demands the same practices as part of a sacred covenant with the divine, the question of logical justification rarely arises. The practices that the New Atheists dismiss as irrational and wasteful turn out to be the very engine of group solidarity. Costly rituals function as honest signals of commitment: only those who genuinely intend to remain in the group will pay the price of admission, which means that groups who demand high costs are better protected against free riders. Sacredness binds people together precisely because it exempts certain practices from rational scrutiny, preventing the constant renegotiation that would otherwise erode shared commitments.

Intuition: Costly Commitment Signals

Imagine two clubs. The first asks only that you show up when you feel like it. The second requires you to observe a strict diet, attend weekly meetings, donate a percentage of your income, and learn a body of shared knowledge. Which club will have more trustworthy members? The second club's demands function as a filter: only people who genuinely value membership will pay those costs. This is why religious communes that demanded more from members lasted far longer than those that demanded little. The costliness is not a flaw in the design — it is the design. Sacrificial practices are the mechanism by which groups sort committed members from casual ones, and high-commitment groups are the ones capable of the large-scale cooperation that makes civilizations possible.

Atran and Henrich stop short of claiming that genetic evolution tracked these cultural changes. Moralistic high gods are, they note, a relatively recent cultural development — emerging with agriculture in the last ten thousand years, too recently to have reshaped the human genome. The gene-culture coevolution they describe is purely cultural: religions that built more cooperative groups spread at the expense of less effective religions, not by killing off their competitors but by inspiring adoption. Haidt finds this position too conservative. Given how rapidly genetic evolution can proceed when selection pressures are consistent, it seems implausible that fifty thousand years of living inside religious moral communities left human psychology entirely untouched. The modules that generate religious experience — the hive switch, the capacity for sacralization, the tendency toward moral emotion — may well have been fine-tuned by selection pressures that were themselves shaped by the religions those modules helped create.

The Durkheimian Story: By-Products, Then Maypoles

The biologist David Sloan Wilson spent decades rehabilitating group selection after it was largely dismissed by mainstream evolutionary biology in the 1970s. He produced mathematical proofs that genetic group selection is possible under conditions that early human societies plausibly met. He then did the harder work: examining the histories of real religions to test whether they actually created the social conditions that group selection requires. Wilson's central achievement was to fuse Darwin and Durkheim into a single coherent framework. Darwin worried about how group-level moral behavior could evolve when free riders should always outcompete cooperators within any group. Durkheim showed that religions create unified moral communities in which members function, in critical respects, as a single organism. Wilson's synthesis: if Durkheim is right that religion produces genuine cohesion, then Darwin's hypothesis — that group-level moral adaptations can evolve by group selection — becomes empirically plausible.

In Darwin's Cathedral, Wilson surveys religious communities across history to show this dynamic at work. John Calvin built a strict, demanding form of Christianity in sixteenth-century Geneva that suppressed free-riding and generated remarkable levels of civic trust. Medieval Jewish communities created, in Wilson's phrase, "cultural fortresses" with dense internal social structures that kept members in and outsiders out — and in doing so produced extraordinary levels of internal cooperation. Wilson's most striking example, developed from research by the anthropologist Stephen Lansing, involves the water temple system in Bali before Dutch colonization. Rice farming in Bali required massive coordination: hundreds of terraced paddies fed by a complex network of aqueducts and tunnels, all of which had to be drained and flooded on precise schedules. The Balinese solution was to embed a small temple at every fork in the irrigation system. Each temple united all the downstream farmers who depended on that fork into a congregation. Disputes about water allocation were settled in a sacred context, which reduced the temptation to cheat. Gods and rituals were woven into the hydraulic infrastructure at every level, allowing thousands of farmers spread across hundreds of square kilometers to coordinate without any central government. Dutch hydrologists, who were among the world's best engineers, found almost nothing to improve.

Wilson's preferred metaphor for the role that gods play in this system is the maypole. Watching a single person circle a maypole looks pointless — just someone walking in circles around a stick. But add a dozen more dancers, alternating men and women, each holding a ribbon, circling in opposite directions, weaving around each other — and something remarkable emerges. The individual movements become collective coordination, and the ribbons braid into a fabric around the pole. The pole does not compel the dance; it organizes it. Gods function the same way. They give communities a shared center around which to orient their behavior, their rituals, and their obligations. As Wilson puts it, religions exist primarily to help people achieve together what they cannot achieve alone. Even the morally capricious gods of small-scale hunter-gatherer societies serve this function. The anthropologist Lorna Marshall's description of a healing dance among the !Kung San shows it clearly: whatever tensions and grievances divide the community during ordinary life, the dance sweeps everyone into synchronized movement — stamping, clapping, singing — and for the duration, the group becomes a unit. People who may dislike each other are temporarily fused into a single coordinated body. Haidt observes that the !Kung would probably have a great time at a UVA football game.

If human groups have been doing this — using rituals, gods, and sacred practices to generate collective effervescence and suppress selfishness — for tens of thousands of years, and if doing it well rather than poorly made some groups more likely to survive and flourish, then gene-culture coevolution seems almost inevitable. Especially striking is the timing: the recent genetic evidence suggests that human evolution accelerated dramatically during the Holocene, precisely the period when agriculture was spreading, cities were growing, and moralistic high gods were emerging. The science writer Nicholas Wade, reviewing the evidence for prehistoric religious practice, argues that the adaptive logic of religion is clearest at the group level: religiously cohesive groups were more likely to survive conflict, hold together under stress, and pass their cultural practices on to the next generation. Genes that inclined individuals toward the kinds of hiving, sacralizing, and ritual participation that made groups stronger would, over generations, become more common. Gods and moral communities, in this account, are not parasites infecting otherwise healthy minds — they are the cultural environment in which human minds evolved, and the two have shaped each other across an extraordinarily long time.

This conclusion has practical implications that differ sharply from those of the New Atheist account. If religion is a parasite, the cure is education and rational argument. But if religion is something close to a co-evolutionary partner of the human mind — a product of the same long process that built our capacity for language, moral emotion, and large-scale cooperation — then expecting people to abandon it through rational persuasion misunderstands what religion actually is. People can and do leave specific organized religions, which are relatively recent cultural forms. But the underlying psychology — the need to sacralize, to belong, to participate in shared rituals that generate collective identity — is not so easily reasoned away. Asking human beings to live entirely without sacred communities and shared moral matrices may be like asking them to give up physical gravity: it can be imagined, but the longing for what was lost would be constant, and the social consequences would be unpredictable.

Is God a Force for Good or Evil?

The New Atheists claim that religion is one of the primary engines of human violence and oppression. Their critics, many of them religious believers, respond that atheists are unmoored from any genuine moral foundation and cannot be trusted. John Locke, one of the architects of liberal political thought, argued that promises and oaths — the basic mechanisms of civil society — cannot bind someone who does not believe in God, because the fear of divine punishment is what gives such commitments their force. Both sides, Haidt suggests, are partly right and mostly missing the point.

For decades, studies of religious generosity pointed in contradictory directions. On surveys, religious people reported giving more to charity and expressed more altruistic values. But in laboratory experiments where subjects could actually help anonymous strangers, religious believers performed no better than non-believers. The apparent contradiction dissolves, however, once you recognize what religion is designed to do. If religion is a group-level adaptation that produces cooperation within a moral community, then it should generate parochial altruism — exceptional generosity toward fellow members, particularly when reputation is at stake — not unconditional altruism toward strangers. And that is exactly the pattern the data reveal. Americans in the least religious fifth of the population give about one and a half percent of their income to charity. Those in the most religious fifth — measured by church attendance rather than stated belief — give roughly seven percent, the bulk of it to religious organizations. Religious people also volunteer far more than their secular counterparts, mostly through their religious communities. Even in laboratory trust games, religious subjects cooperate more with each other. A team of German economists found that knowing a trading partner was religious led people to extend more trust — and that religious partners actually returned more money, even without knowing anything about their counterpart's religiosity. High-trust environments, the researchers found, are especially productive when religious people interact with other religious people.

Historical evidence reinforces the picture. In the ancient world, temples were commercial infrastructure: contracts were signed and oaths sworn before a deity, with supernatural punishment specified for breach. In the medieval world, Jews and Muslims excelled at long-distance trade in part because their religious communities provided mechanisms for creating trust and enforcing agreements across vast distances. Even today, markets that require unusually high trust — the diamond trade is the classic example — are often dominated by religiously cohesive ethnic communities, whose transaction costs are lower because cheating within the community carries social and spiritual consequences that secular institutions cannot replicate.

So religion demonstrably helps groups cohere and cooperate. But does it help or harm outsiders? The political scientists Robert Putnam and David Campbell tackled this question systematically, using large survey datasets on American religious life. Their answer surprised many people: the more regularly Americans attend religious services, the more generous they are — not only to religious causes but to secular ones as well, and not only in church-organized volunteer work but in civic associations of all kinds. Religious Americans gave more to the American Cancer Society, spent more time in neighborhood organizations, and reported higher levels of civic engagement than their secular counterparts, even after controlling for other factors. When Putnam and Campbell looked for what actually drove these differences, they found it was not theology. Belief in hell, frequency of prayer, specific denominational affiliation — none of these correlated with the prosocial outcomes. The only reliable predictor was how embedded a person was in relationships with fellow members of their religious community. The mechanism is social, not doctrinal. Close relationships within a morally serious community, oriented toward values larger than individual self-interest, bring out cooperative behavior regardless of what any particular creed says. Putnam and Campbell's conclusion cuts directly against the New Atheist framework: what matters for being a good neighbor is religious belonging, not religious believing.

This finding does not vindicate every religion in every time and place. Parochial altruism has a shadow side: the same solidarity that makes members generous toward one another can make them indifferent or hostile toward outsiders. The question is whether, on balance, the social surplus that religion generates stays inside the community or spills out into the broader society. In contemporary America, Putnam and Campbell's data suggest it spills out substantially. But that generalization should not be extrapolated uncritically to all historical contexts.

Chimps and Bees and Gods

The finding that religion promotes prosocial behavior within communities does not mean that religion is always a force for peace between them. Religion co-evolved with human groupishness, and groupishness is inherently parochial — it defines an in-group partly by defining an out-group. Haidt argues that religion did not cause suicide bombing, for example. The political scientist Robert Pape, analyzing a database of every suicide attack over a century, found that suicide terrorism is primarily a response to military occupation by a culturally alien power — specifically, boots on the ground in what communities regard as sacred territory. Ideology matters too: there must be some framework capable of convincing young men that self-destruction in service of a larger cause is noble. That ideology can be secular, as with the Marxist-Leninist Tamil Tigers of Sri Lanka, or religious. Religion is often an amplifier of group conflict rather than its root cause. It is particularly well suited to sacralize the in-group and demonize the out-group, which is why it so frequently appears as an accessory to atrocity even when it is not the original driver.

Stepping back from these worst cases, Haidt argues that the overall arc of religion in human history is one of net social benefit, even accounting for the violence it has accompanied. Humans are, as he has argued throughout this book, simultaneously individualistic and groupish — part chimp, part bee. Successful religions have worked on both levels: they provide gods who create incentives for individual compliance with group norms, and they provide rituals that periodically dissolve the sense of individual separateness and allow people to experience genuine self-transcendence. Religions are, in his phrase, "moral exoskeletons." Living inside one means being embedded in a web of norms, relationships, and institutions that continuously shape behavior in the direction of cooperation and restraint — not primarily by convincing the rider through argument but by working on the elephant through habit, social pressure, and shared identity. An atheist living outside any such community must rely more heavily on an internal moral compass, guided by individual reasoning. This is not impossible, but it is more demanding, and societies that abandon shared moral matrices without replacing them with something equally binding risk what Durkheim called anomie — a kind of normlessness in which individuals, freed from shared expectations, find themselves more anxious and less connected. Durkheim showed more than a century ago that declining social cohesion correlates with rising rates of depression and suicide. The first fully secular societies have only emerged in parts of northern Europe in the last few generations, and they are, notably, among the least reproductively successful societies in human history — wealthy and peaceful, but struggling to regenerate themselves. Whether that reflects the absence of religious community specifically, or some broader loss of shared purpose, remains an open question.

The Definition of Morality (At Last)

Haidt has spent eleven chapters building toward a definition of morality, and he has deliberately delayed offering one. The delay was strategic: the definition he arrives at would have seemed wrong, or at least strange, at the beginning of the book, before the reader had been walked through the evidence against moral rationalism, the expansion of the moral domain across six foundations, and the argument for groupishness as a key driver of human social evolution. Now he is ready. His starting point is Durkheim's formulation: the moral is whatever promotes solidarity and forces people to regulate their behavior according to something beyond their own self-interest. Durkheim, as a sociologist, focused on social facts — institutions, norms, shared meanings — that constrain individual egoism from the outside. Haidt wants to include the inside as well: the moral emotions, the automatic intuitions, the evolved psychological architecture that generates moral experience in the first place. His synthesis: moral systems are interlocking sets of values, virtues, norms, practices, identities, institutions, technologies, and evolved psychological mechanisms that work together to suppress or regulate self-interest and make cooperative societies possible.

This is a functionalist definition — it describes morality by what it does rather than by specifying which values count as genuinely moral. It is explicitly descriptive rather than normative. Haidt is not claiming that every moral system is equally good; he is claiming that every genuine moral system is performing the same basic function of enabling large-scale human cooperation. A definition that designates a narrow set of values — say, harm and fairness — as the only truly moral values, and dismisses everything else as mere social convention, is not a universal description of morality. It is the moral framework of a particular community — educated, politically liberal, Western — dressed up as a universal standard. The six foundations exist across cultures; any account of morality that only recognizes two of them is incomplete as a descriptive theory of what morality actually is.

At the same time, Haidt does not think his descriptive definition makes normative ethics impossible. It does mean that normative theories need to take social facts seriously. Utilitarian ethics since Bentham has focused on maximizing individual welfare, often treating social institutions as merely instrumental. But a Durkheimian utilitarianism would recognize that human flourishing is partly constituted by belonging — by being embedded in communities with shared purposes, shared practices, and shared obligations. Such a framework would be more open to the possibility that the binding moral foundations — loyalty, authority, and sanctity — contribute something irreplaceable to social order, and that public policies which erode them carry real costs that a purely harm-and-welfare calculus tends to miss. For questions of personal ethics, Haidt does not claim to know the right normative framework. But for the design of laws and public institutions in diverse societies, he thinks utilitarian reasoning, enriched by Durkheimian sociology, is the most defensible starting point — provided that Bentham is willing to take seriously what Durkheim showed about who we actually are.

In Sum

If you approach religion as primarily a system of claims about invisible beings, you will almost certainly misjudge it. You will see it as a collection of false beliefs that persist through cognitive weakness and cultural exploitation — a parasite masquerading as a partner. But if you follow Durkheim and Darwin together, a different picture emerges. Religion is first and foremost a technology for building moral communities. Its beliefs and practices work in concert to generate the trust, commitment, and shared identity that allow large numbers of people — people who are not kin, who have competing interests, who might never meet again — to cooperate reliably at scale.

The hypersensitive agency detector probably did give rise to supernatural beliefs as a by-product, just as the New Atheists claim. But once those beliefs existed, they became raw material that cultural evolution could shape. Groups that used their gods to demand costly commitment, suppress cheating, and generate collective identity had a decisive advantage over groups that failed to do so. The religious communes that lasted were the ones that demanded the most. The trust games in which religious people outperformed secular ones illustrate the mechanism in miniature: gods who see everything and punish defection make credible commitments credible, and credible commitments make cooperation possible. Over many thousands of years — possibly accelerating during the Holocene as agriculture created new scales and new demands for cooperation — religious minds and religious institutions co-evolved into a tightly fitted system.

Religion is a team sport in the deepest sense: it is not about what any individual privately believes, but about what a group does together, around shared sacred objects, in repeated rituals that generate belonging and reinforce mutual obligation. Understanding this does not require endorsing any particular religion's metaphysical claims. It does require taking seriously what those communities actually accomplish — and recognizing that the human longing for sacred community is not a cognitive error to be corrected but a feature of our social nature to be understood. In the final chapter, Haidt will bring this framework to bear on political psychology: why people sort themselves into political teams, what those teams demand of their members, and how the same binding-and-blinding dynamic that operates in religion operates in partisan politics as well.
