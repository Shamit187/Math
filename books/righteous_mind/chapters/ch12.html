Part III · Morality Binds and Blinds

Can't We All Disagree More Constructively?

A Chicago humorist once quipped that politics is no game for the faint-hearted — and the remark has been invoked ever since to excuse the rough and often dishonest character of American political life. Rational idealists might imagine a world where dispassionate experts craft policy in a spirit of pure reason, but real democracy runs on competition: parties compete for votes, candidates compete for dollars, and in that contest deception and demonization are perennial temptations. Everybody's inner press secretary works overtime to cast their own side as virtuous and the opposition as catastrophically misguided. Still, the question lingers: does it really have to be this bad? Many Americans sense that something has shifted, that the country has grown not merely divided but actively dysfunctional. The evidence bears them out. Surveys over the past decade show a slow but real migration away from the political center, with the share of people identifying as moderate declining while the shares calling themselves liberal or conservative each tick upward. The electorate as a whole has spread apart, but that spreading is modest compared with what has happened in Washington and in media culture.

The modest ideological drift in the broader electorate pales beside what has happened inside Washington itself. Starting in the 1990s, the norms and social architecture of Congress changed in ways that corroded the human relationships that had long softened partisan conflict. Members of Congress were discouraged from forming friendships across party lines. Once those personal ties frayed, it became easier to treat the opposing party not as a rival club of colleagues but as an enemy force. Campaign strategy shifted toward digging up damaging information on opponents and feeding it to journalists. One veteran congressman described the chamber as no longer collegial but tribal, its members arriving on the floor already primed with contempt. This turn toward a righteous, us-versus-them mentality was corrosive enough during the relative prosperity of the 1990s. But as the country's fiscal and political circumstances worsened, the consequences of gridlock became genuinely dangerous. In 2011, the two parties could not reach agreement on routine legislation to raise the debt ceiling, leading a credit-rating agency to downgrade the United States for the first time in its history — an event that rattled global markets and darkened economic prospects worldwide. American hyperpartisanship had become a problem not just for Americans but for anyone who depended on a stable global economy.

So what drives this escalating tribalism? Earlier in this book, the culture war was described as a clash between a three-foundation morality and a six-foundation morality. But that framing raises a prior question: what draws people to one moral framework or the other in the first place? The psychology of political identity is not random. Some people bind themselves to liberal teams, some to conservative ones, and some to neither. Understanding the origins of that binding — and the blinding that comes with it — is essential for understanding how American politics reached this point.

A Note About Political Diversity

The discussion that follows focuses on liberals and conservatives — the two poles of the most commonly used political dimension. Many people resist reducing the full complexity of ideology to a single left-right axis, and they are right to be wary: Moral Foundations Theory itself offers six distinct dimensions and allows for an enormous variety of combinations. Real people do not sort neatly into two types. The problem is that most research in political psychology has been conducted using American samples and measured along the liberal-conservative dimension, so that is largely what the evidence can speak to.

Even so, this single dimension captures something real and important. Most Americans and most Europeans can locate themselves on it, even if many cluster near the middle. More significantly, it is the primary axis along which congressional voting divides and along which the culture war is fought. Understanding the psychology of the two endpoints — even if relatively few people inhabit the extremes — is therefore essential for grasping a problem whose effects reach well beyond America's borders.

From Genes To Moral Matrices

Ideology can be defined simply as a set of beliefs about how society should be ordered and how that order can be achieved. The most fundamental ideological question is whether the existing order should be preserved or changed. That divide was literally spatial at the French Assembly of 1789, where preservation-minded delegates sat to the right of the chamber and change-minded delegates sat to the left — bequeathing us the terms we still use today. Political theorists from Marx onward long assumed that people chose sides based on economic self-interest: those with property and power wanted stability, while those without wanted transformation. But the link between class and ideology has largely dissolved in the modern era. Wealthy people spread across both parties, as do the poor, and when researchers have tested the self-interest hypothesis directly, it performs poorly as a predictor of political attitudes. For most of the late twentieth century, political scientists fell back on socialization theories — people absorb the politics of their parents or their media environment — and some went so far as to claim that most ordinary citizens had no coherent political ideology at all.

Then twin studies changed the picture. When researchers began comparing large samples of identical twins to same-sex fraternal twins, a consistent pattern emerged: identical twins, who share all their genes, resembled each other more closely on nearly every measurable trait — including political orientation. Crucially, identical twins raised in separate homes still tended to converge, while unrelated children raised together in the same household tended to diverge. Genes, it turned out, contribute to virtually every dimension of personality, from taste in music to religiosity to political leaning. Estimates suggest that genetic differences account for somewhere between a third and a half of the variation in political attitudes across individuals, while the household one is raised in accounts for far less than most people assume. This seems puzzling: how can there be a genetic basis for opinions about tax policy or foreign aid, questions that did not exist a century ago? The key is to remember that "innate" does not mean "fixed at birth." Genes shape the initial architecture of the brain — the first draft — but that draft is revised continuously by experience. The path from genes to a voting booth involves at least three steps.

Step 1 is that genes shape brain architecture. A large Australian study found several genes that differed between self-identified liberals and conservatives, many of them related to how the brain processes threat and regulates the neurotransmitters glutamate and serotonin. Conservatives, on average, react more strongly to signals of danger and contamination; liberals, on average, show stronger responses to novelty and new experience — a difference partly traceable to genes that influence dopamine systems. None of these genetic effects is large on its own, but collectively they nudge the brain's baseline settings on two key dimensions: threat sensitivity and openness to experience. Researchers have consistently found that these two dimensions, more than any others, distinguish people who end up on the left from those who end up on the right. Step 2 is that those dispositional traits steer children down different paths. Personality, according to psychologist Dan McAdams, operates at three levels. The lowest and most stable level consists of broad "dispositional traits" — not special modules that some people have and others lack, but more like dial settings on systems that everyone shares. A child whose dial sits high on threat sensitivity and low on novelty-seeking will, from early on, move through the world differently from a sibling whose dials are set in the opposite direction — even if the two grow up in the same home, attend the same school, and have the same parents. Their different temperamental orientations will attract different responses from teachers, pull them toward different friendships, and steer them toward different activities and environments.

Above those basic dispositional traits sits a second level: "characteristic adaptations," the habits, goals, and coping strategies that develop as a person navigates specific situations. A child predisposed toward novelty-seeking who ends up in a rigidly structured school may become rebellious in ways she would not have been had the school been more permissive — and that rebelliousness can become a defining feature of her personality. Consider a hypothetical pair of fraternal twins, a brother and a sister, whose genes have set their dials in opposite directions. Both grow up in the same house, but their different temperaments cause the world to treat them differently from the start. By the time they reach high school, they are choosing different extracurricular activities and different peer groups. The sister, drawn to novelty, leaves for a large urban university where she becomes immersed in a social world organized around care for vulnerable people. Her brother, more sensitive to threat and more comfortable with familiar structure, stays close to home, joins his family's church, works his way into a stable professional role, and becomes embedded in moral communities where loyalty to groups and personal responsibility are the most frequently invoked virtues. By the time they are adults, each twin has been shaped by thousands of micro-environments that their own temperament helped select. Neither outcome was foreordained on the day they were born, but neither was it accidental.

The third level of personality, in McAdams's framework, is the life narrative — the story a person constructs to make sense of who they are and where they are going. Human beings are story-processing creatures as much as logic-processing ones. The narratives we build about our own lives are not strict chronologies of events; they are selective, often idealized reconstructions that link past experiences to present identity and future aspiration. These narratives are saturated with morality. When McAdams asked liberal and conservative Christians to describe the development of their faith and moral beliefs, the stories diverged in precisely the ways Moral Foundations Theory would predict: conservatives emphasized themes of respect for authority, group loyalty, and self-purification, while liberals emphasized themes of suffering witnessed and injustice opposed. Once a person has woven a life narrative in which they are a crusader against abusive power, or a steward of inherited institutions, their political identity is no longer a mere opinion — it is part of who they are. Changing it would require not just revising a belief but rewriting a self.

Keith Richards offers a vivid illustration. As a student, Richards was a well-behaved member of his school choir. When puberty changed his voice and he was dropped from the choir, he and his friends were informed they would have to repeat an entire year of school to compensate for the classes they had missed while travelling for competitions — and the choirmaster who had arranged those trips did nothing to defend them. Richards describes the experience as transformative: it turned him into a rebel with a slow-burning rage against authority. Had his teachers responded differently, or had Richards simply told himself a different story about what happened, his political sympathies might have developed in a very different direction. But once his life narrative cast him as someone who had been betrayed by those in power, every subsequent experience confirmed and deepened that story, and no narrative offered by any conservative political party would ever feel like his own.

The Grand Narratives Of Liberalism And

Conservatism

The sociologist Christian Smith has argued that every moral community has a sacred core, and that grand narratives — stories with a beginning, a conflict, and a resolution — are the primary vehicles through which that sacred core is transmitted and reinforced. Each narrative orients its listeners toward a particular set of virtues and villains, and instructs them about what must be done to protect or restore what is most precious. The liberal grand narrative, as Smith reconstructs it, runs roughly as follows: For most of human history, people were ground down by unjust, hierarchical institutions that exploited the many for the benefit of the few. Through the courageous struggle for autonomy and equality, humanity broke free from those chains and built the modern democratic welfare state. But the vestiges of inequality and oppression persist, and the work of liberation is not yet finished — it is the most important calling a person can answer. This narrative draws its moral force primarily from the Care foundation (deep concern for those who suffer) and the Liberty foundation understood as freedom from oppression and freedom to define oneself. Fairness appears primarily as equality of treatment; Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity are either invisible in the narrative or cast as forces that must be overcome.

The conservative grand narrative has a different structure and a different cast of heroes and villains. The version that Ronald Reagan deployed most powerfully in the 1980 election can be summarized as follows: America was once a beacon of strength, faith, and self-reliance. Then an expanding federal government began confiscating the earnings of hard-working citizens and redistributing them to those who refused to work. Traditional values were mocked, religious faith was pushed out of public life, and the military was weakened while criminals were coddled. The family — the foundation of civilized life — was undermined by social permissiveness and ideological radicalism. America must reclaim what it has lost. Where the liberal narrative is a story of liberation, the conservative narrative is a story of defense and restoration. Its emotional force is less visually dramatic — closer to a family methodically repairing termite damage than to crowds storming a fortress — but it draws on a far wider range of moral foundations. Care appears, but mainly for the victims of crime rather than the victims of poverty; Liberty appears as freedom from government overreach; Fairness appears as proportionality — the insistence that effort and reward should be linked. Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity each play starring roles.

The two narratives are almost perfectly opposed, and that opposition creates a profound obstacle to mutual comprehension. Conservatives, who draw on all six foundations, can at least recognize the Care and Liberty intuitions that power the liberal story, even if they weigh them differently. But liberals, who organize their moral matrix primarily around Care and Liberty, often cannot perceive the moral content of the binding foundations at all. When Haidt and colleagues asked liberals and conservatives to fill out the Moral Foundations Questionnaire as they imagined a "typical" member of the other party would, the results were telling. Conservatives made reasonably accurate predictions about liberal values. But liberals systematically underestimated how much conservatives cared about Care and Fairness — and overestimated how cold and indifferent conservatives were to suffering. When a liberal looks at the Reagan narrative and sees no compassion for the poor, no concern for fairness, only tribal loyalty and moralistic policing of private life, they may conclude that conservatives simply do not value human welfare at all. That conclusion is wrong, but it is an almost inevitable product of looking at a six-foundation moral matrix through a two-foundation lens. The practical consequence is that political liberals often fail to engage conservatism on its own terms — and sometimes dismiss it in terms that are themselves morally revealing.

The asymmetry of comprehension matters enormously for political life. When one side cannot see the other's moral concerns as genuinely moral — when loyalty appears as mere tribalism, authority as oppression, and sanctity as superstition — it becomes impossible to engage in good-faith argument. Instead of disagreeing about how to balance real competing values, the two sides end up disagreeing about whether the other side has any values at all. That is the condition Haidt calls Manichaean politics: the conviction that the other team is not merely wrong but wicked, and that defeating them is a moral imperative rather than a political goal.

The Left's Blind Spot: Moral Capital

Haidt describes his own intellectual journey as having two turning points. The first, in India, cracked open his assumption that Western liberal morality was the only fully developed form of moral thinking. The second came years later, in a used bookstore in New York, when he picked up a volume of readings on the intellectual history of conservatism edited by the historian Jerry Muller. Standing in the aisle — and then sitting on the floor — he read Muller's introduction and found himself confronting a version of conservatism he had never seriously considered: not a politics of religious orthodoxy or aristocratic reaction, but a reasoned, Enlightenment-grounded tradition that sought to discover, through history and experience, what actually makes human societies flourish. The key insight that stopped him in his tracks was that modern conservatism is not simply the rejection of liberal ideals; it is a competing theory of how to achieve human happiness, built on a different set of assumptions about human nature.

Those conservative assumptions, as Muller laid them out, were largely familiar from Haidt's own research. People are not naturally benevolent when unconstrained — they tend to behave selfishly when accountability and social structure are removed. Human reasoning is systematically biased and overconfident, which makes pure rationalist social engineering dangerous. Institutions — laws, customs, traditions, religious practices — accumulate authority over time precisely because they have proven their worth in coordinating behavior and suppressing the worst human impulses. Strip those institutions of their authority by treating them as arbitrary conveniences, and social cooperation begins to break down. Reading through conservative thinkers from Edmund Burke in the eighteenth century to Friedrich Hayek and Thomas Sowell in the twentieth, Haidt found a concept he had not previously encountered in liberal social science: what he calls moral capital.

Moral capital builds on the more familiar idea of social capital — the networks of trust, reciprocity, and mutual obligation that make groups more productive and cooperative. But where social capital refers to the relationships among people, moral capital refers to the broader environment of values, institutions, norms, practices, and shared identities that sustains those relationships and makes cooperation possible in the first place. High moral capital enables a community to suppress free-riding, coordinate around shared goals, and hold together under stress. Low moral capital — the condition of anomie that Durkheim described — produces individuals who are self-seeking and communities that fracture under pressure. Crucially, moral capital does not arise automatically from good intentions or rational design. It is built up slowly through the accumulation of practices that have proven their worth, and it can be destroyed quickly by reforms that, however well-intentioned, undermine the structures that held the community together.

Intuition: Moral Capital as Social Infrastructure

Think of moral capital the way an engineer thinks about physical infrastructure. A bridge is not just steel and concrete; it is the accumulated product of centuries of engineering knowledge, tested materials, and hard-won construction norms. A community with high moral capital is similarly the product of accumulated norms, institutions, and practices that have been tested by time. You can tear out a bridge quickly, but rebuilding it takes far longer — and in the interim, people cannot get across the river. Liberal reforms that dismantle social institutions without building replacements risk leaving communities without the infrastructure they need to function morally, even when the old infrastructure had genuine flaws.

This concept helps explain a recurring pattern in the history of progressive reform: well-intentioned changes that weaken existing institutions sometimes destroy more moral capital than the reforms create in new social goods. Communities, like markets, depend on trust and predictability. When reformers treat traditions, religions, or group loyalties as arbitrary obstacles to a more rational social order, they may inadvertently remove the very structures that were keeping people honest, cooperative, and engaged with their neighbors. This is not an argument against reform. Many historical institutions have been deeply unjust, and the suffering they sustained was real. But it is an argument for caution and sequencing — for asking not just whether an institution is flawed, but whether dismantling it will leave people better or worse able to cooperate in pursuit of any shared moral vision at all.

Moral capital must be distinguished from a simple good. High moral capital can exist in communities that are tightly knit but parochial — even in cults or authoritarian states, where solidarity is achieved through coercion and exclusion rather than through genuine shared values. A community can be internally cohesive and externally predatory. Moral capital also does not automatically generate fairness in the sense of equal opportunity: a tight-knit community may powerfully enforce its own norms while systematically excluding outsiders. These qualifications are real and important. The point is not that moral capital is always worth preserving at any cost, but that reformers ignore it at their peril. The left's characteristic blind spot, on this account, is a tendency to see institutions, traditions, and group loyalties only as sources of oppression — never as the fragile scaffolding that holds moral communities together. That blind spot helps explain why liberal reforms sometimes backfire, and why communist revolutions, which pursued ambitious moral goals by destroying all existing institutions at once, so often ended in tyranny. Conservatism, conversely, tends to see existing institutions as inherently valuable and can fail to recognize which ones genuinely perpetuate injustice. Neither orientation alone is sufficient.

A Yin And Two Yangs

The Chinese concept of yin and yang describes forces that appear opposed but are in fact complementary — each necessary, neither complete without the other. Night needs day; winter needs summer; neither is the enemy of the other. John Stuart Mill applied a version of this logic to politics: a party of stability and a party of reform are both essential to a healthy political life. The philosopher Bertrand Russell extended the thought across the full sweep of Western intellectual history, noting that philosophers have persistently divided into those who want to tighten social bonds and those who want to loosen them — and that both are partly right. Excessive tightening produces the ossification of rigid tradition; excessive loosening produces the dissolution of any shared life at all. A healthy society needs both impulses, in shifting and productive tension.

With that framework in mind, it becomes possible to evaluate specific ideological positions not as absolute truths or total errors, but as partial insights that each capture something real about human social life. Rather than treating politics as a binary contest between liberals and conservatives, it is more illuminating to divide the opponents of the left into two distinct camps: libertarians, who hold individual liberty as their supreme value, and social conservatives, who hold the health of inherited institutions as their supreme value. These two groups have significantly different personalities, different moral foundation profiles, and different insights to offer. What follows is a brief case for what each tradition gets right — and where the liberal tradition, for all its genuine contributions, needs to be balanced by the other two.

Intuition: Political Diversity as Moral Biodiversity

Ecologists know that a forest is healthier when it contains many species, because different species respond differently to changing conditions — what devastates one may leave another untouched. Political orientations work similarly. Liberals are exquisitely sensitive to the suffering of victims and to the injustices embedded in existing arrangements; this is indispensable. Libertarians are exquisitely sensitive to the costs that government action imposes on individual freedom and market efficiency; this too is indispensable. Social conservatives are exquisitely sensitive to threats to the institutions and norms that hold communities together; losing this sensitivity would leave societies unable to diagnose moral entropy. Eliminating any of these orientations from public life would be like eliminating a keystone species — the ecosystem would become less resilient, less capable of adapting to whatever challenges it has not yet imagined.

Yin: Liberal Wisdom

The liberal moral matrix rests most firmly on the Care foundation, with significant weight also placed on Liberty understood as freedom from oppression. Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity are not entirely absent from liberal moral life — liberals can be fiercely loyal to their own communities and regard certain things as sacred — but these foundations carry far less weight than they do in conservative or libertarian moral matrices. The most sacred value in the modern American liberal tradition, the one whose violation triggers the strongest moral reaction, is care for those who are victimized or oppressed by existing arrangements. Challenging the victim status of a group that liberals have come to regard as oppressed, or displaying prejudice toward that group, reliably produces a sharp and unified tribal response. Two insights from this moral matrix are genuinely important for any society that hopes to function well over the long run.

Point 1: Governments Can and Should Restrain Corporate Superorganisms. Earlier chapters argued that large corporations are not merely analogous to superorganisms — they are superorganisms, in the strict biological sense: entities composed of many individual agents whose coordinated behavior gives rise to a collective that pursues its own survival and reproduction. Superorganisms, by their nature, expand when conditions permit, reshape their environments to favor their own reproduction, and push competitors to the margins. Left entirely to themselves, corporations tend to grow more politically powerful as they grow economically powerful, capturing the regulatory agencies and legislative bodies that are supposed to oversee them and bending rules in their favor.

Economists use the term "externalities" to describe costs that are generated by a transaction but borne by parties who did not choose to be part of it — pollution that a factory dumps into a river, health consequences that a food processor imposes on consumers, psychological suffering that a platform company inflicts on its users. Corporations are structurally incentivized to maximize returns to shareholders, which means they are structurally incentivized to find ways to transfer costs onto others whenever doing so is legal and invisible enough to avoid backlash. The only institution capable of representing those external parties — the public, future generations, non-human animals — is government. Markets function best not in a regulatory vacuum but within a framework of rules that force market participants to bear their own costs rather than exporting them to others.

None of this is an argument for reflexive hostility to business; that reflex is a liability for liberals, not a virtue. But it is an argument for keeping government capable of serving as a genuine counterweight to corporate power — which requires, among other things, keeping corporate money from dominating the political process that is supposed to regulate corporations. Liberals are right that this tension is permanent and structural, not a contingent problem that markets will eventually solve on their own.

Point 2: Some Problems Really Can Be Solved by Regulation. The history of leaded gasoline is one of the most instructive examples in modern public policy. From the 1930s onward, lead was added to gasoline to improve refining efficiency. By the early 1970s, American automobiles were releasing roughly 200,000 tons of lead per year into the air. Evidence mounted that this lead was entering human bodies and interfering with the neural development of children — lowering IQs and increasing vulnerability to behavioral disorders. The chemical industry blocked remediation for decades. When the ban finally came — pushed through over fierce industry opposition — blood lead levels in children fell in direct proportion to the decline in atmospheric lead. Researchers subsequently found that the phaseout may have been responsible for a significant portion of the dramatic and otherwise puzzling drop in violent crime that occurred throughout the 1990s. Millions of children, disproportionately poor and urban, had grown up with neurologically damaging levels of lead during the 1950s and 1960s; when that cohort reached adolescence and young adulthood, they contributed to the crime wave that terrified the country. The generation that grew up after the ban had better impulse control. One regulatory decision, fought for by people the other side dismissed as anti-market idealists, saved lives, raised IQ scores, and reduced crime — all simultaneously.

Lead is far from unique. Childhood exposure to polychlorinated biphenyls, certain pesticide compounds, and mercury emitted by coal-burning power plants all demonstrably impair brain development and increase the risk of behavioral disorders. The cheapest and most humane way to reduce crime may therefore not be to build more prisons but to give more resources and authority to environmental regulators. Conservatives are right that government intervention can have unintended consequences — but those consequences are sometimes positive and large. And when conservatives argue that markets outperform regulation, they owe an account of how markets, left to themselves, will eliminate the externalities that markets have every incentive to produce.

Yang #1: Libertarian Wisdom

Libertarians are sometimes described as fiscally conservative and socially liberal, but that framing obscures more than it reveals. Libertarians are the direct intellectual heirs of the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century reformers who fought to free individuals and markets from the control of kings, clergy, and aristocratic monopolies. Their sacred value is liberty itself — not as one concern among several, but as the overriding principle from which all other political conclusions follow.

In the late nineteenth century, liberals who shared this commitment to liberty split over a fundamental question: which institution posed the greater threat to freedom — the state or the corporation? Those who came to see powerful private corporations as the primary enemy of ordinary freedom took the progressive path, embracing government as the instrument through which liberty could be extended to those who had been exploited by industrial capitalism. Those who continued to regard government itself as the most dangerous threat to freedom became what Americans now call libertarians. The split is visible in the moral foundation profiles of the two groups. Both liberals and libertarians draw heavily on the Liberty foundation, but liberals weight Care much more heavily, and it is that Care sensitivity — feeling the suffering of those ground down by unconstrained markets — that moved progressives toward government as a remedy. Libertarians score very low on Care, even lower than conservatives, and extremely high on economic liberty. They are, in one formulation, liberals who love markets and lack bleeding hearts.

On the Moral Foundations Questionnaire, libertarians join liberals in scoring low on Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity — the binding foundations that conservatives weight heavily. Where they diverge most sharply from liberals is on Care (low) and economic liberty (very high). The practical effect is that libertarians and conservatives have been political allies since the 1930s, united by a common opponent in the progressive welfare state — even though their underlying moral psychologies are quite different, and even though libertarians often find social conservative positions on family and religion as objectionable as any liberal does.

Counterpoint 1: Markets Are Miraculous. When David Goldhill's father died from a hospital-acquired infection — a preventable death of the kind that kills roughly 100,000 Americans every year in a system that could reduce such deaths by two-thirds simply by following a standard sanitary checklist — Goldhill began investigating why the American health care system seemed immune to obvious improvements that would have led any ordinary business to bankruptcy. His conclusion was that health care had ceased to function as a market. Consumers were insulated from price signals; suppliers competed for reimbursement rates rather than for patient satisfaction; and the feedback loops that normally punish inefficiency and reward innovation had been severed.

Consider the miracle of a can of peas at a supermarket. The price on that can reflects the coordinated decisions of farmers, distributors, metalworkers, truckers, and retailers, each responding to price signals generated by millions of independent buyers and sellers. Competition at every link in that chain rewards whoever can shave a fraction of a cent off the cost of getting the can from field to shelf. No central planner designed this outcome; it emerged from the interaction of self-interested actors operating within a framework of property rights and enforceable contracts. The result is a can of peas for under a dollar. Now imagine that the price tag is removed and replaced by a food insurance system: you pay a flat fee at checkout, your insurer covers most of the rest, and you receive a bill weeks later. Immediately, the incentives that produce the cheap can of peas begin to dissolve. Suppliers no longer compete to satisfy consumers; they compete to maximize insurance reimbursements. Costs rise, quality stagnates, and innovation slows to a crawl. That, Goldhill argues, is what has happened to health care.

The force of the libertarian insight is that when people are shielded from bearing the costs of their own choices — when someone else always pays — the information-processing machinery of the market breaks down. The solution is not to convene panels of experts to set prices; it is to restore the conditions under which prices can do their job. Where those conditions can be established — as they have been, for example, in laser eye surgery, where the procedure is rarely covered by insurance and patients shop actively on both price and quality — costs fall and quality rises. The broader lesson is that liberals who invoke compassion as a reason to insulate people from market prices should reckon with the systemic damage that such insulation can inflict — on fiscal sustainability, on innovation, and ultimately on the availability and quality of the very services they hope to make universal.

There is a certain irony in the fact that liberals who readily accept Darwinian evolution — a process of undirected selection producing remarkably sophisticated adaptation — sometimes reject Adam Smith's account of how undirected market competition produces remarkably sophisticated economic coordination. Both are examples of complex order arising from decentralized processes without any guiding intelligence. The libertarian reminder that markets, when they function properly, are extraordinarily powerful tools for human welfare deserves to be taken seriously by anyone who cares about outcomes and not just intentions.

Yang #2: Social Conservative Wisdom

Conservatives are the party of stability and order in Mill's formulation — resistant to changes that the progressive tradition regards as obviously necessary. But describing them only as resistant to change misses what they are positively for. A better description is that conservatives are acutely sensitive to threats against the institutions and inherited practices that provide the moral infrastructure of social life. They do not oppose change as such — they are generally enthusiastic about technological innovation, for example — but they resist changes that they believe will degrade the structures that keep selfishness in check and make cooperation possible. Their most sacred value is not any specific institution or tradition but the health of the institutional fabric as a whole. As the historian Samuel Huntington observed, conservatism is not defined by which particular institutions it protects — those differ across countries and eras — but by its insistence that some institutional framework is always necessary and that the existing framework, whatever its flaws, is usually preferable to the chaos of its sudden dismantling.

Social conservatives, among all the groups studied, show the broadest moral foundation profiles — they weight all six foundations at relatively high and comparable levels. This breadth gives them access to moral intuitions that liberals, who weight Care and Liberty most heavily, often cannot perceive at all.

Counterpoint 2: You Cannot Help the Bees by Destroying the Hive. Liberals tend to be suspicious of exclusion in all its forms. From a Care-and-Liberty moral matrix, any boundary that keeps some people out looks like potential discrimination, and any hierarchy looks like potential oppression. John Lennon's "Imagine" captures the liberal ideal precisely: a world without countries, without religion, where nothing divides us and everyone is free to define themselves on their own terms. From within that vision, the exclusions maintained by nations and religious communities are simply irrational holdovers that a more enlightened world would discard.

Social conservatives believe this vision is a recipe for social dissolution. Throughout this book, the argument has been that large-scale human cooperation is an extraordinary achievement — one that required the co-evolution of moral psychology, religious practice, shared identity, and group loyalty over tens of thousands of years. Human beings are groupish creatures; we develop our virtues in communities, and those communities necessarily maintain some sense of shared identity and shared expectation that distinguishes members from non-members. Edmund Burke's observation that attachment to one's "little platoon" — one's immediate community — is the foundation on which broader civic and even cosmopolitan loyalty is built, was not sentimental nostalgia; it was a theory of how moral commitments actually develop in human beings. Adam Smith made a complementary point: people improve the things they can actually see and affect, which means that parochial concern is not the enemy of the general welfare but often its engine.

Robert Putnam's research provides systematic evidence for these intuitions. His studies of American communities found that tight networks of shared engagement — particularly through religious participation — make people measurably more cooperative, more generous, and more civically active. Enmeshing individuals into dense webs of mutual obligation reduces selfishness. But a subsequent study found that high levels of ethnic and residential diversity had the opposite effect: in diverse communities, people pulled back not just from those who were different from themselves but from everyone, including their own neighbors and co-religionists. Diversity, under certain conditions, produces anomie rather than cosmopolitan solidarity. Putnam called the phenomenon "hunkering down" — a retreat into isolation rather than an expansion of the moral circle. This finding does not argue for homogeneity as a policy goal; it argues for paying careful attention to the conditions under which diverse communities can build the bridging social capital that makes cooperation possible across difference, rather than assuming that removing existing group identities will automatically produce a more unified society.

The practical implication is that liberal reforms driven by concern for a specific group of victims can, if they weaken the institutions that maintain moral capital for everyone, harm even those they set out to help. Welfare programs designed to ease urban poverty in the 1960s, by changing the incentive structure around marriage and work, may have contributed to the breakdown of family structures in precisely the communities they targeted. Legal reforms that gave students stronger grounds to challenge teacher and school authority in the name of rights may have eroded the orderly environments in which disadvantaged students most need effective instruction. Immigration policies that emphasized ethnic distinctiveness over common identity may have increased rather than decreased intergroup suspicion. In each case, a genuine moral concern — for poor families, for student rights, for immigrant dignity — was pursued in ways that did not account for the costs to moral capital, and the results were mixed at best. The social conservative insight is not that these concerns were wrong but that the tools of social reform must be chosen with awareness of what they might destroy in the process of building.

Toward More Civil Politics

The yin-yang metaphor comes from a tradition that valued balance and complementarity. But the dominant metaphor in Western political life has often been more Manichaean — a cosmic battle between the forces of light and the forces of darkness, in which compromise is not wisdom but treason. The third-century Persian prophet Mani preached that the visible world is a battlefield between absolute good and absolute evil, and that every person must choose a side. That framework is useful for mobilizing armies; it is poisonous for governing a diverse society. American politics has grown measurably more Manichaean since the early 1990s, with predictable consequences: more acrimony, less capacity for the kind of negotiation and coalition-building that democratic governance requires.

The roots of this polarization are partly structural and difficult to reverse. The realignment of American political parties that followed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 gradually sorted liberals into the Democratic Party and conservatives into the Republican Party, eliminating the ideological overlap that had previously made cross-party cooperation natural. When both parties were internally diverse, legislators from the two sides shared caucuses, committees, and — crucially — social relationships. Once the parties became ideologically coherent, the human infrastructure of bipartisan friendship began to disappear. The shift was reinforced by a practice that began in the mid-1990s: incoming members of Congress were encouraged to leave their families at home and commute to Washington rather than move there. Before this change, the spouses and children of congressmen from both parties had attended the same schools, played on the same sports teams, and attended some of the same social events. Those personal connections softened political conflict. Their disappearance hardened it. Restoring them would not solve the problem on its own, but their absence is an example of the kind of small structural change that can cumulatively transform the emotional texture of political life.

Other structural factors also play a role. The way primary elections are run tends to reward candidates who appeal to the most ideologically committed voters rather than those who can build broad coalitions. The way electoral districts are drawn can entrench partisan advantage and reduce the number of genuinely competitive seats. The way campaigns are financed amplifies the voices of donors who tend to have more extreme preferences than the median voter. None of these factors is the sole driver of polarization, and none will be easy to change, but all are at least in principle subject to reform through legislation and institutional design. The problem also extends beyond Washington. Technology and residential sorting have made it easier for individuals to live almost entirely within communities of people who share their political views. In 1976, only a small fraction of Americans lived in counties that were political landslides — overwhelmingly one party or the other. By 2008, close to half did. Shopping choices, religious affiliations, dietary preferences, and voting patterns increasingly cluster together, such that where a person buys their groceries can be a reasonably reliable predictor of how they vote. This clustering is not inherently pathological, but it removes the ordinary social friction — the casual encounters with people who see the world differently — that keeps any single moral matrix from becoming totally self-enclosed.

Pledges to be more civil are unlikely to change any of this. Riders can sign pledges; elephants do not read them. Moral intuitions come first, and intuitions are changed not by resolutions but by experience — by actually encountering members of the other side as full human beings rather than as abstract representatives of an enemy camp. If you can sustain even a single genuinely friendly interaction with someone from the political group you find most threatening, your intuitions about that group begin to shift, and your capacity to engage with their arguments improves. You may still disagree — in fact, you almost certainly will — but the disagreement becomes less Manichaean and more honest. Understanding the six moral foundations, and training yourself to notice which foundations are driving a particular argument, gives you a cognitive tool for translating across moral matrices. It will not eliminate disagreement. But it can turn moral conflict from a war between incomprehensible enemies into a conversation between people who are each, in their own way, trying to protect something real.

In Sum

Political identity does not arise randomly or by passive absorption of ambient culture. People whose temperaments incline them toward novelty-seeking and low threat-sensitivity are predisposed — though not predestined — to develop life narratives and social environments that resonate with the liberal grand narrative of liberation and progress. People whose temperaments incline them in the opposite direction are similarly predisposed to resonate with conservative narratives of defense, restoration, and moral order. These temperamental differences, partly heritable, interact with childhood environments and life experiences to produce the characteristic adaptations and personal stories that ultimately translate into political identity. Once that identity forms, it becomes embedded in a moral matrix — a coherent world of shared values, trusted institutions, and sacred objects — that makes it genuinely difficult to perceive opposing views as anything other than error or wickedness.

Liberals face a particular version of this difficulty. Because their moral matrices are built primarily on Care and Liberty, they tend to be unable to see the Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity foundations as anything other than rationalizations for tribalism and oppression. This makes it harder for them to perceive moral capital — the institutional infrastructure that sustains cooperative communities — as something worth conserving alongside the pursuit of greater equality and freedom. Conservatives, in turn, are better at perceiving threats to moral capital but worse at perceiving the suffering of those who are excluded or exploited by existing arrangements. Libertarians are better than either at perceiving the systemic costs of government intervention but relatively blind to the suffering that unregulated markets can produce, and to the social goods that only institutions and collective action can provide. None of these orientations is adequate on its own. Liberals, libertarians, and social conservatives each perceive something real about human social life — something the others are systematically less equipped to see. A politics that can hold all three perspectives in productive tension will make better decisions than one dominated by any single moral matrix, because the disagreements between them, rather than being suppressed, will function as a self-correcting mechanism, catching the errors that each orientation tends to generate when it governs alone.

Morality binds and blinds. It binds us into communities capable of achieving things no individual could accomplish alone, and it blinds us to the perspectives of those outside our community. Understanding how it does both — tracing the path from genes to temperament, from temperament to narrative, from narrative to moral matrix — does not dissolve the binding or cure the blindness. But it creates the possibility of a more honest kind of disagreement: one in which each side can recognize that the other is not simply corrupt or deluded, but is responding, however imperfectly, to genuine features of the human condition that we neglect at our peril.
