Part II · There's More to Morality than Harm and Fairness

The Moral Foundations of Politics

For a long time, social scientists operated under the assumption that human beings were fundamentally self-interested calculators — a species nicknamed Homo economicus — who weigh every decision purely in terms of personal cost and benefit. Under that model, moral behavior is either a disguised form of selfishness or an outright mistake. But when you actually confront people with thought experiments that pit narrow self-interest against moral intuition, the self-interest model collapses almost immediately. People consistently treat certain actions as far worse than others even when the material consequences are identical — they recoil at degradation, betrayal, and cruelty in ways that no spreadsheet can capture. That gap between what economic theory predicts and what humans actually feel is precisely where the moral foundations live.

A Note on Innateness

Claiming that any aspect of human behavior is innate used to be a scientific minefield. The old standard demanded that an innate trait be hardwired, universal across every known culture, and completely impervious to experience. Under those strict criteria almost nothing qualified, and anyone who proposed otherwise was quickly silenced by an anthropologist pointing to a distant tribe that seemed to be an exception. That picture has changed dramatically. Neuroscience now tells us that the brain arrives in the world not as a blank slate but as what the neuroscientist Gary Marcus calls a "prewired" organ — one whose architecture is shaped by genes before birth but remains genuinely flexible throughout development. A better metaphor than wiring diagrams, Marcus suggests, is a book whose first draft is written in the womb. No chapter arrives complete at birth; many are little more than rough outlines that culture, family, and experience will fill in over the years. But none of those chapters — whether covering language, sexuality, food preferences, or morality — starts with entirely empty pages. Society can revise the draft, but it cannot write the whole thing from scratch. The most useful definition of innateness that follows from this is simple: nature provides a first draft, which experience then revises. "Built-in" means organized in advance of experience, not fixed forever.

The six moral foundations are Haidt's attempt to specify what that first draft looks like for our moral minds. But the theory does more than just name the foundations — it also tries to explain how the draft gets revised differently in different cultures and across the political spectrum, producing the dizzying variety of moral systems we observe around the world.

1. The Care/Harm Foundation

Reptiles invest almost nothing in their offspring once the eggs are laid. When mammals evolved, they adopted a radically different reproductive strategy: fewer offspring, but intensive care for each one. Primate mothers pushed this further still, and human mothers pushed it to an almost implausible extreme. A human infant's brain is so large relative to the mother's pelvis that babies must be born more than a year before they can walk, at which point they are still almost entirely helpless. Caring for such a needy creature demands enormous sustained effort — effort that a mother often cannot supply alone, which is why human childrearing has always been a cooperative project involving fathers, grandparents, and community members. Given how costly each child is, it would be astonishing if evolution had left mothers with no innate sensitivity to infant distress. In fact, it left no such gap: mothers who were wired to respond automatically to signals of need — crying, unusual stillness, signs of pain — raised more surviving children than those who were not. That automatic responsiveness is the evolutionary seed of the Care/Harm foundation.

The original trigger for Care was the suffering of one's own children, but the foundation expanded well beyond that narrow origin. The set of things that now activates it is far broader than what evolution strictly required. Baby-like proportions — large eyes, round heads, chubby cheeks — trip the same circuits, which is why people find stuffed animals and cartoon characters irresistible and why toy designers exploit those features deliberately. The foundation can also be triggered by the suffering of children who are not our own, and even by animals in distress, in ways that offer no fitness payoff whatsoever. Evolution only had to build the basic mechanism; culture, media, and politics then expand and redirect what sets it off. Political movements regularly compete to make their chosen victims the most salient current trigger of your Care foundation, whether the cause is famine relief, animal welfare, or veterans' care. Liberals and conservatives both invoke Care, but they tend to aim it at different targets — liberals more often toward universally defined victims, conservatives more often toward those who have sacrificed on behalf of the group.

Key insight: The Care/Harm foundation did not evolve so that you would care about strangers on the other side of the planet. It evolved so that you would care about the vulnerable infant in front of you. The fact that it now moves us to donate to disaster relief halfway around the world is a byproduct — a beautiful one, but still a byproduct. Understanding this helps explain why liberal universalism and conservative particularism are both drawing on the same underlying emotion, just aimed at different targets.

2. The Fairness/Cheating Foundation

Imagine a colleague offers to handle your entire workload for a week so you can extend a vacation. A perfectly self-interested creature would pocket the favor without a second thought — free time, no strings attached. But real people do not respond that way. Most of us feel an immediate sense of debt, accompanied by effusive gratitude and a sincere commitment to return the favor. That discomfort in the face of unrequited help is not learned behavior — it is the Fairness/Cheating foundation at work, a set of emotions shaped by millions of years of cooperative living among individuals who needed to distinguish reliable partners from exploiters.

The theoretical backbone of this foundation comes from the biologist Robert Trivers, who in the early 1970s introduced the concept of reciprocal altruism. Trivers showed that natural selection could produce genuinely generous behavior in a species where individuals remember past interactions and adjust their cooperation accordingly. You scratch my back today; I scratch yours tomorrow. The mathematics of this arrangement works only if individuals also carry strong emotional responses to cheating — anger, contempt, and a desire to exclude or punish those who take without giving back. Human social life is a continuous series of opportunities for mutually beneficial exchange: hunters pooling effort to take prey too large for any one of them, neighbors trading tools and watching each other's homes, coworkers covering shifts. For those exchanges to function, everyone needs a reliable inner alarm system that fires when someone tries to free-ride on the group's generosity.

The original triggers of the Fairness modules were concrete acts of cooperation or betrayal directed personally at the individual — someone who helps you genuinely, or someone who takes advantage of you. Over time, the foundation's reach has expanded enormously. On the political left, fairness concerns tend to focus on equality: powerful groups are seen as having rigged the system in their favor, accruing advantages they did not earn while failing to contribute their share. On the political right, fairness concerns tend to center on proportionality: people should receive rewards commensurate with their effort and contribution, and transfers from the productive to the non-productive feel like a form of cheating. Both sides are genuinely animated by fairness — they simply disagree about what fairness means.

This distinction is important. Fairness is not a single thing. The egalitarian version holds that all outcomes should be equal; the proportionality version holds that outcomes should track inputs. Liberals lean toward the first, conservatives toward the second, and their political arguments often sail past each other precisely because each side assumes its own version of fairness is self-evident.

3. The Loyalty/Betrayal Foundation

In the summer of 1954, the social psychologist Muzafer Sherif arranged for twenty-two groups of eleven-year-old boys to arrive at a summer camp in Oklahoma's Robbers Cave State Park, split into two groups that initially had no knowledge of each other. Even during the early days when each group believed it was alone, the boys spontaneously organized themselves into cohesive units with names — the Rattlers and the Eagles — along with leaders, norms, rituals, and territorial claims. They were not instructed to do any of this. Tribalism simply emerged on its own, like a reflex the boys could not suppress. When the two groups eventually discovered each other and were placed in competition, the results were dramatic: flags were made and desecrated, bunks were raided, insults were traded, and improvised weapons appeared. The counselors had to intervene repeatedly to prevent outright fighting.

What Sherif's study reveals is something about the deep structure of the male mind in particular — a readiness to form into coalitions, assign roles, mark territory, and defend the group against rivals. This is not a cultural artifact. Evidence from primatology and archaeology now suggests that organized intergroup conflict is ancient and not uniquely human: chimpanzees patrol borders, raid neighboring territories, and kill rival males. Our ancestors lived for millions of years in a world where the ability to form, maintain, and defend a loyal coalition was a matter of survival. The Loyalty/Betrayal foundation is the psychological legacy of that pressure — it makes us relish being part of a committed team and makes us feel visceral contempt toward those who defect or sell out.

The original trigger for loyalty modules is relatively narrow: signals that tell you who is a reliable team member and who is a traitor, especially under conditions of intergroup conflict. But because humans enjoy tribalism so deeply, we have exported this foundation into many domains that carry no survival stakes at all — sports fandom, school rivalry, brand loyalty. The hatred of traitors is often more intense than the hatred of enemies, a pattern visible across history and religion. Dante reserved the innermost circle of Hell for betrayers, not for murderers or heretics. The Koran commands the death of apostates while treating unbelievers with far more tolerance. Across political cultures, the left tends to be more universalist and skeptical of in-group loyalty as a virtue, while the right embraces it openly. This asymmetry makes it much easier for conservative politicians to build campaigns around loyalty themes — patriotism, national unity, standing by the troops — than it is for liberal politicians, who often find that same rhetoric leaves them cold.

4. The Authority/Subversion Foundation

Not long after returning from fieldwork in India, Haidt was speaking with a taxi driver — a recent immigrant from Jordan — who said he intended to take his family home rather than raise his son in the United States. His reason was not economics or climate or homesickness: he simply never wanted to live in a country where a son might say "fuck you" to his father and face no serious consequences. The story illustrates how dramatically cultures differ in what they demand from relationships between those who hold authority and those who are subject to it. Many languages encode these expectations grammatically, distinguishing formal from informal address based on relative status. English once did this through titles and naming conventions, and the social discomfort that arises when those conventions are violated — a stranger using your first name without invitation, or a revered elder suddenly asking to be addressed casually — is a small but real activation of the Authority/Subversion foundation.

The evolutionary roots of this foundation lie in the dominance hierarchies that characterize virtually all group-living primates. Among chimpanzees, knowing who ranks above whom and behaving accordingly is a survival skill. But the Authority foundation in humans is more than just submission to raw power. The primatologist Frans de Waal observed that even in chimpanzee groups, the alpha male plays a stabilizing role — mediating conflicts, suppressing violence, and providing a kind of rough justice that benefits the whole group. Among humans, this "control role" of legitimate authority is far more developed. The earliest written legal codes, like the Code of Hammurabi, frame the ruler's authority not as personal dominance but as a divine commission to protect the weak from the strong. Human authority, at its best, is inseparable from responsibility.

The anthropologist Alan Fiske described a mode of relating he called Authority Ranking, which is defined not by coercion but by mutual obligation: subordinates offer deference and respect, while superiors assume genuine pastoral responsibility for those below them. This is the relationship of a good parent to a child, or a traditional officer to the soldiers in his care. The Authority/Subversion foundation is built around exactly this kind of relationship. Its modules look in two directions simultaneously — upward toward legitimate superiors and downward toward dependents — and they make us sensitive to whether people are fulfilling the obligations that their rank entails. When someone in a hierarchy acts in ways that undermine the whole social order, most people feel the wrongness of it even if they are not personally affected. This is why disrespect toward institutions, traditions, and roles of legitimate leadership strikes many people not merely as rude but as genuinely immoral. The political right draws heavily on this foundation; the political left, which tends to define itself partly in terms of resistance to hierarchy, finds it harder to speak its language.

None of this means that hierarchy is always good or that authority is always legitimate. The foundation can obviously be exploited by those in power to justify oppression. But dismissing the Authority foundation entirely — treating every deference to tradition or rank as a symptom of false consciousness — leaves out something real about how moral order is maintained in human communities. Stable societies have always required some shared sense of who owes what to whom.

5. The Sanctity/Degradation Foundation

In 2001, a German man named Armin Meiwes posted an advertisement online seeking someone willing to be killed and eaten. He received hundreds of responses. One respondent, Bernd Brandes, followed through, and the two men filmed their encounter to document Brandes's consent. What followed was an act that most people find almost impossibly repellent — but Brandes was a consenting adult, no third party was harmed, and no deception was involved. When Meiwes eventually stood trial, the absence of a nonconsenting victim posed a serious legal and philosophical problem. For someone operating purely within John Stuart Mill's harm principle — the idea that the only legitimate basis for restricting a person's freedom is preventing harm to others — the Meiwes case is hard to condemn on rational grounds. Yet almost everyone does condemn it, and condemns it powerfully. That gap between the utilitarian verdict and the intuitive verdict is the Sanctity/Degradation foundation at work.

The foundation's evolutionary origins lie in a challenge that every omnivore faces. Unlike a koala, which is born knowing it should eat eucalyptus leaves, humans must figure out what is safe to consume — and the range of things we might potentially eat is enormous. The psychologist Paul Rozin called this the "omnivore's dilemma": the same flexibility that allows us to survive in almost any environment also exposes us to a vast range of toxins, parasites, and pathogens. Disgust evolved as a solution to this problem, a rapid and powerful signal to avoid things that are likely to be dangerous. The psychologist Mark Schaller has extended this insight into what he calls the "behavioral immune system": a set of cognitive mechanisms that trigger avoidance toward people, objects, or situations associated with contamination or disease. It is far more efficient to keep pathogens out of your body through social and behavioral barriers than to fight them once they are inside. Disgust is one of those barriers.

But disgust did not stay confined to food and physical contamination. Like all the other foundations, its current triggers have expanded far beyond its original evolutionary function. Across cultures and eras, the Sanctity foundation gets activated by symbolic as well as physical impurity — by things that seem to violate what is considered natural, holy, or essentially human. Liberals and conservatives differ significantly in how readily they reach for this foundation. Conservatives, particularly religious conservatives, are far more likely to speak of bodies as temples, of life as sacred, and of certain actions as defiling even when no measurable harm occurs. Liberals tend to be higher in what personality psychologists call "openness to experience," which in practice means greater comfort with novelty, less concern with contamination, and a preference for moral reasoning that focuses on conscious suffering rather than categorical taboos.

The Sanctity foundation is not merely a relic of pre-scientific squeamishness. It does real moral work. Without a sense of the sacred — of things that should not be violated regardless of the cost-benefit calculation — it is hard to explain why people treat certain principles as non-negotiable rather than as items for trade. Sacred values are the glue of moral communities: flags, shrines, founding documents, and martyrs create focal points that bind diverse individuals into a group willing to act collectively and, when necessary, make sacrifices. When something sacred is desecrated, the response is swift, emotional, and collective in a way that purely harm-based violations rarely produce. The Sanctity foundation makes extreme commitment possible; without it, communities tend to fragment under the pressure of individual self-interest.

The foundation also shapes some of the most contested debates in contemporary politics. On bioethical questions — cloning, stem cell research, assisted dying, abortion — the disagreement between left and right often has less to do with differing factual beliefs about consequences and more to do with whether the Sanctity foundation is engaged at all. If it is not engaged, these questions reduce to calculations about pain and benefit. If it is engaged, certain thresholds feel absolute regardless of any utilitarian argument. The philosopher Leon Kass, defending restrictions on cloning in 1997, put the point directly: our sense of repugnance at certain acts may be the most reliable signal we have that something important is at stake, even when we cannot articulate exactly what. "Shallow are the souls," he wrote, "that have forgotten how to shudder."

It would be a mistake, however, to assume the Sanctity foundation belongs exclusively to the religious right. Progressive environmentalism draws on it heavily when it treats industrialism not merely as physically polluting but as a kind of desecration of the natural world. New Age health culture invokes purity and contamination imagery that is structurally identical to religious notions of cleanliness. The foundation is universal; what varies is what gets treated as sacred and what counts as defilement.

Key insight: The Sanctity/Degradation foundation explains the persistent gap between two kinds of moral reasoning. Harm-based ethics asks: who gets hurt, and how much? Sanctity-based ethics asks: has something essential been violated, regardless of measurable harm? These are not the same question, and no amount of empirical evidence about pain levels will fully resolve a disagreement between someone who thinks the body is a temple and someone who thinks it is a tool. Recognizing this is the beginning of understanding why bioethical debates so rarely produce consensus.

In Sum

Each of the moral foundations described in this chapter traces back to a distinct evolutionary challenge that our ancestors faced repeatedly over millions of years. The Care/Harm foundation developed out of the extraordinary demands of mammalian parenting — particularly the long, costly, helpless infancy of human children. It makes us responsive to suffering and cruelty, and it drives us to protect the vulnerable. The Fairness/Cheating foundation emerged from the dynamics of reciprocal cooperation: humans who lived by mutual aid needed reliable ways to identify trustworthy partners and to punish those who free-rode on others' generosity. It gives us our sense of proportionality and our anger at exploitation. The Loyalty/Betrayal foundation is the psychological product of a long history of intergroup competition — coalitions that held together survived; those that fractured did not. It makes us prize team players and feel contempt for defectors. The Authority/Subversion foundation grew from the challenge of navigating social hierarchies in a way that maximized cooperation and minimized destructive conflict. It gives us our sense that legitimate rank entails real obligations, and that violations of proper social order are morally wrong, not merely inconvenient. The Sanctity/Degradation foundation began as a defense against contamination and pathogens, then expanded into a broader capacity to treat certain things, people, and principles as sacred — elevated beyond ordinary cost-benefit analysis — and to regard their violation as a form of defilement.

Moral Foundations Theory also carries a political implication that is both simple and consequential. When researchers ask liberals and conservatives which of these foundations matter morally, a consistent pattern emerges: liberals weight the Care and Fairness foundations most heavily, and they are skeptical of the others, often viewing Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity as sources of tribalism, oppression, and irrationality rather than genuine moral goods. Conservatives, by contrast, draw on all five foundations — and a sixth, Liberty/Oppression, which evolved as a response to bullying and domination — at roughly comparable levels. They see Loyalty as a real virtue, Authority as a legitimate source of social order, and Sanctity as a marker of things worth protecting absolutely.

This asymmetry has a direct political consequence. When a politician wants to move voters, he or she must activate at least one moral foundation. A party that draws on only two foundations is working with a narrower emotional palette than one that draws on all six. It is not that conservative morality is richer or truer than liberal morality — each set of foundations tracks something real about human social life. But it does mean that conservative politicians have more emotional registers available to them, which may help explain patterns in electoral outcomes that are otherwise puzzling. The righteous minds of liberals and conservatives are not malfunctioning versions of each other; they are genuinely different moral instruments, tuned to different frequencies, playing music that the other side sometimes cannot even hear.

If you want to understand why political arguments so rarely change minds, this is part of the answer. People on opposite sides of the political spectrum are not simply reasoning from different premises — they are responding to different moral signals, experiencing different intuitions as self-evidently obvious, and often literally failing to perceive the moral concerns that animate the other side. Moral Foundations Theory does not tell us who is right. But it does help explain why each side so consistently finds the other incomprehensible.
