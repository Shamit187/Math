Part II · There's More to Morality than Harm and Fairness

Taste Buds of the Righteous Mind

Imagine a restaurant that serves nothing but sweeteners — thirty-two varieties of sugar, honey, syrup, and artificial substitute, each presented in a small spoon. The owner, a biologist obsessed with the taste of sweetness, has concluded that because the sweet receptor triggers the strongest dopamine response of the five taste receptors on the tongue, it is the one receptor worth dedicating an entire dining experience to. Business, needless to say, is terrible. That scenario is fictional, but it captures how Haidt says he often feels when reading books about moral philosophy and psychology. Morality is vast and internally contradictory — a rich landscape of competing concerns. Thinkers like Richard Shweder embrace that complexity, offering pluralist frameworks that can account for moral diversity within and across cultures. Yet far too many moral theorists reduce everything to a single master value: usually some version of welfare maximization (help people, prevent harm) or justice (protect rights, honor autonomy). That gives us The Utilitarian Grill, serving sweetness and nothing else, and The Deontological Diner, serving salt and nothing else. Neither Shweder nor Haidt argues that all moral systems are equally valid, or that anything goes. But grounding all of morality in one principle — what Haidt calls moral monism — tends to produce theories that leave most people cold and societies that are vulnerable to cruelty, because they ignore the full range of human moral concerns.

The Taste Receptor Metaphor. The tongue has five types of taste receptor — sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and savory. Knowing that everyone has a sweetness receptor cannot tell you why one person loves Thai food while another prefers Mexican, or why no one stirs sugar into beer. To understand those differences, you need to examine the history of each culture and the food habits of each individual. The same logic applies to moral judgment. Knowing that everyone has a Care receptor cannot tell you why moral systems vary so dramatically across cultures and political identities. You have to look at the full set of moral receptors, trace the evolutionary pressures that shaped each one, and study how culture and individual experience calibrate them differently.

We humans all share the same five taste receptors, but we do not all want the same food. Explaining that gap requires more than knowing what the receptors are — it requires examining cultural histories and individual childhoods. The moral world works the same way. We all share the same foundational moral sensibilities, but the specific concerns that a given person acts on depend on the culture they were raised in and the particular experiences that shaped their moral intuitions. The Chinese philosopher Mencius made this connection over two thousand years ago when he wrote that moral principles please the mind the way that good meat pleases the mouth. In this chapter and the two that follow, Haidt develops that analogy into a full theory: the righteous mind is like a tongue with six distinct taste receptors. Morality is the cuisine — a cultural construction shaped by environment and historical accident, but not infinitely flexible. A cuisine cannot be built entirely from tree bark or from bitter tastes alone. It must satisfy the palate. Likewise, moral systems vary across cultures, but all of them must resonate with the same six underlying moral sensibilities that human minds are equipped with.

The Birth Of Moral Science

Today, secular culture tends to tell the story of the Enlightenment as a clean contest between reason and religion, with reason winning. But David Hume was fighting on three fronts, not two. Enlightenment thinkers agreed that divine revelation was not a valid source of moral knowledge, but they disagreed sharply about what should replace it. Some believed that morality was transcendent — that it emerged from the structure of rationality itself and could be worked out through pure logic, as Plato had suggested. Others, including Hume, believed that morality was part of human nature, like language or the capacity for taste, and therefore had to be studied through careful observation rather than deduced from first principles. Hume had a deep skepticism about the reach of reason, and he thought that philosophers who tried to derive moral truths without first looking at what human beings are actually like were making the same kind of mistake as theologians who looked for moral truth in scripture. Both groups were reaching past human experience toward something transcendent. Hume's own project was the opposite: to bring morality back down to earth and study it with the methods of natural science.

His first major work, A Treatise of Human Nature, carried the programmatic subtitle: Being an Attempt to Introduce the Experimental Method of Reasoning into Moral Subjects. Hume believed that moral science had to begin with an honest inquiry into human nature as it actually is — not as philosophers wished it to be. What he found, when he looked at people in history, in politics, and in daily life, was that sentiment and intuition are the real engines of moral life, while reasoning tends to be recruited after the fact to justify what we already feel. He also found not one moral virtue but many, and he rejected the fashion among some contemporaries of collapsing all of morality into a single principle like kindness, or replacing virtues with a short list of moral laws. Because he saw morality as rooted in a variety of sentiments — each producing pleasure when we witness virtue and discomfort when we witness vice — Hume often reached for sensory analogies, and especially the taste analogy: moral distinctions, he wrote, arise from mental taste in the same way that the distinctions between sweet and bitter arise from the particular functioning of a sense organ. Moral perception is a kind of sensing, not a kind of calculating. And because of that, Hume argued, moral science must begin by examining the receptors — not by deducing them from pure reason, and not by looking for them in sacred texts. When Hume died in 1776, he and the sentimentalist tradition had laid what Haidt regards as a solid foundation for moral science, one largely vindicated by modern psychology. But what happened next was not the rapid progress one might have expected.

In the decades following Hume's death, the rationalist strand of Enlightenment thought won the day. The moral sciences were hijacked and sent down a two-hundred-year detour — one that would make them less useful for understanding how people actually think and feel about right and wrong.

Attack Of The Systemizers

Autism is not a single, sharply defined disorder. Researchers typically describe it as a spectrum because people can be more or less autistic, and there is no clean boundary separating those with a diagnosable condition from those who are simply less skilled at reading other people's minds. At the most extreme end of the spectrum, autistic individuals are effectively mind-blind: they lack the social-cognitive machinery the rest of us use to infer what other people are feeling and intending. Simon Baron-Cohen, one of the leading autism researchers, proposes that all human beings can be placed along two independent dimensions: empathizing and systemizing. Empathizing is the drive to identify and share in the emotional states of other people. People high on this dimension tend to prefer fiction, enjoy conversations about others' inner lives, and pick up quickly on subtle emotional cues. Systemizing is the drive to find the underlying rules that govern how things work. People high on this dimension tend to be good at reading maps and instruction manuals, enjoy understanding how machines operate, and feel the pull of elegant formal structures. Baron-Cohen's insight is that autism is not simply a deficit — it is the result of a brain that is exceptionally low on empathizing and exceptionally high on systemizing. Rather than a disease with a clear on-off boundary, autism occupies a region of personality space defined by these two dimensions.

The two moral theories that came to dominate Western philosophy were both invented by men who sat at the extreme high end of the systemizing scale and rather far down the empathizing one. Understanding those theories — and their limitations — requires understanding those men.

Bentham And The Utilitarian Grill

Jeremy Bentham was born in England in 1748. He entered Oxford at twelve, trained as a lawyer, and spent his career trying to impose rational order on the tangled, self-contradictory mass of rules and punishments that had accumulated over centuries of English law. His most famous work, the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, proposes a single governing principle for all laws, all reforms, and all human choices: the principle of utility. An action is right or wrong, on this view, to the degree that it increases or decreases the happiness of whoever is affected by it. Each law should maximize the collective utility of the community, defined as the arithmetic sum of everyone's expected pleasures and pains.

Bentham then systematized the variables needed to calculate utility — the intensity, duration, and certainty of what he called "hedons" (pleasures) and "dolors" (pains) — and offered an algorithm he called the "felicific calculus" for reaching a moral verdict about any action, performed by any person, anywhere in the world. The degree of systemizing on display here is extraordinary. But Philip Lucas and Anne Sheeran, in an article examining Bentham's life against the diagnostic criteria for Asperger's syndrome, find that systemizing in the absence of empathizing comes at a cost. By most accounts, Bentham had almost no close relationships. He left a trail of estranged acquaintances throughout his adult life, never married, called himself a hermit, and seemed largely indifferent to the people around him. One contemporary wrote that Bentham regarded the people in his vicinity no more than the flies of a summer. John Stuart Mill, himself a utilitarian but one with a much richer emotional life, came to despise Bentham for what he saw as a fundamental incompleteness of mind — an inability to imagine perspectives and inner lives different from his own. Lucas and Sheeran conclude that had Bentham been alive today, he would very likely have received a diagnosis of Asperger's syndrome.

The irony is acute: the philosopher who designed the most systematic theory of human welfare may have been constitutionally limited in his ability to feel what other people's welfare actually means to them.

Kant And The Deontological Diner

Immanuel Kant was born in Prussia in 1724. He was familiar with Hume's work and, early in his career, was sympathetic to sentimentalist approaches to ethics. But he grew increasingly troubled by what he saw as the subjectivity they implied. If moral obligations depend on what people happen to feel, then moral truth shifts with the individual or the culture. Kant wanted something more solid — a morality that held for all rational beings regardless of their particular feelings or cultural background. To find it, he insisted that observation was the wrong tool. Looking around at what virtues different societies happened to practice would never yield the timeless moral law he was after. Only a priori reasoning — thinking that proceeds before and independent of experience — could reveal what morality truly requires. Kant believed he had found the bedrock principle, which he called the categorical imperative: act only on principles that you could consistently will to be universal laws. Rather than specifying any concrete moral content, this abstract logical test was supposed to generate, by itself, all valid moral obligations.

Bentham had told us to use arithmetic to determine right action. Kant told us to use logic. Both men achieved spectacular feats of systemization, distilling the whole of morality into a single sentence. Whether Kant also had Asperger's syndrome is less certain than it is for Bentham. Kant was a loner who never married, was famous for the rigid precision of his daily routine, and could be emotionally cold. But he was also widely liked by those who knew him and genuinely valued companionship, even if his socializing sometimes had a calculating quality — he regarded it as beneficial for his health. The safer conclusion, using Baron-Cohen's framework, is that Kant was one of history's most extraordinary systemizers while operating well below average on the empathizing dimension, without necessarily occupying the very bottom-right corner of the personality map that Bentham seems to have inhabited.

Getting Back On Track

Pointing out that utilitarianism and Kantian ethics were invented by men who may have had Asperger's syndrome is not an argument against those theories on the merits. That would be an ad hominem fallacy, and both traditions have generated enormous value in philosophy and public policy. But the goal of moral psychology is different from the goal of moral philosophy. Philosophers ask how people ought to reason about ethics. Psychologists ask how people actually do reason — or, more often, feel and react — about ethics. That question cannot be answered by math or formal logic. It can only be answered by observation, and observation tends to be sharper when the observer has well-developed empathy.

Philosophy, however, retreated from observation and empathy over the course of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, placing ever more weight on systematic reasoning. As Western societies became more educated, industrialized, and democratic, their intellectual classes became more analytic in their thinking style. Utilitarianism and deontology came to feel more rigorous, more respectable, than Hume's pluralist and sentimentalist approach. When Haidt encountered moral psychology in graduate school, it bore this mark: Lawrence Kohlberg had embraced Kantian rationalism and declared that moral development has one and only one endpoint — a full and principled understanding of justice. The whole framework felt, to Haidt, over-systematized and under-empathized. It was the sweetener restaurant all over again — a single-receptor morality presented as though it were the whole of the moral world.

Broadening The Palate

If harm and fairness are not the whole of morality, what else is there? Shweder's three ethics — autonomy, community, and divinity — offered a useful starting map, but Shweder, like most cultural anthropologists of his generation, was wary of evolutionary explanations. The dominant view in anthropology held that once our species developed the capacity for culture, biological evolution became largely irrelevant. Culture is powerful enough to override our ancient primate instincts. Haidt was convinced this view was wrong — that you cannot understand morality without understanding the evolutionary pressures that shaped the moral mind — but he also took seriously Shweder's warnings against naive evolutionary storytelling. Evolutionary explanations can be reductionist, ignoring the rich shared meanings that anthropologists document. They can also be too quick to assume that every behavior evolved to serve a specific adaptive function — the "just-so story" problem that Rudyard Kipling satirized. The challenge was to formulate an evolutionary account of moral intuition that was genuinely cautious, one that connected real evolutionary evidence to specific features of moral psychology without overreaching.

Haidt began by cataloguing virtue concepts from cultures around the world. Virtues are always shaped by local conditions — the virtues prized in a warrior society differ from those prized in a farming or trading one — but the catalogues also revealed overlapping themes. Almost everywhere, some version of kindness, fairness, and loyalty appears. That convergence raised a question: could there be a small set of pan-human moral sensibilities, analogous to taste receptors, that make certain types of social event particularly salient and morally charged across all cultures? The taste analogy helped here too. Many cultures have a sweet beverage — derived from local fruit, or in industrialized societies from refined sugar — but it would be silly to posit separate receptors for mango juice, apple cider, and Coca-Cola. One sweetness receptor underlies them all, and each culture has found its own ways to trigger it. If a culture has no sweet beverage, that does not mean its members lack the receptor; it just means their local conditions gave them little access to fruit. And when primatologists show that chimpanzees will work hard in a laboratory to earn a sip of soda, the case for an innate receptor grows stronger. Haidt's goal was to identify a small set of innate moral receptors and connect each one to well-established evolutionary theory — not by asking "can I invent a story about why this might be adaptive?" but by looking for the most obvious and well-supported links between the evolutionary record and the moral concerns that appear across human cultures.

His method was to find convergence between two fields he trusted deeply: cultural anthropology, which documents what moral concerns actually look like across societies, and evolutionary psychology, which explains why certain adaptive challenges would reliably leave their mark on the social minds of any species living in groups for long enough.

Moral Foundations Theory

Haidt joined forces with Craig Joseph, a friend from his time at the University of Chicago who had also worked with Shweder and had studied virtue concepts among Muslims in Egypt and the United States. Together they borrowed the concept of modularity from cognitive anthropologists Dan Sperber and Lawrence Hirschfeld. Modules are specialized cognitive systems that function like switches in the brain — dormant until a relevant pattern appears, then activated to produce a characteristic response. Many animals, for example, react with fear the very first time they encounter a snake, not because they learned to be afraid but because their brains contain circuitry that functions as a snake detector. As Sperber and Hirschfeld put it, an evolved cognitive module is an adaptation to a class of stimuli that recurrently mattered — presented problems or opportunities — in the ancestral environment of the species. Its job is to process a particular kind of input: snakes, faces, social threats, signs of contamination.

This framework was exactly what Haidt and Joseph needed to describe universal moral taste receptors. Such receptors would be adaptations to recurrent adaptive challenges in social life. They would orient attention toward certain classes of socially significant events — cruelty, unfairness, betrayal, disrespect — and trigger rapid intuitive reactions, sometimes accompanied by specific emotions like sympathy, anger, or disgust. Sperber and Hirschfeld distinguished between a module's original triggers — the ancestral patterns it was designed to detect — and its current triggers — all the things that happen to activate it today, including some that would never have existed in the ancestral environment. A snake-detector module fires not only for real snakes but also for coiled ropes and curved sticks spotted in the grass. Hover flies have evolved yellow and black stripes, exploiting the wasp-avoidance module of birds that would otherwise eat them. Cultural evolution, Haidt argues, works partly by adjusting the current triggers of moral modules. In the past fifty years, people in many Western societies have come to feel compassion in response to a wider range of animal suffering, while feeling disgust about a narrower range of sexual behaviors. These shifts happen within a single generation — far faster than genetic evolution could alter the underlying modules. Within any given culture, moral controversies often take the form of competing attempts to link a behavior to a particular module. Whether spanking a child is an act of cruelty (activating the Care/harm foundation) or an act of proper discipline (activating the Authority/subversion foundation) is not settled by biology alone; it is a cultural and political contest over which module gets hooked up to which behavior. So even though all humans share the same small set of moral modules, cultures can wire actions to modules in so many different combinations that they end up constructing very different moral matrices on the same underlying foundations.

Haidt and Joseph called their framework Moral Foundations Theory. They built it by identifying the adaptive challenges of social life that appear most prominently in evolutionary psychology, and then connecting each challenge to virtues that appear, in some recognizable form, across a wide range of human cultures.

The Six Moral Foundations. Moral Foundations Theory identifies six innate but culturally variable moral receptors, each evolved to meet a distinct adaptive challenge:

  • Care / Harm — evolved to protect vulnerable children; generates compassion, nurturing, and sensitivity to suffering.
  • Fairness / Cheating — evolved to enable cooperation with non-kin through reciprocity; generates concerns about justice, proportionality, and exploitation.
  • Loyalty / Betrayal — evolved to form and sustain coalitions; generates pride in group membership and contempt for traitors.
  • Authority / Subversion — evolved to navigate status hierarchies; generates respect for legitimate leaders and concern for order.
  • Sanctity / Degradation — evolved to avoid pathogens and contaminants; generates feelings of purity, sacredness, and disgust.
  • Liberty / Oppression — evolved to resist bullying and domination by powerful individuals; generates resentment of those who coerce others.

A political liberal is like a diner who has trained only the sweetness and salt receptors — Care and Fairness — and judges all cuisine by those two tastes alone. A conservative is like a diner who actively uses all six receptors, drawing on a richer and more complex moral palette. Neither is more sophisticated; they are literally tasting different things.

Five adaptive challenges stood out most clearly in the analysis: protecting vulnerable offspring, forming cooperative partnerships with non-relatives, building coalitions capable of competing with rival groups, navigating dominance hierarchies, and keeping oneself and one's family free from pathogens and parasites that spread rapidly in close-quartered communities.

If our ancestors faced these challenges for hundreds of thousands of years, natural selection would have favored individuals whose minds solved each problem quickly and intuitively — automatically — rather than relying on slow, effortful deliberation every time. The second row lists the original triggers: the specific patterns in the social world that each module was designed to detect. The fifth row lists the virtue concepts we use to describe people who reliably trigger a particular moral taste in others. The Care/harm foundation illustrates how the system works. Imagine watching your four-year-old son undergo surgery through a glass window. He has been given a general anesthetic and is unconscious when the surgeon's scalpel breaks his skin. Do you feel relief, knowing he is finally getting the procedure that will save his life? Or do you feel visceral pain and have to look away? For most people, the sight of a child being cut — even in a clearly beneficial surgical context — triggers an immediate emotional response that overrides conscious understanding. That response is exactly what you would predict from a module: we react emotionally to signs of harm or suffering directed at children, especially our own children, even when we know that the action is not really harmful. It works exactly like the Müller-Lyer illusion — you cannot stop seeing one line as longer than the other even after you have been told they are identical.

The Care foundation also helps explain why we respond differently to the two nurses attending the surgery. Suppose one nurse, older and more experienced, pauses now and then to stroke your son's head, as if offering comfort, even while knowing that a deeply anesthetized patient cannot feel or hear anything. The younger nurse is all focused efficiency. If you are a utilitarian, you have no rational basis for preferring the older nurse — her gesture produced no measurable improvement in your son's outcome. If you are a Kantian, you might even give her lower marks: she acted on feeling rather than on a principled maxim. But if you are a Humean, you see something else entirely. The older nurse has so thoroughly cultivated the virtue of care that she expresses it automatically, even when it is literally pointless. That automaticity, that effortless expression of a moral disposition, is what virtue looks like from a sentimentalist perspective. It tastes right.

In Sum

The second principle of moral psychology is: there is more to morality than harm and fairness. Chapter 6 begins to spell out exactly what more there is:

Morality resembles taste in deep ways — an analogy drawn independently by Hume and Mencius and supported by modern psychology. The dominant moral theories in Western philosophy, utilitarianism and Kantian deontology, are single-receptor moralities. They tend to appeal most strongly to people who are high on systemizing and low on empathizing, and they squeeze the full complexity of moral life into a single dimension.

Hume's pluralist, sentimentalist, and naturalistic approach to ethics is more faithful to how moral minds actually work, and it provides a better foundation for moral psychology. The next step — resuming Hume's project with the tools of modern science — is to identify all the taste receptors of the righteous mind. Modularity gives us a way to think about innate moral receptors and how each one generates a distinctive set of first impressions that culture then shapes and calibrates in different directions. Five strong candidates for these receptors are Care, Fairness, Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity — with Liberty/Oppression to be added in Chapter 8.

Theories in science are inexpensive — anyone can propose one. Progress happens when theories are tested against evidence and refined or discarded. The next step was to see whether Moral Foundations Theory could make sense of something deeply puzzling: why people on opposite sides of the political spectrum seem to inhabit entirely different moral worlds. That test would come next.
