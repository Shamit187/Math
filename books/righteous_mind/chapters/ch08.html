Part II · There's More to Morality than Harm and Fairness

The Conservative Advantage

In January 2005, Haidt was invited to speak to the Charlottesville Democratic Party about moral psychology. He had spent a good chunk of the previous year mentally drafting alternative speeches for John Kerry during evening walks with his dog. One of his objections to Kerry's acceptance address at the Democratic National Convention was that its rallying slogans barely touched any moral foundation at all. "America can do better" pointed at nothing emotionally meaningful, and "Help is on the way" offered only a faint whisper of Care — and then only if voters imagined themselves as helpless. Haidt's own mental rewrite tried something different: he imagined Kerry asking, after each broken Bush promise, "You gonna pay for that, George?" That kind of challenge would have framed the administration's spending spree as dishonest — activating the cheater-detection instinct buried in the Fairness foundation. The lesson Haidt delivered to the Charlottesville Democrats was blunt: Republicans grasp how moral psychology actually works; Democrats largely do not. The Republican Party had long understood that the elephant — the emotional, intuitive part of the mind — steers political behavior, and their messaging was crafted accordingly.

Republican strategists had demonstrated this understanding in stark terms with the 1988 Willie Horton advertisement, which hit voters squarely in the gut by linking the Democratic candidate Michael Dukakis to a violent crime committed by a furloughed prisoner. Democrats, by contrast, had often pitched their appeals to the deliberative rider, stacking their messages with policy details and projected benefits. Neither George H. W. Bush nor George W. Bush was a naturally gifted orator, yet both consistently defeated Democrats who were intellectually sharp but emotionally cool — Dukakis, Al Gore, and John Kerry. The one major exception on the Democratic side was Bill Clinton, whose personal warmth and near-musical emotional range let him connect with voters on a visceral level. Beyond fear-mongering, Republicans triggered the full breadth of moral intuitions described by Moral Foundations Theory. Like Democrats, they could appeal to Care — invoking innocent victims of bad policy — and to Fairness, particularly the perceived injustice of taxing hardworking people to subsidize the careless. But Republicans also held something close to a monopoly on Loyalty, especially through patriotism and military pride, and on Authority, through respect for parents, teachers, religious elders, and the police. When the party welcomed the Christian right during Reagan's 1980 campaign, it also inherited a potent vocabulary of Sanctity and sexuality that let it paint the Democratic Party as the party of moral dissolution. Against the backdrop of rising social disorder in the 1960s and 1970s, this five-foundation morality attracted broad appeal — including the famous "Reagan Democrats." The Democratic moral vision of that era, focused almost entirely on protecting victims and advancing equal rights, seemed thin by comparison. Democrats were offering two flavors — sweetness (Care) and salt (Fairness as equality) — while Republicans were playing every note on the moral keyboard.

That was the argument Haidt brought to the Charlottesville Democrats. He did not accuse Republicans of manipulation; he accused Democrats of psychological naivety. After two consecutive losses to George W. Bush, the audience was hungry enough for an explanation that most seemed willing to entertain his. At that moment, however, everything he said was still just informed speculation. He had not yet gathered any systematic data to confirm that conservatives actually draw on a broader set of moral foundations than liberals do.

Measuring Morals

The empirical test arrived in the form of a graduate student named Jesse Graham, who joined Haidt's lab at the University of Virginia around the same time. Graham brought an unusual combination of preparation to the project: undergraduate training at the University of Chicago, a master's degree from the Harvard Divinity School, and a year spent teaching English in Japan. For his first-year research project, he set out to create a questionnaire that could actually measure where someone fell on each of the five moral foundations. Working alongside Haidt and colleague Brian Nosek, he built the first version of the Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ). The instrument asked participants how relevant various considerations were to their moral judgments, using a scale from zero ("not at all relevant") to five ("extremely relevant"). Sample items included "whether or not someone was cruel" for the Care foundation, and "whether or not someone showed a lack of respect for authority" for the Authority foundation. Because Nosek ran one of the largest research websites on the internet — ProjectImplicit.org — the team was able to recruit over 1,600 participants within a single week.

When Graham plotted the results across the political spectrum — from people who identified as "very liberal" on one end to people who called themselves "very conservative" on the other — the pattern matched exactly what Haidt had predicted in his Charlottesville talk. The data provided the first concrete evidence for the foundations model.

Intuition: The Moral Taste Palette

Think of moral foundations as taste receptors on the tongue. Liberals have a palate strongly attuned to two flavors — Care and Fairness — and find the others mild at best. Conservatives taste all five flavors and find each one meaningful. This is not about which side is more "moral"; it is about which emotional registers each side treats as morally relevant. Republicans reach all five registers in their messaging; Democrats historically reach only two.

Figure

The Care and Fairness lines ran high across the entire spectrum — every respondent, regardless of political affiliation, rated compassion, cruelty, fairness, and injustice as morally important. Even so, both lines sloped gently downward as political identity moved from left to right, meaning liberals rated these concerns as slightly more central than conservatives did.

The real divergence appeared with Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity. Liberals largely dismissed these foundations, rating them far below Care and Fairness — so far below, in fact, that one could reasonably describe the liberal moral world as effectively a two-foundation system. Moving rightward along the political spectrum, however, those three lines climbed steadily. By the time respondents identified as "very conservative," all five lines had converged at roughly similar levels. Conservatives, in other words, treated all five foundations as equally legitimate moral concerns. Over the following year, the team refined the MFQ by adding items that asked participants to rate their agreement with statements designed to trigger each foundation directly — such as "One of the worst things a person can do is hurt a defenseless animal" for Care, or "It is more important to be a team player than to express oneself" for Loyalty. Jesse's original findings replicated cleanly across these new items and across samples from multiple countries. The team then built YourMorals.org — a public research website where visitors could take surveys, see how their moral profiles compared to others, and participate in ongoing studies. After a September 2007 New York Times article mentioned the site, 26,000 new visitors signed up within a single week. By 2011, the dataset had grown to over 130,000 participants, all showing the same basic pattern: Care and Fairness slope downward as political conservatism increases; Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity slope upward. Liberals weight the first two heavily and the last three lightly; conservatives weight all five roughly equally.

Figure

The pattern held up across entirely different kinds of measurement. In one study, respondents were shown a list of traits for a hypothetical dog breed and asked how each trait would affect their interest in owning one. Traits associated with gentleness and treating the owner as an equal appealed strongly to liberals — consistent with Care and egalitarian Fairness. Conservatives preferred traits associated with loyalty and obedience — consistent with the Loyalty and Authority foundations. (Cleanliness showed no partisan tilt at all; both sides liked clean dogs.) Jesse also obtained the texts of dozens of sermons delivered in Unitarian churches and dozens more from Southern Baptist congregations. Using a word-counting program, he identified hundreds of words conceptually tied to each foundation — for example, "suffer," "cruel," and "brutal" on the harm side of Care, and "obey," "duty," and "honor" on the positive side of Authority. The results confirmed what the surveys showed: Unitarian preachers drew heavily on Care and Fairness language, while Southern Baptist preachers made proportionally more use of Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity words.

Even brain waves reflected the difference. Teaming up with a social neuroscientist at UVA, the researchers had liberal and conservative students read sentences that either endorsed or rejected one of the moral foundations while wearing electroencephalogram (EEG) caps. Within the first half-second after a key word appeared on screen, liberal brains registered more surprise in response to sentences that dismissed Care or Fairness concerns, and also when sentences unexpectedly endorsed Loyalty, Authority, or Sanctity. Conservative brains showed the reverse pattern. These pre-conscious neural flickers are exactly the kind of intuitive elephant-lean that Haidt describes throughout the book. The rider comes afterward, constructing reasons to justify what the elephant already feels.

What Makes People Vote Republican?

When Barack Obama secured the Democratic presidential nomination in 2008, Haidt felt a flicker of optimism. Obama seemed to be a rare liberal who could speak across all five foundations. His book The Audacity of Hope showed genuine appreciation for conservative concerns about social order and the importance of tradition.

Early in the campaign, Obama gave a Father's Day speech at a black church praising marriage and urging men to take responsibility for their children. He gave a speech on patriotism that acknowledged the damage done by anti-war protesters in the 1960s who burned flags and snubbed returning veterans. But as the summer wore on, Haidt grew uneasy. Obama's address to a major civil rights organization leaned almost entirely on Care and Fairness, with fairness framed primarily as equality of outcomes. His famous Berlin speech cast him as a "citizen of the world," and he had already generated controversy by declining to wear an American flag pin — a gesture that struck liberals as trivial but that reinforced a growing conservative narrative that he could not be trusted to prioritize American interests. John McCain's campaign motto, "Country First," was a direct response, activating the Loyalty foundation that Obama seemed reluctant to touch. Alarmed that Obama might follow the path of Gore and Kerry, Haidt wrote an essay on the moral psychology of the race. The piece was published on Edge.org under the title "What Makes People Vote Republican?" He began by cataloguing the standard psychological explanations that academics had offered for conservatism over the decades: overly strict parenting, fear of novelty, existential anxiety, rigid black-and-white thinking. What united all of these explanations, Haidt pointed out, was that they treated conservatism as a pathology rather than as a legitimate moral perspective. He proposed a different starting point: assume conservatives are just as sincere as liberals, and then use Moral Foundations Theory to understand what each side actually cares about.

The essay's central insight was a contrast between two radically different ways of thinking about how societies hold together. The first is the vision associated with the philosopher John Stuart Mill: society as a voluntary contract among free and equal individuals. In this picture, the moral architecture rests almost entirely on preventing harm and protecting rights. People should be free to live as they choose, limited only by the requirement not to harm others. A Millian society at its best is open, tolerant, and creative — bound together by mutual respect and voluntary cooperation. This vision resonates deeply with liberals and libertarians. But it draws almost exclusively on Care and Fairness. If you assume those two foundations are all that matter, then Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity will look like mere superstitions — at best unnecessary, at worst dangerous.

The second vision is associated with the French sociologist Emile Durkheim. Rather than imagining society as a contract invented by individuals, Durkheim saw it as something that grew organically — a web of relationships, obligations, and shared meanings that formed over generations. The basic unit is not the individual but the family, embedded in larger institutions and traditions. People are shaped by the communities they are born into, and those communities must actively manage selfish impulses, punish those who violate norms, and preserve the social fabric through shared rituals and symbols. Without those binding forces, Durkheim warned, individuals drift into anomie — a kind of normless disconnection that leaves them purposeless and demoralized. A Durkheimian society at its best is stable and cohesive, with strong in-group loyalty, clear expectations of duty and self-discipline, and institutions that give individuals a sense of belonging to something larger than themselves. This vision requires all five moral foundations — not just Care and Fairness, but Loyalty, Authority, and Sanctity as well. Haidt argued that American liberals tend to see the Durkheimian world as a moral problem rather than a moral achievement. Its hierarchy, its religiosity, and its limits on personal autonomy look from a liberal vantage point like domination rather than order.

When your entire moral matrix rests on Care and Fairness, it becomes nearly impossible to hear the moral weight carried by America's unofficial national motto: E pluribus unum — from many, one. The work of converting a diverse collection of individuals into a coherent national community is a kind of miracle, and every successful nation performs it. When nations stop performing it, they fracture. Since the 1960s, Democrats have increasingly become the party of pluribus — celebrating diversity, supporting immigration without insisting on cultural assimilation, opposing official-language measures, and presenting themselves as citizens of the world. Republicans, by contrast, have positioned themselves as the stewards of unum. The president functions as a kind of high priest of what sociologist Robert Bellah called the American civil religion — invoking God, honoring founding texts, and weaving the many into the one. A party that is uncomfortable with that priestly role will struggle in presidential elections, because presidential campaigns are partly about national identity, not just policy. Haidt closed the essay by urging Democrats to stop treating conservatism as a defect to be explained away, and to start learning how to address a broader range of moral concerns in both their messaging and their actual policy thinking.

What I Had Missed

The essay drew a wide range of responses from readers who contacted Haidt directly.

On the left, many readers filtered the essay through their existing moral lens and emerged with the same conclusion they started with: conservatism is a character defect. One reader agreed with Haidt's diagnosis of the problem but added that Republican voters were also narcissists — incapable of compassion by temperament, not just by political choice. Responses from the right were generally warmer. Veterans, religious readers, and people with backgrounds in hierarchical institutions tended to recognize themselves accurately in Haidt's description of Durkheimian morality. One retired Coast Guard officer wrote to say that after moving into a civilian research office, he was struck by how much harder it was to accomplish anything collectively when there was no shared sense of loyalty and hierarchy — the very values Haidt had described. But a distinct group of economic conservatives pushed back hard. They felt Haidt had mischaracterized their moral views, particularly around fairness. Several sent angry emails making clear that their objection to Democratic economic policy had nothing to do with selfishness or indifference to others — it had to do with a deep conviction that people should get back what they put in. Hard work should be rewarded; dependence and irresponsibility should not be subsidized.

One reader put it with characteristic bluntness, accusing Haidt of being an overeducated philosopher out of touch with the real moral weight of earned reward and personal accountability. Another posted his own counter-list of reasons why people vote Democratic, and while the rhetoric was caustic, the underlying moral logic was clear: the list was saturated with concerns about people taking from the system without contributing — laziness, dependency, the avoidance of personal responsibility.

The central charge was that Democrats enable people who want something without earning it.

They shield individuals from the natural consequences of their own choices.

They undermine the basic moral expectation that adults should be responsible for themselves.

They celebrate a victim identity that excuses poor decisions and blames others for self-inflicted problems, while penalizing those who play by the rules and succeed through their own effort.

These emails were overflowing with moral conviction, yet Haidt found it difficult to fit that conviction neatly into his existing framework. The concern was clearly related to Fairness — but not the kind of fairness that runs on equality. It was the fairness of proportionality, of earned reward, of cosmic reciprocity: people should reap what they have sown.

This realization exposed a blind spot in the original Moral Foundations Questionnaire. The items designed to measure the Fairness foundation had been weighted toward questions about equal rights and equal treatment — the liberal version of fairness. As a result, the data consistently showed that liberals cared more about fairness, which infuriated economic conservatives who felt that they cared deeply about fairness — just a different kind. The original framework had lumped equality and proportionality together as two expressions of the same underlying foundation. But the angry emails made Haidt wonder whether they were actually distinct. Equality of outcomes follows a different emotional logic than the insistence that effort be rewarded and free-riding be punished. After follow-up studies at YourMorals.org, the team concluded that the desire for political equality was more closely tied to the psychology of resisting domination than to the psychology of reciprocal exchange. That finding led them to carve out a provisional sixth foundation — Liberty/oppression — and to reshape the Fairness foundation to emphasize proportionality more explicitly.

The Liberty/Oppression Foundation

A puzzle sits at the heart of human social life: we are a primate species with a strong inherited tendency toward dominance hierarchies, yet nomadic hunter-gatherer societies — which represent the vast majority of human prehistory — are strikingly egalitarian. Adult males share resources, particularly meat, and no single individual acts as a permanent chief or ruler. Archaeological evidence suggests our ancestors lived this way for hundreds of thousands of years.

Hierarchy only becomes common once groups begin farming and herding, which creates durable private property, larger populations, and the conditions under which a ruling class can accumulate and hold wealth across generations. Anthropologist Christopher Boehm spent years studying both tribal societies and chimpanzees, and he was struck by how closely humans and chimps share the same behavioral repertoire of dominance and submission. His conclusion was that human beings are innately inclined toward hierarchy — but that at some point during the past million years our ancestors developed the capacity to collectively suppress would-be alpha males. Even among chimpanzees, subordinates occasionally gang up to topple a dominant individual. The balance of power between the alpha and the larger coalition of excluded males is always fragile. Once early humans developed better weapons for hunting — spears, in particular, which the fossil record suggests became widespread around 500,000 years ago — physical strength alone could no longer guarantee dominance. Anyone could kill a bully with a spear. Add the uniquely human ability to use language for gossip, and you have a powerful tool for coordinating collective action against abusers of power.

Boehm argues that the first true moral communities emerged from this dynamic. Groups used shared gossip to identify norm-violators, particularly those who tried to dominate or take more than their share. When gossip alone was insufficient, they had the physical means to remove the threat — including lethal force when necessary. He cites a dramatic example from the !Kung people of the Kalahari Desert, where a man who had already killed three others was ambushed and shot by the entire group in a coordinated public act. Every member of the community, male and female, participated — symbolically distributing collective responsibility for the killing so that no single person bore the weight of it. The mechanism at work here is what Boehm calls a "reverse dominance hierarchy": instead of one individual dominating the many, the many cooperate to restrain any individual who tries to dominate. Boehm notes the irony that this arrangement echoes, in a rough way, Marx's idea of the dictatorship of the proletariat — though it emerged from evolutionary pressures rather than political theory. The result is a fragile political egalitarianism maintained not because human nature has changed, but because groups have developed the tools to rein in the tendencies that remain.

For groups that made this transition, the web of moral norms became considerably denser. Reputation management became crucial — those who cooperated skillfully and earned trust gained allies; those who bullied or defected were expelled or killed. Over time, Boehm suggests, this created a process of "self-domestication": just as selective breeding can produce calmer animals over generations, our ancestors effectively selected for individuals who could function within shared moral frameworks. The Liberty/oppression foundation, Haidt proposes, evolved out of exactly this pressure. Its original trigger was the sight of someone trying to dominate or bully — particularly the aggressive posturing of a would-be alpha. The emotional response it produces is a distinctive kind of righteous anger, sometimes called reactance, that fuses personal indignation with a powerful impulse to recruit others and resist together. People who failed to recognize and respond to these cues risked being subordinated and losing access to food, mates, and social standing.

The Liberty foundation stands in permanent tension with the Authority foundation. We recognize legitimate authority in certain contexts, but we remain vigilant for the moment when authority slides into self-aggrandizement or tyranny. Historically, this foundation has powered revolutions on both the left and right. The American Declaration of Independence is essentially a long catalog of tyrannical acts, culminating in a collective pledge of resistance. The French Revolution likewise fused liberty, equality, and fraternity because all three were needed to mobilize commoners against an entrenched ruling class. Virginia's state flag carries this logic in visual form: it depicts a woman — Virtue — standing over the body of a slain king, beneath the motto Sic semper tyrannis ("Thus always to tyrants") — the phrase attributed to Brutus at Caesar's assassination, and shouted by John Wilkes Booth after shooting Lincoln.

The Virginia state flag, with its image of virtue triumphant over a fallen tyrant, illustrates how the Liberty/oppression foundation can sanctify even violent acts of resistance when those acts are framed as the overthrow of illegitimate power.

The emotional logic of this foundation helps explain why revolutionaries so often feel that killing a tyrant is not merely justified but virtuous — a moral act, not a moral transgression. The current triggers of the foundation have expanded well beyond literal kings and bullies. On the American right, the foundation powers hostility toward government overreach: excessive taxation, regulatory intrusion, and international agreements that appear to constrain national sovereignty. On the left, it fuels hostility toward concentrated economic power. One liberal reader wrote to Haidt expressing the view that capitalism is inherently predatory, and that a moral society would be organized around mutual aid rather than market competition. Social justice rhetoric relies heavily on this foundation — the framing of economic inequality as domination, and of policy reform as a collective act of resistance. The key difference between left and right applications of this foundation appears to lie in scope. Liberals, who tend toward moral universalism and weight the Care foundation heavily, deploy Liberty/oppression on behalf of vulnerable groups anywhere in the world — racial minorities, undocumented immigrants, animals, children. This universalism leads them toward policies that prioritize equality of outcomes rather than equality of opportunity. Conservatives, who are more parochial in their moral concern — more focused on their own communities and nation — apply the same hatred of tyranny to different targets: an overreaching federal government, regulatory agencies, and international bodies that they see as infringing on individual and national sovereignty.

Figure

American conservatives, therefore, have made liberty — rather than equality — their sacred political value. That alignment connects them politically to libertarians. Jerry Falwell named his ultraconservative institution Liberty University precisely to capture this moral register. Many students there are pro-authority in the traditional sense — they favor intact families, religious observance, and deference to elders — but they are simultaneously opposed to what they see as the domination of secular government. The tension is not a contradiction; it reflects the way the Liberty and Authority foundations co-exist in the conservative moral matrix, each operating in its proper domain.

Fairness As Proportionality

The Tea Party, which burst onto the American political scene in early 2009, provides a case study in the Fairness-as-proportionality intuition. The movement's emotional spark is often traced to a live broadcast from the floor of the Chicago Mercantile Exchange in February 2009, when a business news correspondent named Rick Santelli erupted in fury at a proposed federal program to help distressed homeowners refinance their mortgages.

Santelli's core complaint was not that the government was spending money; it was that the government was rewarding people who had made bad decisions and broken their agreements, at the expense of those who had been responsible. He was not arguing for cruelty toward struggling homeowners. He was arguing for karma — for a world in which consequences track behavior.

His rant asked, in effect, why disciplined and prudent people should subsidize neighbors who had borrowed money they could not repay. He called for rewarding "people that could carry the water instead of drink the water" — a formulation that drew immediate cheers from traders on the floor. Many liberal commentators heard this as callous Social Darwinism. But Haidt argues it is better understood as a sincere moral intuition rooted in proportionality: outcomes should reflect contributions.

Haidt admits it took him a long time to understand this kind of fairness, because he had been approaching the concept through the lens of reciprocal altruism — the evolutionary logic that individuals benefit by exchanging favors with reliable partners. Under that model, fairness is primarily about detecting cheaters and maintaining tit-for-tat relationships. But recent work in evolutionary theory has complicated the picture. Several high-profile examples of reciprocal altruism in other species — including the famous claim about vampire bats sharing blood meals — have turned out to be better explained by kin selection than by genuine reciprocity. The experimental evidence for reciprocity among chimpanzees and capuchins is suggestive but not definitive. It appears that reliable reciprocal altruism requires a full moral community — one sustained by gossip and the threat of punishment — rather than simply high social intelligence.

Reciprocal altruism also struggles to explain why people punish cheaters at personal cost, especially in one-shot interactions where no future benefit is possible. A classic series of experiments by economists Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter illustrated this dramatically. Participants played multiple rounds of a public-goods game in which each player received tokens they could keep or invest in a shared pot. The pot was multiplied and redistributed equally, so full cooperation maximized everyone's returns — but individual self-interest favored contributing nothing and pocketing a share of others' contributions. As expected, contributions started moderately and declined steadily as players felt exploited by low-contributing partners. Then the experimenters introduced a new rule: players could now spend some of their own tokens to punish specific other players, even though the groups were reshuffled each round and no future interaction was possible. Economic logic says no rational actor should ever pay to punish a stranger they will never encounter again. Yet the majority of participants did exactly that — and cooperation shot up dramatically as a result, climbing steeply over the remaining rounds. Punishing free riders feels good. It feels just. People are not only motivated to avoid being cheated; they are motivated to see that cheaters are made to pay, even at personal cost.

This willingness to pay for punishment reveals something important: the desire for karma is not merely instrumental. It is intrinsic. We want the world to be arranged so that people get what they deserve — and we are willing to enforce that arrangement. Once Haidt's team began distinguishing Liberty/oppression as a separate foundation, they found that liberal egalitarianism in their survey data tracked concern about domination and victimhood — not a desire for reciprocity. This meant that the Fairness foundation, properly understood, is primarily about proportionality, not equality. Equality of outcomes is one special case of proportionality — it makes sense when everyone's contributions are equal. But when contributions diverge sharply, most people's intuitions demand that rewards diverge too. The Fairness foundation drives the desire to see free riders punished and diligent contributors rewarded. In a modern welfare state, those intuitions attach to the social safety net: people who rely on it for sustained periods — rather than as an emergency backstop — trigger the same cheater-detection circuitry that fires when someone defects in a public-goods game.

Reformulating the Fairness foundation around proportionality rather than equality resolves what had looked like a split personality in the original framework. It explains why conservatives tend to score higher on Fairness once the questions are rewritten to ask about proportionality rather than equal rights. And it explains why conservative rhetoric about personal responsibility, hard work, and earned reward is not simply a cover for selfishness — it is a genuine moral vision, one that takes karma seriously as a binding social principle.

Intuition: Mill vs. Durkheim

Imagine two architects asked to design a society. Mill's architect starts with the individual: give people maximum freedom, protect them from harm, and let them build what they wish. Durkheim's architect starts with the community: weave individuals into overlapping groups, enforce shared norms, honor tradition, and let the resulting structure provide purpose and belonging. Neither design is simply wrong. Mill's society produces openness and innovation but can struggle to bind pluribus into unum. Durkheim's society produces cohesion and belonging but can struggle with conformity and exclusion. Most people need both — and the tension between them is the central fault line of modern politics.

Three Versus Six

Putting all of this together, Moral Foundations Theory now identifies at least six psychological systems that underlie the world's diverse moral matrices. Left-leaning moral cultures tend to organize themselves around Care/harm and Liberty/oppression. Together, these two foundations support the ideal of social justice: compassion for those who suffer, and collective resistance to domination by powerful elites. Movements for racial equality, labor rights, and civil liberties all draw on this combination. The absence of a separate "equality" foundation in the framework is intentional: Haidt's data suggest that people do not crave equality for its own sake so much as they fight for it when they feel bullied or oppressed. The American Revolution, the French Revolution, and the cultural upheavals of the 1960s were all triggered by perceived domination, not by a free-floating desire for symmetry.

Figure

Everyone, across the political spectrum, endorses Care/harm to some degree — but liberals endorse it more strongly and consistently. Across dozens of studies and surveys, liberals are more disturbed than conservatives by depictions of suffering and violence. The same pattern holds for Liberty/oppression, with an important twist: both liberals and conservatives care about liberty, but they define the threat differently. Liberals are most alert to oppression by majorities or powerful institutions acting against vulnerable minorities, and they look to government as the remedy. Conservatives are most alert to oppression by government itself — the intrusion of bureaucratic or regulatory power into areas they regard as private or local. Small business owners, for example, vote Republican in large numbers partly because they experience government regulations as a form of domination. Libertarians align with conservatives on this axis, placing freedom from government interference above virtually all other values. The Fairness/cheating foundation — now understood as primarily about proportionality — generates strong feelings on both sides of the spectrum, but conservatives rely on it more heavily once it is disentangled from equality. Questions about whether hard work gets rewarded, whether everyone pulls their own weight, and whether the consequences of irresponsibility are allowed to land where they belong all tap this foundation more cleanly than questions about equal rights. The three remaining foundations — Loyalty/betrayal, Authority/subversion, and Sanctity/degradation — show the largest and most consistent political differences. Liberals treat these foundations with ambivalence at best. Social conservatives embrace all three as morally central. Libertarians largely ignore them, which is why libertarians often hold socially liberal positions on issues like same-sex marriage and drug policy even while holding conservative economic views.

Haidt opened this chapter by reporting an original finding: liberals have a two-foundation morality (Care and Fairness) while conservatives have a five-foundation morality. The research conducted since then has refined that picture.

The updated version: liberals actually draw on three foundations — Care, Liberty, and Fairness — though they are willing to deprioritize proportionality when it conflicts with compassion or with their anti-domination commitments. Conservatives draw on all six foundations, though they are more willing than liberals to allow some harm to occur in service of other moral objectives — maintaining group loyalty, preserving authority and tradition, or protecting what they regard as sacred.

In Sum

Moral psychology offers a more satisfying explanation than cynicism for why the Democratic Party has struggled in presidential elections since 1980. Republicans have an intuitive grasp of how moral psychology works: they aim their messages at the elephant rather than the rider, and they activate all six moral taste receptors. Democrats have historically aimed at the rider — emphasizing policy details, evidence, and the specific benefits their programs would deliver — while reaching only two or three foundations emotionally. The chapter contrasted the Millian and Durkheimian visions of society. The Millian vision — individual freedom, mutual respect, voluntary cooperation — resonates deeply with liberals but leaves the communal and binding aspects of human life largely unaddressed. The Durkheimian vision — organic community, shared obligations, hierarchy, tradition, and sacred symbols — captures what conservatives are trying to protect, but liberals tend to see it primarily as a system of domination rather than a source of genuine human goods. The result is a mismatch: Democrats pursue policies that celebrate pluralism at the expense of national unity, leaving themselves vulnerable to charges of prioritizing outsiders over insiders, the cosmopolitan over the local, and abstract rights over lived community. The chapter also summarized the two revisions to Moral Foundations Theory that emerged from this research.

The Liberty/oppression foundation accounts for the egalitarian instinct that both liberals and conservatives express, but in different directions. For liberals, it drives solidarity with the marginalized and oppressed everywhere. For libertarians and many conservatives, it drives resistance to government overreach and defense of individual autonomy against bureaucratic control.

The Fairness foundation was reoriented around proportionality rather than equality. It begins with the psychology of reciprocal exchange — rewarding cooperation and punishing defection — and expands into a communal concern with cheaters, slackers, and anyone who consumes collective resources without contributing to them. This is the moral logic of karma, and it motivates the impulse to punish free riders even at personal cost.

These revisions resolve one of the most politically charged puzzles of recent decades: why do working-class and rural voters so often support a party whose economic policies critics say work against their material interests? The standard liberal explanation is that they have been deceived — manipulated into voting against their wallets. But Moral Foundations Theory suggests a different answer: they are voting for their moral interests. A Democratic platform that speaks primarily to Care and social justice, while dismissing or misunderstanding Loyalty, Authority, Sanctity, and the proportionality version of Fairness, will feel alien — even offensive — to voters whose moral world is organized around all six foundations. Until Democrats genuinely understand the Durkheimian moral vision and the full scope of the six-foundation matrix, they will keep asking the wrong question about why people vote Republican.
