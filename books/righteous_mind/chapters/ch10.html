The Hive Switch

In September 1941, William McNeill was drafted into the U.S. Army and sent to basic training, where for months his unit did little besides march together in tight formation. At first McNeill dismissed the drilling as something the Army imposed simply to fill time — his base had no weapons to train with. But as the weeks passed and the men moved with increasing precision, something unexpected happened to him. The rhythmic, collective motion produced what he could only describe as a feeling of profound well-being, a strange sense that he was expanding beyond the boundaries of his own body — as though participation in the group ritual was temporarily dissolving the self and replacing it with something larger. McNeill went on to become a distinguished historian after the war, and his personal memory of drillfield marching became a scholarly puzzle he spent years trying to explain. His research led him to the view that close-order drilling and marching in step were not incidental to the success of Greek, Roman, and later European armies — they were the secret ingredient. He called the underlying mechanism "muscular bonding": the way synchronized bodily movement generates feelings of unity, suppresses individual self-interest, and transforms a collection of separate people into something closer to a single organism. This capacity, he argued, was one of the deepest and oldest technologies humans ever developed for winning group competition. Figure 10.1 shows perhaps the most famous product of muscular bonding — the Macedonian phalanx that Alexander the Great used to defeat armies far larger than his own.

McNeill pored over veterans' accounts of combat and found a pattern that ran against the conventional patriotic narrative: men in battle rarely risk death for abstract ideals or national allegiance. They risk it for the specific men beside them. The experience of shared danger and shared effort fuses individual identities together. Many veterans describe this merger not as a loss but as the most intensely meaningful experience of their lives — a moment when the ordinary anxious self gives way to something larger and more real. The boundary between "I" and "we" becomes porous, and with it the fear of personal death recedes, because what feels most alive is the collective. The soldier who falls does not feel he disappears — he flows forward into the group that continues without him.

The Hive Hypothesis

In the previous chapter, Haidt proposed that human nature is roughly ninety percent chimpanzee and ten percent honeybee. The chimpanzee portion reflects our deep primate heritage as intensely competitive individuals, each striving to outmaneuver neighbors in the social arena. This is why Haidt calls us Glauconians — creatures more naturally concerned with how virtuous we appear than with whether we actually are. But layered over that competitive foundation is a second, more recently evolved overlay: the groupish side. Like bees, we are ultrasocial animals whose psychology was partly shaped by the pressure of group competing against group across evolutionary time. Our ancestors who could suppress self-interest under the right circumstances and genuinely commit to collective goals left more descendants than those who could not.

The key word is "conditional." Humans are not unconditional team players who sacrifice for any group at any time. Rather, under certain circumstances — the right cues, the right context, the right emotional activation — we can flip into a mode where the self temporarily recedes and collective identity takes over. Haidt calls the mechanism responsible for this shift the hive switch. He argues that the hive switch cannot be explained by natural selection operating on individuals alone, because it confers no obvious advantage in within-group competition. The ability to lose yourself in collective purpose only makes sense as an adaptation shaped by selection between groups — it makes groups more cohesive, and cohesive groups outcompete fragmented ones. If the hive hypothesis is correct, its implications reach far beyond evolutionary theory. It would explain why war can feel exhilarating, why religion is so universally powerful, and why the most meaningful moments in many people's lives occur not alone but in the company of others pursuing shared goals.

Intuition: What Is the Hive Switch?

Think of the hive switch as a hidden gear in human psychology. Most of the time we operate as self-interested individuals — the chimp mode. But under specific conditions, that gear disengages and a different one takes hold: we stop calculating personal costs and benefits and instead feel ourselves to be expressions of something larger. The self does not disappear; it temporarily expands outward to encompass the group. Soldiers feel it in the field, athletes feel it in championship moments, singers feel it in a choir, and worshippers feel it in a packed sanctuary. The hive switch is not a metaphor — Haidt argues it is a real neurobiological system with identifiable components, shaped by millions of years of between-group competition.

Collective Emotions

When European explorers fanned out across the globe beginning in the late fifteenth century, they encountered dazzling variety in the animal and plant kingdoms. But their observations of human behavior told a surprisingly consistent story. Wherever they traveled — Africa, the Americas, the Pacific — they found communities gathering around fires to dance together, bodies moving in unison to percussion, often continuing until exhaustion. The reactions of these European observers were almost uniformly negative. The dancers, with their body paint and rhythmic abandon, looked to European eyes like animals surrendering to instinct. What the explorers could not recognize, filtered as they were through a culture that had progressively suppressed this kind of communal expression, was that they were witnessing one of humanity's most ancient and widespread social technologies.

The historian Barbara Ehrenreich argues in Dancing in the Streets that ecstatic group dancing is essentially a universal biotechnology for social binding. Like McNeill, she sees synchronized movement as a form of muscular bonding — it cultivates trust, dissolves social hierarchies, and generates powerful feelings of love and solidarity. This practice was central to ancient Greek religion and was present in early Christianity, only to be systematically suppressed by Church authorities during the Middle Ages. Why did Europeans diverge from this near-universal pattern? Ehrenreich traces a long historical arc, but the pivot point she identifies is the rise of individualism beginning in the sixteenth century. The Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution accelerated the process, producing the hyper-individualist Western culture that now pervades the modern world. The WEIRDer a person is — Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic — the harder it becomes to perceive the world through the lens of relationship and collective identity rather than through the lens of separate, autonomous individuals. What looks like savage excess to the WEIRD observer is, from another angle, a profound act of social bonding.

Ehrenreich noted with some frustration that psychology offered her little help in understanding collective joy. The discipline has a rich vocabulary for describing emotions between pairs of individuals, from brief attraction to consuming love to dangerous obsession. But it has almost nothing to say about the emotions that can exist among dozens of people simultaneously — what she calls, borrowing a phrase, the love that has no name at all. The most useful scholar she found was the French sociologist Emile Durkheim. Durkheim insisted that social facts — patterns that emerge from human interaction, like suicide rates or patriotic sentiment — are real phenomena that cannot be reduced to the psychology of individuals. He criticized contemporaries like Freud for trying to explain religion and morality through individual psychology alone. His alternative was the concept of Homo duplex: the idea that human beings live on two distinct levels at once, the individual and the collective, and that we have evolved distinct emotional capacities for each.

The emotions that bind us to specific other people — affection, respect, fear, gratitude — are easily explained as products of individual selection. But Durkheim noticed a second class of emotions that behave differently: emotions that bind us not to persons but to the group as a whole, emotions that are activated most powerfully when the group faces another group. Under the influence of these second-level sentiments, the self does not merely connect to others — it submerges. The individual becomes a part of the collective, following its current and subject to its pull. The most important of these Durkheimian emotions is collective effervescence — the electric charge of excitement and self-transcendence that group rituals can generate. Durkheim described it as a kind of social electricity: when people assemble together, their proximity amplifies their energy until they reach a state of heightened exaltation that no individual could reach alone. In such a state, the ordinary concerns of daily life — money, reputation, health, status — fall away, and the group crosses temporarily into what Durkheim called the realm of the sacred. This crossing, he believed, is the source of our ideas about gods and spirits, about moral order, and about the very distinction between the holy and the mundane.

Intuition: Collective Effervescence

Durkheim's term "collective effervescence" sounds abstract, but the experience it describes is entirely familiar. Think of the moment when a stadium full of strangers erupts together as a last-second goal goes in, or the hush that falls over a congregation during a particularly moving passage of music, or the electric shared silence of a crowd watching fireworks. These moments are not just the sum of individual emotional reactions happening in parallel — something genuinely different is occurring at the group level. Durkheim argued that this collective emotional electricity is as real as any individual feeling, and that it is the engine behind religious experience, political movements, and the sense that some moments in life carry a weight that ordinary time simply does not. The hive switch is the neurobiological mechanism that makes such effervescence possible.

So Many Ways To Flip The Switch

Collective effervescence, it turns out, does not require a bonfire and a crowd. The hive switch can be triggered in many different ways, some social, some solitary, some entirely unexpected. Three in particular illustrate how wide the range of triggers really is.

1. Awe in Nature. In the 1830s, Ralph Waldo Emerson delivered a series of lectures that launched American Transcendentalism — a revolt against the dry rationalism of the era's elite universities. Emerson argued that the deepest truths are not reasoned but felt, and that the natural world is the most reliable portal to such feeling. Standing on open ground under a night sky, or looking out across rolling hills, he described a sensation of the ordinary self dissolving — all petty ego simply evaporating — and being replaced by a feeling of unmediated participation in the life of the universe. Charles Darwin recorded a nearly identical experience in Brazil, overcome by the grandeur of the forest with a conviction that the human spirit was something vastly larger than mere biology. Both Emerson and Darwin, Haidt notes, had stumbled across the same phenomenon Durkheim was trying to theorize: the hive switch can be flipped by the right encounter with the natural world, even without any other people present. The emotion of awe does the work. Awe arises when we confront something so vast that it cannot be absorbed into our existing mental categories — we must change our frameworks to accommodate what we have experienced. This cognitive disruption temporarily quiets the self-focused mind, creating an opening into something larger. Mystics and ascetics across cultures have known about this mechanism for millennia; modern psychology is only beginning to catch up.

2. Durkheimogens. When Hernando Cortés arrived in Mexico in 1519, he found the Aztecs using mushrooms containing psilocybin as a religious sacrament — the flesh of a god made edible, capable of transporting the user from the ordinary world to the sacred in the span of half an hour. Farther north, indigenous communities built their spiritual practices around peyote; farther south, around ayahuasca. What all these substances share, along with synthetic compounds like LSD, is the ability to suppress the normal boundaries of the self and generate experiences that users consistently describe in religious terms: unity with the cosmos, the dissolution of ordinary time and space, a felt sense of profound truth. Haidt proposes calling these substances "Durkheimogens" — compounds that pharmacologically produce the state Durkheim described sociologically. They flip the hive switch through brain chemistry rather than through collective movement. Their traditional use was almost always embedded in ritual: initiation rites in which a young person undergoes a symbolic death and rebirth, emerging from the experience as a morally responsible adult committed to the community rather than to childhood self-interest. One anthropological review concludes that such rites were designed specifically to create group bonding and to shift the newly initiated person from individual to collective orientation.

When Westerners take psilocybin outside of any ritual container, the experience is still remarkably consistent. In a landmark experiment conducted before such substances became illegal, twenty divinity students gathered in a church basement in Boston. Half received psilocybin; the other half received niacin, a B vitamin that produces a noticeable but non-psychedelic warmth. Within an hour the difference was unmistakable. The researchers collected reports immediately after the session and again six months later. The psilocybin group consistently reported nine distinct qualities of experience: a sense of complete unity and loss of individual self; transcendence of ordinary time and space; deep and sustained positive mood; a feeling of sacredness; intuitive knowledge that felt absolutely authoritative; paradoxical qualities that defied normal logic; difficulty putting the experience into words; the recognition that the experience was temporary; and lastly, lasting positive changes in attitude and behavior that persisted long after the drug had cleared. A follow-up study conducted twenty-five years later found that every single psilocybin participant still regarded the experience as having been genuinely mystical and as one of the most spiritually significant events of their lives.

3. Raves. Rock music has always carried an undercurrent of wildness and individual desire. But in the 1980s a different kind of music culture emerged in Britain, one that replaced rock's individualism with something more communal and ecstatic. New electronic music genres — techno, trance, house — combined with advances in laser and lighting technology and the widespread availability of MDMA, a drug that produces sustained energy alongside intense feelings of openness and love. The result was the rave: an all-night dance event in which thousands of people moved together to hypnotic repetitive beats in a darkened space filled with light. Tony Hsieh, who would later found Zappos, encountered his first rave in San Francisco and found that it permanently altered his sense of what was possible in human experience. He described a feeling of awe that had nothing to do with the visual spectacle. Despite being, by his own description, a person of relentless logic and rationality, he was overwhelmed by a sense of connection — not just to the people in the room but to something larger and less definable. The crowd stopped feeling like individuals in proximity and became, in his experience, a single unified entity, moving together, sharing a heartbeat. He described individual consciousness dissolving into group consciousness.

What Hsieh experienced that night, Haidt argues, was muscular bonding in a thoroughly modern form — the same mechanism McNeill and Ehrenreich had identified in ancient armies and indigenous rituals, now activated by electronic music and pharmacology. The switch had been flipped, the self had been dissolved, and the result was not frightening but profoundly meaningful. That experience shaped Hsieh's vision for what a corporation could be, pushing him toward building a company culture grounded in genuine community rather than transactional self-interest. Beyond raves, Haidt notes that his students at the University of Virginia report the hive switch being activated by choral singing, marching band performance, meditation, political rallies, and sermons. The specific trigger varies widely; what is constant is the result — a temporary loosening of the individual self and a felt sense of belonging to something larger. Most people have experienced this at least once. It is, Haidt argues, one of the most distinctively human capacities we have.

Recognizing the hive switch changes how Haidt sees his students. He still sees them competing for grades, status, and romantic partners — the chimpanzee level of Homo duplex, ceaseless and familiar. But he now also sees them searching for something that competition cannot provide: the experience of genuine belonging, of mattering to a cause, of being part of something that transcends them. The intensity with which they throw themselves into theater productions, sports teams, volunteer organizations, and political movements is not separate from the moral psychology the book has been tracing — it is an expression of the same deep human need to be embedded in a group, to have a role in a shared story, to find a calling that can only be found in company with others.

The Biology Of The Hive Switch

If the hive switch is a real biological adaptation — something shaped by group-level selection to make groups more cohesive — then it must have a physical substrate. It will not be a single brain region unique to humans. Evolution rarely builds new structures from scratch; it repurposes existing circuits and molecules to accomplish novel things. The hive switch, Haidt proposes, is a functional system assembled from at least two well-studied biological components: oxytocin and mirror neurons.

Oxytocin is a hormone and neurotransmitter produced in the hypothalamus, long known for its role in maternal bonding — it drives uterine contractions, milk letdown, and the overwhelming motivation in mothers to hold and care for their newborns. Evolution has repeatedly reused oxytocin across species to build other kinds of bonds. In pair-bonding animals, male brains have been modified to be more sensitive to oxytocin's effects. In humans, the hormone reaches far beyond family attachment. Experimentally delivered oxytocin makes people more trusting in financial games with anonymous partners. Behaving trustingly causes oxytocin levels to rise in the person who was trusted. Watching others suffer raises oxytocin in people who report feeling empathy. Even a back rub from a stranger elevates levels. The press has understandably swooned over oxytocin, christening it the "love drug" or the "cuddle hormone." But if it really made us love everyone equally, it would be an extraordinarily strange product of evolution. And it does not.

If the hive switch evolved through group selection, it should exhibit the signature feature of group-selected traits: it should bind us tightly to our own group while leaving us relatively indifferent to outsiders. That is exactly what oxytocin does. In a set of studies using Dutch participants, men given oxytocin nasal spray became more generous toward their own team but showed no increased concern for members of competing teams. In some conditions, oxytocin actually made them more willing to harm the other team when doing so protected their own group. Related studies found that oxytocin led Dutch men to show stronger preference for Dutch names and to place higher value on Dutch lives in trolley-problem scenarios. Notably, this increased in-group favoritism was not paired with greater hostility toward outgroups — oxytocin did not make people hate foreigners more. It simply amplified parochial love. The researchers concluded that the oxytocin system evolved specifically to coordinate cooperation within groups, not to promote universal human solidarity. The second biological candidate for the hive switch is the mirror neuron system. Mirror neurons were discovered in the 1980s by Italian researchers placing microelectrodes in individual neurons in macaque monkeys' motor cortex. They found cells that fired not only when the monkey performed a specific action — say, pinching a nut between thumb and forefinger — but also when the monkey watched a researcher perform the same action. These neurons did not distinguish between doing and observing. The monkey's brain was mirroring the actions of others using the very circuits it would use to perform those actions itself.

Later research refined the picture. Most mirror neurons respond not to specific physical movements but to the underlying intentions those movements express. The same hand reaching for a cup triggers different neurons depending on whether the context suggests drinking or tidying up. These circuits appear designed to read and predict the goals of others, which is a prerequisite for the kind of shared intentionality that makes human cooperation possible. In macaques, the mirror system seems to serve each monkey's individual needs — learning and prediction. But in humans, the mirror neuron system connects much more directly to emotional brain regions, particularly the insular cortex and the amygdala. The result is that humans do not merely predict the actions of others — they feel what others feel to an extent unmatched among other primates. When someone smiles near you, some of the same neurons active when you smile yourself become active in your brain. Their emotional state propagates into yours, and yours propagates back, creating a kind of emotional resonance that ripples through groups. This is the neurological mechanism behind Durkheim's collective sentiments and the emotional electricity of collective effervescence. Neuroscientist Tania Singer demonstrated the selective nature of this empathy circuit in a revealing experiment. Subjects played an economic game with two strangers — one who played cooperatively, one who played selfishly. When the subjects' brains were later scanned while mild shocks were delivered to each of the players' hands, the results were asymmetric. Shocks to the cooperative player's hand activated the same pain circuits in the subject that fired when the subject themselves was shocked. But shocks to the selfish player's hand produced far less empathy, and in some cases measurable neural signals of pleasure. We do not mirror everyone equally. The hive switch is conditional: it opens toward those who have behaved in accordance with our moral standards and closes toward those who have violated them.

Hives At Work

The word "corporation" derives from the Latin corpus — body. A corporation is, in the most literal sense, a superorganism: a legal fiction that treats a collection of individuals as a single entity capable of acting, owning, and persisting through time independently of any particular member. This seemingly abstract legal invention turned out to be one of the most consequential institutional technologies in human history. It allowed people to pool labor, suppress free-riding through institutional rules, and pursue projects of a scale no individual or informal alliance could sustain. The result was a new kind of human hive — one governed not by genetics but by law and shared purpose.

The corporation's success drove the industrial revolution in England and spread from there to dominate the global economy. But not all corporations are equally effective at harnessing human nature. A company can be staffed entirely by the kind of self-interested actors economists traditionally assume — people who work hard only when they are monitored and rewarded, and slack off the moment incentives are misaligned. This approach, sometimes called transactional leadership, works to a point. But it is expensive to administer, and it leaves untapped the deepest motivational reserves of the people it employs. The alternative is to engage the groupish overlay — the hive side of human nature. Organizations that successfully do this generate something economists call social capital: the dense web of trust, loyalty, and mutual commitment that allows groups to accomplish more with less friction than the market can otherwise explain. Employees in genuinely hivish organizations work harder, enjoy their work more, stay longer, and cooperate more freely. They are team players in the fullest sense, not because they have calculated that cooperation serves their self-interest in this particular instance, but because they have temporarily transcended the calculus of self-interest altogether.

How does a leader build such an organization? Haidt draws on research by Robert Hogan, Robert Kaiser, and Mark van Vugt, who argue that leadership can only be understood as the complement of followership — studying leaders in isolation is like trying to understand applause by watching only one hand. The more interesting puzzle is not why people want to lead but why people are willing to follow. Humans evolved in small, relatively egalitarian groups with strong instincts against domination. We follow readily only when two conditions are met: we perceive a genuine collective need, and the person who steps forward does not trigger our finely tuned sensitivity to bullying and exploitation. Effective organizational leadership must therefore engage three moral foundations simultaneously: Authority, to give the leader's direction legitimacy; Liberty, to ensure that people do not feel subjugated; and above all, Loyalty, which is the foundation most directly tied to group cohesion. Concretely, Haidt argues that organizations can nudge people toward hivishness through a handful of practical mechanisms. First, emphasize shared identity over demographic differences. Research consistently shows that people are warmer and more cooperative with those who seem similar to them — and similarity can be constructed from shared values, common goals, and mutual dependence, not just demographics. Second, build in synchrony. Groups that move together — in exercise, in chants, in rehearsed rituals, even in shared dancing or karaoke — report higher levels of trust and willingness to sacrifice for the group. Japanese manufacturing companies have long used morning exercises for exactly this reason. Third, create intergroup competition rather than individual competition. Pitting individuals against each other for scarce bonuses destroys social capital. Friendly rivalry between teams amplifies in-group love without generating proportional out-group hatred, producing a net gain in cohesion and morale. Transformational leaders, as the research literature summarizes, change the way followers understand themselves — from isolated individuals to members of something worth belonging to.

Research in social psychology makes clear that perceived similarity — regardless of what the similarity is actually about — increases warmth, trust, and cooperation. Race is one dimension of perceived similarity, but it is not a privileged one. It can be drowned out by other dimensions: shared goals, shared values, shared experiences, and shared identity. Leaders who want diverse teams to function like hives need not pretend that differences do not exist; they need to build a sea of similarities strong enough that the differences become less salient than the commonalities.

Synchrony deserves particular emphasis. When people move in coordinated ways — marching, singing, dancing, even tapping a table in rhythm together — they signal shared intentionality at a bodily level. The signal is not symbolic or verbal; it bypasses the analytic mind and speaks directly to the social brain. Studies find that people who have performed synchronous tasks together are more trusting and more willing to help each other afterward. The effect shows up in domains from sports preparation to religious ritual to military drill. Intergroup competition works through a different channel. Soldiers throughout history have fought not for abstract national ideals but for the specific men in their unit. Research confirms that intergroup competition dramatically increases in-group affiliation while producing only modest increases in out-group hostility — the net effect on social capital is strongly positive as long as the competition does not become destructive.

The summary from Kaiser and Hogan captures the core distinction well: transactional leadership works through self-interest, offering incentives for individual compliance. Transformational leadership works by changing how people see themselves — expanding their identity from "I" to "we." Transformational leaders model collective commitment, use inclusive language, celebrate shared values, and make people feel that their membership in the group is part of who they are rather than merely a job they hold. The result is membership rather than mere employment — and membership in a functioning hive is one of the most reliable sources of meaning, satisfaction, and moral motivation that human psychology makes available.

Political Hives

The same dynamics that operate in corporations operate at the scale of nations. Great political leaders have always understood, at least intuitively, what Durkheim worked out theoretically: that people have a second level of social sentiments, one that binds them not to individuals but to the collective, and that this level can be activated by the right appeal at the right moment. John F. Kennedy's 1961 inaugural address is perhaps the most celebrated American example. His call to sacrifice personal comfort for the national struggle — "ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country" — was not merely a rhetorical flourish. It was an appeal precisely calibrated to flip the hive switch: to shift listeners from Durkheim's lower, profane level of individual calculation to the higher, sacred level of collective purpose and shared fate.

The yearning to be part of something larger than oneself has powered political movements of every variety. It is a real human need, not a manufactured one. But its power makes it dangerous. Haidt quotes a passage calling on individuals to renounce selfish pleasure and devote themselves to a higher national life founded on duty, sacrifice, and spiritual existence — language that sounds genuinely elevating until one discovers that it comes from Benito Mussolini's The Doctrine of Fascism. Fascism, Haidt argues, is the hive switch scaled to grotesque extremes: the nation as a total superorganism, within which the individual counts for nothing except as an instrument of the collective. If hive psychology can produce fascism, should we be suspicious of any leader who tries to activate it? Ehrenreich addresses this concern directly. She points out that the original function of collective ecstasy — dancing, carnival, festival — is to dissolve hierarchy, not reinforce it. These ancient forms of communal celebration characteristically inverted or suspended the normal social order: peasants dressed as nobles, leaders were mocked, the usual rules of deference were suspended. Everyone participated as equals. When the celebration ended, people returned to their stations — but those stations felt slightly less rigid than before, and the connections across them felt slightly warmer.

Fascist rallies, by contrast, were nothing like festivals. They were spectacles of hierarchy — passive audiences observing military processions and adoring a leader elevated to near-divine status. Participants did not dance or mock authority; they stood and applauded in submission. The hive psychology being deployed was real, but it had been corrupted — stripped of its equalizing function and repurposed to reinforce domination. This distinction matters for how we evaluate the hive switch in general. The switch itself, Haidt argues, is not dangerous. Its natural scale — dozens to a few hundred people bonded into a community of trust and cooperation — is overwhelmingly beneficial. Such groups generate the social capital that makes individuals healthier, happier, and more resilient. The danger emerges only when hive psychology is captured and scaled by a demagogue with coercive power behind him. Haidt invites a thought experiment: compare a society full of overlapping, cross-cutting hives — work teams, religious communities, sports leagues, civic organizations — with one in which no such structures exist and every person is radically autonomous. In the first society, people regularly experience self-transcendence, genuine belonging, and the meaning that comes from contributing to something larger than themselves. In the second, people may enjoy freedom but are likely to be chronically lonely and hungry for precisely the kind of meaning that rational self-interest cannot supply. The first society, Haidt argues, scores higher on virtually every measure of human flourishing — and is also more resistant to political takeover, because its citizens are not desperate for someone to give their lives purpose.

A nation composed of many small hives is structurally different from a nation captured by a single totalizing hive. The American founders understood this intuitively, designing a system of competing factions precisely to prevent any single group from dominating all the others. Political scientist Robert Putnam's research on social capital makes the same point empirically: local groups — bowling leagues, churches, neighborhood associations, community sports teams — make people smarter, healthier, safer, and wealthier, and strengthen democracy in the process. These local hives do not threaten political freedom; they protect it, by giving citizens sources of meaning and belonging that are independent of any political authority. A society of atomized individuals, by contrast, is precisely the social substrate that demagogues exploit: people who lack meaningful community and collective identity are far more vulnerable to a leader who promises to supply those things in exchange for their loyalty and submission.

The conclusion Haidt draws is not that hive psychology should be suppressed but that it should be nurtured at appropriate scales and in appropriate forms. When it operates naturally — in families, sports teams, workplaces, religious communities, civic associations — it is one of the great sources of human happiness and moral motivation. When it is captured and weaponized at the national scale by an authoritarian willing to define "us" against a despised "them," it becomes catastrophic. The task is not to eliminate the switch but to understand it well enough to build the kinds of institutions and communities that activate it in healthy ways.

A society that fails to provide legitimate channels for collective self-transcendence is not a society that has freed itself from the need for it. It is a society that has left that need unmet — and therefore vulnerable to whoever comes along offering to fill it.

In Sum

When Haidt began writing The Happiness Hypothesis, he held what he considered the philosophically mature view: that happiness is fundamentally an inside job. The Stoics and the Buddha had said as much — the world will not conform to your wishes, so the wise path is to change your desires rather than the world. By the time he finished, he had abandoned that position. Happiness, he concluded, comes from between — from the quality of a person's relationships with other people, with meaningful work, and with something larger than themselves. The hive switch is central to that third category. It is the mechanism by which people reach the level of experience that Durkheim called the sacred: temporary, ecstatic, real, and, for many people, the most meaningful moments of their lives. We evolved to live in groups, and our minds were shaped not only for within-group competition but for the periods of collective purpose that make group life possible. The hive switch activates that second mode. Haidt reviewed three broad classes of triggers for the switch: encounters with vast and overwhelming nature that generate the emotion of awe; substances he calls Durkheimogens that pharmacologically produce states of unity and self-transcendence; and large-scale synchronized movement experiences like raves, which combine muscular bonding with music and sometimes pharmacology to create collective identity at scale. The neurobiology of the switch appears to involve two main systems: oxytocin, which bonds us tightly to our own group without extending that warmth universally to all of humanity; and mirror neurons, which allow us to resonate emotionally with others, especially those who share our moral matrix. Both systems show the signature of group selection — they amplify in-group solidarity rather than universal love.

It would be pleasant to conclude that humans are capable of unlimited universal love — that the hive switch, properly activated, could bind all of humanity together. But this is not what the evidence supports, and it is not what evolution would have produced. What we can achieve is parochial love: deep, genuine, self-transcending care within the groups to which we actually belong. That love, amplified by shared identity and common purpose, and tempered by the suppression of free-riding, may be the most powerful prosocial force available to us. It cannot replace the moral aspiration to care about all people everywhere. But it can make the groups we inhabit genuinely worth belonging to — and that, in the end, may be the most reliable foundation we have for human flourishing.
