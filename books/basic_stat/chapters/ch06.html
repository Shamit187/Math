Chapter 6

Conditional Distributions

The piece Chapter 1 promised but did not deliver — what conditioning really means when the thing you condition on has probability zero.

Book pages: 101–113 ~40 min reading time Prereqs: Chapter 1, comfort with densities

Why revisit conditioning?

Back in Section 1.10, Keener gave you a definition of conditional probability that you had already met as an undergraduate: $$P(Y \in B \mid X = x) = \frac{P(Y \in B,\, X = x)}{P(X = x)}.$$ Useful, familiar, but the chapter ended with an honest warning: this formula breaks the moment $X$ is continuous, because then $P(X = x) = 0$ for every individual $x$ and the right-hand side is $0/0$.

We pushed past that crisis by using identities — the tower property in particular — that held regardless of whether $X$ was discrete or continuous. But the underlying object, "the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x$", was never properly constructed. This chapter pays that debt.

The whole chapter in one sentence

We replace the ratio formula $P(B \mid A) = P(A \cap B)/P(A)$ with a definition based on what the conditional distribution should do inside an integral. That definition works whether or not $P(A) = 0$, and it agrees with the ratio formula whenever the ratio makes sense.

Along the way we will (i) work out joint and marginal densities carefully, (ii) write down a measure-theoretic definition of conditional distribution and show how to compute it when joint densities exist, (iii) use conditioning to build hierarchical models with two worked examples — bivariate normal and beta-binomial — and finally (iv) close the loop on Chapter 3 by proving the factorization theorem.

6.1 Joint and marginal densities

Setup

Let $X$ be a random vector in $\mathbb{R}^m$ and $Y$ a random vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Stack them into the combined vector $Z = (X, Y)$ in $\mathbb{R}^{m+n}$. Suppose the distribution $P_Z$ has a density $p_Z$ with respect to the product measure $\mu \times \nu$, where $\mu$ lives on $\mathbb{R}^m$ and $\nu$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

The density $p_Z$ is called the joint density of $X$ and $Y$. By the Radon-Nikodym definition, $$P(Z \in B) = \int\!\!\int 1_B(x, y) \, p_Z(x, y) \, d\mu(x)\, d\nu(y).$$ Fubini's theorem (from Section 1.9) tells us the order of integration can be swapped freely.

Why keep $\mu$ and $\nu$ abstract?

In your undergrad class, $\mu$ and $\nu$ were always Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$, and you wrote $\int\!\int p_Z(x, y) \, dx \, dy$. Keener keeps them abstract because the framework also covers (a) discrete $X$ and discrete $Y$, where $\mu$ and $\nu$ are counting measures and the integral is a double sum; (b) mixed cases, like $X$ discrete and $Y$ continuous, where $\mu$ is counting and $\nu$ is Lebesgue. One symbol for all three cases — exactly the unification Chapter 1 promised.

Marginalizing: removing a variable

We want the distribution of $X$ alone. The trick: $X \in A$ if and only if $Z \in A \times \mathbb{R}^n$, since the second coordinate is unrestricted. So $$P(X \in A) = P(Z \in A \times \mathbb{R}^n) = \int\!\!\int 1_A(x) \, p_Z(x, y) \, d\nu(y) \, d\mu(x).$$ The indicator $1_{A \times \mathbb{R}^n}(x, y) = 1_A(x)$ — it only constrains the first coordinate. Pulling $1_A(x)$ out of the inner integral, $$P(X \in A) = \int_A \left\{ \int p_Z(x, y) \, d\nu(y) \right\} d\mu(x).$$

Reading this expression: the thing in braces is a function of $x$, and we're integrating it against $\mu$ over $A$. Comparing with the definition of density, we have just discovered that $X$ has the density $$p_X(x) = \int p_Z(x, y) \, d\nu(y) \tag{6.1}$$ with respect to $\mu$. We call $p_X$ the marginal density of $X$. By a symmetric argument, $Y$ has marginal density $$p_Y(y) = \int p_Z(x, y) \, d\mu(x).$$

In one phrase

To marginalize out a variable, integrate the joint density against the dominating measure for that variable. In the all-continuous case, that's "integrate out $y$". In the all-discrete case, it's "sum over $y$". Same formula either way.

Example 6.1 — a mixed joint density

Let $\mu$ be counting measure on $\{0, 1, \dots, k\}$ and $\nu$ Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$. Define $$p_Z(x, y) = \binom{k}{x} y^x (1 - y)^{k - x}, \qquad x \in \{0, 1, \dots, k\},\ y \in (0, 1),$$ and $p_Z = 0$ otherwise.

To get the marginal of $X$, use (6.1) and integrate $y$ out against Lebesgue measure on $(0, 1)$: $$p_X(x) = \int_0^1 \binom{k}{x} y^x (1 - y)^{k - x} \, dy = \binom{k}{x} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(x+1)\Gamma(k-x+1)}{\Gamma(k+2)} = \frac{1}{k+1}.$$ We used the beta integral $\int_0^1 u^{\alpha - 1}(1-u)^{\beta-1} du = \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)$ with $\alpha = x+1$ and $\beta = k - x + 1$. So $X$ is uniformly distributed on $\{0, 1, \dots, k\}$.

For $Y$, sum out $x$ against counting measure: $$p_Y(y) = \sum_{x = 0}^{k} \binom{k}{x} y^x (1 - y)^{k - x} = (y + (1 - y))^k = 1, \qquad y \in (0, 1).$$ That's the binomial theorem. So $Y \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$.

Pause and look

We just marginalized a single joint density two different ways — once by integrating, once by summing — and the formula $(6.1)$ handled both without modification. The dominating measure tells you which operation to perform; the algebra does not change.

6.2 Conditional distributions

The problem with the elementary formula

Suppose we observe $X = x$ and ask: what is the distribution of $Y$ now? When $X$ is discrete and $p_X(x) > 0$, the elementary formula $$P(Y \in B \mid X = x) = \frac{P(X = x,\, Y \in B)}{P(X = x)}$$ is honest arithmetic. We compute a numerator, a denominator, take the ratio.

But when $X$ is continuous, the event $\{X = x\}$ has probability zero, and so does the joint event. The ratio is $0/0$ for every $x$. So we cannot define the conditional distribution by this formula; we have to change the definition.

Common pitfall

Students sometimes try to rescue the elementary formula by writing $P(X \in [x, x + dx])$ in the denominator and taking $dx \to 0$. This works as informal motivation, but it is not a definition — it relies on you picking the right "shrinking event" $\{X \in [x, x+dx]\}$, and different choices can give different limits. (For a famous illustration, search "Borel-Kolmogorov paradox".) The clean way out is to change what we ask the conditional distribution to do, not to take limits of ratios.

What should the conditional distribution accomplish?

Step back from formulas and ask: what is conditioning for? In Chapter 1 we used it for one thing — the smoothing identity $$E\, f(X, Y) = E\, E[f(X, Y) \mid X]. \tag{6.2}$$ Writing $E[f(X, Y) \mid X] = H(X)$ where $H(x) = \int f(x, y) \, dQ_x(y)$, equation (6.2) expands to $$E\, f(X, Y) = \int H(x) \, dP_X(x) = \int\!\!\int f(x, y) \, dQ_x(y) \, dP_X(x). \tag{6.3}$$

Here $Q_x$ stands for "the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x$", whatever that means. The point is this: identity (6.3) is what we want the conditional distribution to achieve. So we will turn it around and use it as the definition.

The switch in perspective

Elementary conditioning: "$Q_x$ is a thing, defined by a ratio". Measure-theoretic conditioning: "$Q_x$ is whatever family of probability measures makes the smoothing identity (6.3) true". The ratio formula is one way to produce a $Q_x$ when $P(X = x) > 0$. The new definition admits other ways when it isn't.

The formal definition

Definition 6.2 — conditional distribution

A function $Q$ assigning a probability measure $Q_x$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ to each $x \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is a conditional distribution for $Y$ given $X$, written $Y \mid X = x \sim Q_x$, if:

  1. $Q_x(\cdot)$ is a probability measure on the Borel sets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ for every $x$.
  2. For every Borel set $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, the function $x \mapsto Q_x(B)$ is measurable.
  3. For all Borel sets $A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ and $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, $$P(X \in A,\, Y \in B) = \int_A Q_x(B) \, dP_X(x).$$
Reading the three clauses

Clause 1: for each fixed $x$, $Q_x$ is an honest probability distribution — it assigns probabilities to Borel sets, and the total is one.

Clause 2: as $x$ varies, $Q_x(B)$ must vary measurably. Otherwise we couldn't integrate it against $P_X$ in clause 3. This is the technical condition that distinguishes "conditional distribution" from "an arbitrary family of probability measures".

Clause 3: this is (6.3) specialized to $f = 1_{A \times B}$. It says the joint probability of $\{X \in A,\, Y \in B\}$ can be recovered by averaging the conditional probability $Q_x(B)$ over the marginal distribution of $X$. Plain English: the law of total probability becomes the definition.

A theorem (cited by Keener — Billingsley 33.3 for the scalar case, Rao 2005 Chapter 5 in general) guarantees that when $X$ and $Y$ are random vectors, a conditional distribution always exists. Outside the random-vector setting, existence can fail: clause 1 can fail to be satisfiable. But for everything in this book, we are safe.

Why this is the right definition

The definition does not divide by anything. It nowhere uses $P(X = x)$ on its own. It uses only integrals — and integrals are happy to integrate against measures whose mass is spread out continuously. That is the entire reason this definition survives the continuous case.

Where does this definition come from? The Radon-Nikodym viewpoint

Definition 6.2 may feel pulled from a hat. Here is the deeper reason it is correct.

Fix a Borel set $B$ and define a set function on $\mathbb{R}^m$ by $$\eta_B(A) = P(X \in A,\, Y \in B).$$ Easy to check: $\eta_B$ is a finite measure, and $\eta_B(A) \leq P(X \in A) = P_X(A)$. So $\eta_B$ is dominated by $P_X$, meaning $P_X(A) = 0 \Rightarrow \eta_B(A) = 0$. By Theorem 1.10 (Radon-Nikodym), $\eta_B$ has a density with respect to $P_X$: there exists a measurable function $\phi_B(x)$ such that $$P(X \in A,\, Y \in B) = \int_A \phi_B(x) \, dP_X(x).$$

Compare with clause 3 of Definition 6.2: $\phi_B(x) = Q_x(B)$. The conditional probability $Q_x(B)$ is a Radon-Nikodym derivative — the density of "joint probability that $X$ is in a region and $Y$ is in $B$" with respect to "marginal probability that $X$ is in the region".

The big picture

Chapter 1 already taught you that PDFs and PMFs are both Radon-Nikodym derivatives. Now we see that conditional probabilities are too. The whole subject is one idea — derivative of one measure with respect to another — reused in three different roles.

Two subtleties hide inside this construction. First, the Radon-Nikodym theorem gives $\phi_B$ uniquely up to a $P_X$-null set. So $Q_x(B)$ is defined for $P_X$-almost every $x$, not literally every $x$. When $X$ is continuous, this means $Q_x$ is undetermined on a Lebesgue-null set of $x$'s, and we are free to define it arbitrarily there. Second, $\phi_B$ is built separately for each $B$. Bundling these into a single family of probability measures $Q_x$ — one $Q_x$ that works for all $B$ simultaneously — is precisely what clause 1 of the definition asks for, and it is the non-trivial part of the existence theorem.

Computing $Q_x$ when a joint density exists

The definition is abstract. Now we recover something computable: when $Z = (X, Y)$ has a joint density, the conditional distribution is given by the formula your undergraduate textbook taught you — and now we have earned it.

Theorem 6.3 — conditional density

Suppose $X$ and $Y$ have joint density $p_Z$ with respect to $\mu \times \nu$, with marginal density $p_X$ for $X$ defined by (6.1). Let $E = \{x : p_X(x) > 0\}$. For $x \in E$, define $$p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_Z(x, y)}{p_X(x)}, \tag{6.4}$$ and let $Q_x$ be the probability measure with density $p_{Y \mid X}(\cdot \mid x)$ with respect to $\nu$. For $x \notin E$, let $Q_x$ be any fixed probability distribution $P_0$. Then $Q$ is a conditional distribution for $Y$ given $X$.

Proof — going slow

Three things to check: clauses 1, 2, 3 of Definition 6.2.

Clause 1. For $x \in E$, $p_{Y \mid X}(\cdot \mid x)$ is non-negative (ratio of non-negatives) and integrates to one against $\nu$: $$\int p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \, d\nu(y) = \frac{1}{p_X(x)} \int p_Z(x, y) \, d\nu(y) = \frac{p_X(x)}{p_X(x)} = 1.$$ So $Q_x$ is a probability measure. For $x \notin E$, $Q_x = P_0$ is a probability measure by choice.

Clause 2. Measurability of $Q_x(B)$ in $x$ follows from measurability of $p_Z$ and standard Fubini-type results.

Clause 3. This is where the work happens. We will show the stronger statement (6.3) for any integrable $f$, from which clause 3 follows by taking $f = 1_{A \times B}$.

Note first that $P(X \in E) = 1$: the set where $p_X = 0$ has zero $P_X$-mass by construction. So we lose nothing if we replace $p_Z(x, y)$ by $p_Z(x, y) \cdot 1_E(x)$ — the two functions agree $(\mu \times \nu)$-a.e. Both are valid joint densities. With this replacement, $p_Z(x, y) = 0$ whenever $x \notin E$, and the factorization $p_Z(x, y) = p_X(x) p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x)$ holds for all $(x, y)$ (with the convention $0 = 0 \cdot \text{anything}$ off $E$).

Now compute the right-hand side of (6.3): $$\int\!\!\int f(x, y) \, dQ_x(y) \, dP_X(x) = \int\!\!\int f(x, y) \, p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) \, d\nu(y) \, p_X(x) \, d\mu(x).$$ Combining the densities and applying Fubini, $$= \int\!\!\int f(x, y) \, p_Z(x, y) \, d\nu(y) \, d\mu(x) = E\, f(X, Y).$$ That is exactly (6.3). Specializing to $f = 1_{A \times B}$ gives clause 3.

The bottom line

When you have a joint density, the conditional density is the joint divided by the marginal. The familiar formula. The point of Section 6.2 is that this formula is a theorem — Theorem 6.3 — derived from a definition that does not itself involve any division. The definition handles the no-density case; the theorem hands you back your old computational rules whenever a density exists.

Independence as a consequence

Two variables are independent iff their joint density factors: $p_Z(x, y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y)$. Plugging into (6.4): $$p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_X(x) p_Y(y)}{p_X(x)} = p_Y(y).$$ The conditional density doesn't depend on $x$. Independence means "knowing $X$ tells you nothing about $Y$", and now we see that translated into a formula.

Example 6.1 (continued) — a beta-binomial hierarchy

Recall the joint density from earlier: $$p_Z(x, y) = \binom{k}{x} y^x (1 - y)^{k - x}, \qquad x \in \{0, \dots, k\},\ y \in (0, 1).$$ We found $p_X(x) = 1/(k+1)$ and $p_Y(y) = 1$ on $(0, 1)$. Now we read off two conditional densities.

$X$ given $Y$. Since $p_Y(y) = 1$, equation (6.4) gives $$p_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{p_Z(x, y)}{p_Y(y)} = \binom{k}{x} y^x (1 - y)^{k - x}.$$ For fixed $y$, this is the binomial mass function with $k$ trials and success probability $y$. So $$X \mid Y = y \ \sim \ \text{Binomial}(k, y). \tag{6.5}$$

$Y$ given $X$. Since $p_X(x) = 1/(k+1)$, $$p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{p_Z(x, y)}{1/(k+1)} = (k+1)\binom{k}{x} y^x (1-y)^{k-x}.$$ Tidying the constants using $(k+1)\binom{k}{x} = \frac{\Gamma(k+2)}{\Gamma(x+1)\Gamma(k-x+1)}$, $$p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{\Gamma(k+2)}{\Gamma(x+1)\Gamma(k-x+1)} y^{(x+1) - 1}(1 - y)^{(k - x + 1) - 1}.$$ That is a beta density. So $$Y \mid X = x \ \sim \ \text{Beta}(x + 1,\, k - x + 1).$$

Notice the picture: we started with one joint density on a mixed product measure and discovered that it encodes a coupled pair — binomial conditional on the continuous variable, beta conditional on the discrete one. This is the beta-binomial conjugate pair, and we will see it again in Chapter 7 in its natural home: Bayesian estimation.

Smoothing in action — recovering $E\, X$ and $E\, X^2$

From (6.5), $E[X \mid Y] = kY$ since the binomial mean is $k \cdot y$ when the success probability is $y$. Smoothing gives $$E\, X = E\, E[X \mid Y] = k\, E\, Y = k \int_0^1 y \, dy = \frac{k}{2}.$$ We can check directly using the marginal $p_X(x) = 1/(k+1)$: $$E\, X = \sum_{x=0}^{k} \frac{x}{k+1} = \frac{1}{k+1} \cdot \frac{k(k+1)}{2} = \frac{k}{2}. \ \checkmark$$

For the second moment, the binomial second moment is $ky(1-y) + k^2 y^2$, so $$E\, X^2 = E\, E[X^2 \mid Y] = E[kY(1-Y) + k^2 Y^2] = \int_0^1 \big(ky(1-y) + k^2 y^2\big) dy = \frac{k(1 + 2k)}{6}.$$ Comparing with the direct calculation $E\, X^2 = \sum_{x=0}^{k} x^2/(k+1)$, smoothing has given us, almost as a side-effect, the identity $$\sum_{x=0}^k x^2 = \frac{k(k+1)(2k+1)}{6}.$$ Inductionists call this an exercise. Statisticians call it a lemma you get for free.

6.3 Building models

Specifying joint distributions one piece at a time

Up to now we have started with a joint density and pulled out conditionals. The reverse direction is what we do when modelling: we specify a marginal for $X$, then specify how $Y$ behaves given $X$, and let (6.4) generate the joint: $$p_Z(x, y) = p_X(x) \, p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x).$$

Why is this useful? Because real-world stories rarely come out as a single joint density. They come out as a sequence: first the patient gets a disease; given the disease, a test produces a reading; given the reading, a doctor makes a decision. Each step is its own conditional. Multiplying conditionals together reproduces the joint.

The pattern extends to any number of variables. Writing $p(x_k \mid x_1, \dots, x_{k-1})$ for the conditional density of $X_k$ given $X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_{k-1} = x_{k-1}$, $$p_{X_1, \dots, X_n}(x_1, \dots, x_n) = p_{X_1}(x_1)\, p(x_2 \mid x_1)\, p(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2) \cdots p(x_n \mid x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}). \tag{6.6}$$

The chain rule for densities

Equation (6.6) is the density analogue of the chain rule for probabilities you saw in elementary probability. The joint distribution decomposes into a chain of one-step conditionals. Choose the chain carefully and modelling becomes tractable; choose poorly and you write down something incomputable.

Example 6.4 — random walks and autoregression

Suppose $X_k$ is the log price of a stock at time $k$. A natural conditional specification is $$X_k \mid X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_{k-1} = x_{k-1} \ \sim \ N(x_{k-1} + \mu, \sigma^2).$$ That is: tomorrow's log price is centered on today's, plus a drift $\mu$, with noise $\sigma^2$. If $X_1 \sim N(x_0 + \mu, \sigma^2)$, then (6.6) yields the joint density $$\prod_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left[ -\frac{(x_k - x_{k-1} - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right].$$ The differences $X_k - X_{k-1}$ are i.i.d. $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ — this is the classical random walk.

Alternatively, with $|\rho| < 1$, the specification $$X_k \mid X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_{k-1} = x_{k-1} \ \sim \ N(\rho x_{k-1}, \sigma^2)$$ gives the joint density $$\prod_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left[ -\frac{(x_k - \rho x_{k-1})^2}{2\sigma^2} \right],$$ the autoregressive (AR-1) model. The parameter $\rho$ controls memory: $\rho = 0$ gives independence, $\rho \to 1$ approaches the random walk.

Example 6.5 — a simple epidemic model

Take a population of size $N$. Let $X_i$ be the number infected at stage $i$, with infections permanent. Suppose the chance one infected person infects one specific uninfected person between stages is $p = 1 - q$, and infections are independent. Then the probability that one specific uninfected person stays uninfected when there are $x_k$ currently infected is $q^{x_k}$ — and the number of newly infected people in the next step is binomial: $$X_{k+1} - X_k \mid X_1 = x_1, \dots, X_k = x_k \ \sim \ \text{Binomial}(N - x_k,\, 1 - q^{x_k}).$$ Equivalently, $p(x_{k+1} \mid x_1, \dots, x_k) = \binom{N - x_k}{x_{k+1} - x_k}(1 - q^{x_k})^{x_{k+1} - x_k}(q^{x_k})^{N - x_{k+1}}.$ Multiplying these conditionals by (6.6) gives the joint mass function for the whole trajectory.

Worked example — bivariate normal

Worked example — the bivariate normal conditional

Suppose $(X, Y)$ has joint density $$p(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}} \exp\!\left[ -\frac{x^2 - 2\rho xy + y^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)} \right],$$ with $E\, X = E\, Y = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \operatorname{Var}(Y) = 1$, $\operatorname{Cor}(X, Y) = \rho$. What is the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x$?

The marginal of $X$ is $N(0, 1)$, so $p_X(x) = (2\pi)^{-1/2} e^{-x^2/2}$. Apply (6.4): $$p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p_X(x)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1 - \rho^2)}} \exp\!\left[ -\frac{x^2 - 2\rho xy + y^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)} + \frac{x^2}{2} \right].$$ Combine the exponents over the common denominator $2(1 - \rho^2)$ and expand the second term as $x^2(1 - \rho^2)/(2(1 - \rho^2)) = (x^2 - \rho^2 x^2)/(2(1 - \rho^2))$. The numerator becomes $$-(x^2 - 2\rho xy + y^2) + x^2 - \rho^2 x^2 = -(y^2 - 2\rho xy + \rho^2 x^2) = -(y - \rho x)^2.$$ Therefore $$p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1 - \rho^2)}} \exp\!\left[ -\frac{(y - \rho x)^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)} \right],$$ which is the density of $N(\rho x, 1 - \rho^2)$. So $$Y \mid X = x \ \sim \ N(\rho x,\, 1 - \rho^2).$$

Two features deserve a moment of reflection. First, the conditional mean $\rho x$ is a linear function of $x$ — this is the famous "regression line" of the bivariate normal. Second, the conditional variance $1 - \rho^2$ does not depend on $x$. Knowing $X$ shrinks the variance of $Y$ by a factor $1 - \rho^2$, uniformly in $x$. As $\rho \to \pm 1$, the conditional variance collapses to zero — $Y$ becomes a deterministic function of $X$.

Using smoothing, we can compute $\operatorname{Cov}(X^2, Y^2)$ without integrating the joint density twice. Since $Y \mid X \sim N(\rho X, 1 - \rho^2)$, $E[Y^2 \mid X] = (\rho X)^2 + (1 - \rho^2) = \rho^2 X^2 + (1 - \rho^2)$. Then $E[X^2 Y^2] = E[X^2 \, E[Y^2 \mid X]] = \rho^2 E[X^4] + (1 - \rho^2) E[X^2] = 3\rho^2 + (1 - \rho^2) = 1 + 2\rho^2$. Since $E[X^2] = E[Y^2] = 1$, $\operatorname{Cov}(X^2, Y^2) = 1 + 2\rho^2 - 1 = 2\rho^2$.

Stop and check

Before reading on: can you reconstruct the path "joint density $\to$ marginal $\to$ conditional" without looking? Try it for the bivariate normal in your head, then for the beta-binomial pair. The mechanics are identical even when one variable is continuous and the other is discrete.

6.4 Proof of the factorization theorem

Back in Chapter 3 we stated the factorization theorem: $T$ is sufficient for $\theta$ iff the density factors as $p_\theta(x) = g_\theta(T(x))\, h(x)$. We could prove this in the dominated discrete case by elementary arguments. The general case requires conditional distributions, because in general $T$ and $X$ do not have a joint density with respect to any product measure. This section closes that gap. It is technical; you can skim it on first reading and come back later.

Setup

Suppose $P_\theta$ for $\theta \in \Omega$ has density $$p_\theta(x) = g_\theta(T(x)) \, h(x) \tag{6.7}$$ with respect to $\mu$. By absorbing factors into $h$, we may assume $\mu$ is a probability measure equivalent to the family $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\}$ — meaning $\mu$ and the $P_\theta$ have the same null sets. (Keener notes this uses a standard fact about dominated families.) Let:

  • $E^*$ and $P^*$ denote expectation and probability when $X \sim \mu$.
  • $G^*$ and $G_\theta$ denote the marginal distributions of $T(X)$ when $X \sim \mu$ and $X \sim P_\theta$.
  • $Q$ denote the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T$ when $X \sim \mu$.

Forward direction — factorization implies sufficiency

We want to show $T$ is sufficient, i.e. that the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$. The trick is to construct that conditional distribution explicitly using the factorization.

First, find the $G^*$-density of $T$ under $P_\theta$. For any $f$, $$E_\theta f(T) = \int f(T(x)) g_\theta(T(x)) h(x) \, d\mu(x) = E^* f(T) g_\theta(T) h(X).$$ Conditioning on $T$ under $\mu$, $$E^* f(T) g_\theta(T) h(X) = \int\!\!\int f(t) g_\theta(t) h(x) \, dQ_t(x) \, dG^*(t) = \int f(t) g_\theta(t) w(t) \, dG^*(t),$$ where $w(t) = \int h(x) \, dQ_t(x)$. Taking $f$ an indicator, this shows $G_\theta$ has density $g_\theta(t) w(t)$ with respect to $G^*$.

Now define a candidate for the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T$ under $P_\theta$: let $\tilde Q_t$ have density $h(x)/w(t)$ with respect to $Q_t$. (On the null set $w(t) = 0$, define $\tilde Q_t$ arbitrarily.) For any $f$, $$E_\theta f(X, T) = E^* f(X, T) g_\theta(T) h(X) = \int\!\!\int f(x, t) g_\theta(t) h(x) \, dQ_t(x) \, dG^*(t)$$ $$= \int\!\!\int f(x, t) \, \frac{h(x)}{w(t)} \, dQ_t(x) \, g_\theta(t) w(t) \, dG^*(t) = \int\!\!\int f(x, t) \, d\tilde Q_t(x) \, dG_\theta(t).$$ By (6.3), $\tilde Q$ is the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T$ under $P_\theta$ — and the formula $\tilde Q_t = (h / w(t)) \cdot Q_t$ does not depend on $\theta$. Hence $T$ is sufficient.

Mixture distributions — a brief detour

Before the converse, we need one tool. Given a marginal $G^*$ and a conditional $Q$, the mixture distribution $\hat P$ is defined by $$\hat P(B) = \int Q_t(B) \, dG^*(t) = \int\!\!\int 1_B(x) \, dQ_t(x) \, dG^*(t).$$ Linearity then extends to integrable $f$: $$\int f \, d\hat P = \int\!\!\int f(x) \, dQ_t(x) \, dG^*(t).$$ Think of $\hat P$ as "draw $T$ from $G^*$, then draw $X$ from $Q_T$". This is exactly how a hierarchical sample looks.

Reverse direction — sufficiency implies factorization

Now suppose $T$ is sufficient, with $Q$ the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T$ under any (equivalently, every) $P_\theta$. Let $g_\theta$ be the $G^*$-density of $T$ under $P_\theta$ — this density exists because $G^*(N) = 0$ implies $\mu(T^{-1}(N)) = 0$, hence $G_\theta(N) = 0$, by the equivalence of $\mu$ with the family.

Then $$P_\theta(X \in B) = E_\theta P_\theta(X \in B \mid T) = E_\theta Q_T(B) = \int Q_t(B) g_\theta(t) \, dG^*(t).$$ Unrolling $Q_t(B) = \int 1_B(x) \, dQ_t(x)$, $$P_\theta(X \in B) = \int\!\!\int 1_B(x) g_\theta(t) \, dQ_t(x) \, dG^*(t) = \int\!\!\int 1_B(x) g_\theta(T(x)) \, dQ_t(x) \, dG^*(t)$$ (since $T(x) = t$ inside the inner integral against $Q_t$, by the definition of conditional distribution) $$= \int_B g_\theta(T(x)) \, d\hat P(x).$$ So $P_\theta$ has density $g_\theta(T(\cdot))$ with respect to $\hat P$.

One last step: convert "density with respect to $\hat P$" into "density with respect to $\mu$" by checking $\hat P \ll \mu$. If $\mu(N) = 0$, then $P_\theta(N) = 0$ (by equivalence), and unrolling $P_\theta(N) = \int Q_t(N) \, dG_\theta(t) = 0$ forces $Q_t(N) = 0$ for $G_\theta$-a.e. $t$, hence for $G^*$-a.e. $t$ (again by equivalence), hence $\hat P(N) = \int Q_t(N) \, dG^*(t) = 0$. So $h(x) = d\hat P / d\mu$ exists, and finally $$p_\theta(x) = g_\theta(T(x)) \, h(x).$$ That is (6.7). The proof is complete.

What the proof actually shows

The factorization theorem says "the data factor cleanly through the statistic $T$". The conditional distribution machinery makes this rigorous in a setting where $T$ and $X$ may not have a joint density. The crucial move was using a single reference measure $\mu$ equivalent to the whole family, plus the Radon-Nikodym derivative $h = d\hat P / d\mu$ to absorb the "$x$-only" part of the density. Nothing magical — just careful bookkeeping with the tools we now have.

Summary

The chapter, in seven bullets:

  1. The joint density of $(X, Y)$ with respect to $\mu \times \nu$ unifies all-discrete, all-continuous, and mixed cases. Marginals are obtained by integrating out the unwanted variable against its dominating measure (sum or integral, depending on the case).
  2. The elementary ratio $P(B \mid A) = P(A \cap B)/P(A)$ fails when $P(A) = 0$ — so it cannot define conditioning on a continuous random variable.
  3. The measure-theoretic definition (Definition 6.2) replaces the ratio with a family of probability measures $Q_x$ satisfying $P(X \in A, Y \in B) = \int_A Q_x(B) \, dP_X(x)$. Existence holds whenever $X, Y$ are random vectors.
  4. The conditional probability $Q_x(B)$ is a Radon-Nikodym derivative — the density of "joint mass" with respect to "$X$-marginal mass". Two consequences: $Q_x$ is only defined up to a $P_X$-null set, and conditional probabilities are not literally division.
  5. When a joint density $p_Z$ exists, Theorem 6.3 hands back the familiar formula $p_{Y \mid X}(y \mid x) = p_Z(x, y)/p_X(x)$. The general definition specializes to elementary practice whenever elementary practice was already valid.
  6. Joint distributions can be built one conditional at a time: $p_Z = p_X \cdot p_{Y \mid X}$, chained out to $n$ variables by (6.6). This is the engine of time-series, epidemic, and hierarchical models.
  7. The factorization theorem for sufficient statistics, deferred from Chapter 3, follows cleanly from Definition 6.2 plus a reference measure equivalent to the dominated family.
