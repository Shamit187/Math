Chapter 7

Bayesian Estimation

Turning the prior into the posterior, and learning to pick the estimator that minimises expected loss given what you saw.

Book pages: 115–127 ~40 min reading time Prereqs: Ch 3 (risk), Ch 6 (conditioning)

Why does anyone average the risk?

Back in Chapter 3 we measured an estimator $\delta$ by its risk function $R(\theta, \delta) = E_\theta L(\theta, \delta(X))$, a number for every possible $\theta$. Two estimators are easy to compare when one curve sits below the other for all $\theta$. But the moment the graphs cross — one estimator is better for small $\theta$, the other for large $\theta$ — there is nothing in the frequentist framework that breaks the tie.

Bayesians break the tie by collapsing the whole risk function down to a single number:

$$\int R(\theta, \delta) \, d\Lambda(\theta), \tag{7.1}$$

where $\Lambda$ is a probability measure on the parameter space $\Omega$. Now every estimator gets one score, and "smaller is better" makes unambiguous sense. The estimator that minimises this average is called Bayes.

The shift in worldview

In the frequentist view, $\theta$ is a fixed unknown number and only the data $X$ is random. In the Bayesian view, both $\theta$ and $X$ are random. The integrator (7.1) is just the expected loss in this enlarged probability model. Everything else — priors, posteriors, the famous Bayes-rule update — falls out of taking that one extra step seriously.

7.1 Bayesian models and the main result

Setting up the joint distribution

We write the random parameter as a capital $\Theta$, with a lowercase $\theta$ for a value it might take. The Bayesian model consists of two pieces:

  • A prior distribution $\Theta \sim \Lambda$. This says what we believe about $\Theta$ before seeing any data.
  • A sampling model $X \mid \Theta = \theta \sim P_\theta$. This is the same family of distributions we've used all along — only now it's a conditional distribution.

Together these specify the joint distribution of $(\Theta, X)$. By the smoothing identity (Chapter 1, Section 1.10),

$$E\!\big[L(\Theta, \delta(X)) \mid \Theta = \theta\big] = \int L(\theta, \delta(x)) \, dP_\theta(x) = R(\theta, \delta),$$

and taking another expectation over $\Theta$,

$$E\, L(\Theta, \delta) \;=\; E\,R(\Theta, \delta) \;=\; \int R(\theta, \delta) \, d\Lambda(\theta).$$

So expression (7.1) really is "expected loss" in the Bayesian probability model. It is not a different philosophy bolted onto an existing formula; it is the same expectation computed two ways.

Two readings of the same integral

Read $\int R(\theta, \delta)\, d\Lambda(\theta)$ left-to-right and you see a frequentist averaging the risk against a weighting. Read it right-to-left as $E\, L(\Theta, \delta(X))$ and you see a Bayesian computing one number — the expected loss — in the joint probability model. Same integral, two languages.

Where does $\Lambda$ come from?

Honest answer: it depends. Keener lists three flavours of justification, and a good statistician keeps all three in mind.

  • Empirical / frequentist. Sometimes $\Theta$ really is random in a frequentist sense. Keener's vivid example is a postal scanner: $\Theta$ is the zip code on an envelope, $X$ is pixel data, and $\Lambda$ is just the empirical distribution of zip codes seen in the mail stream. Here $\Lambda$ is calibrated by data, not by belief.
  • Subjective. More often, $\Theta$ is a fixed quantity of nature (a population mean, a survival probability) that has no frequentist distribution. Then $\Lambda$ expresses the researcher's informed subjective opinion about plausible values.
  • Pragmatic. Both ideas are tempered by computation. A prior that admits a clean posterior is often chosen because the math is tractable, not because we believe it literally.

The main theorem

We now ask: given a Bayesian model, which $\delta$ actually minimises (7.1)? The answer is the cleanest result in the chapter, and it is a direct application of conditioning.

If we had no data at all, the best constant guess $d$ would minimise $E\, L(\Theta, d)$ — a pure prior optimisation. With data $X = x$ in hand, we should instead minimise the conditional expected loss:

$$E\!\big[ L(\Theta, d) \mid X = x \big].$$

This is called the posterior risk at $d$. The conditional distribution of $\Theta$ given $X = x$ is the posterior distribution, often written $\Lambda(\cdot \mid x)$ with density $\lambda(\theta \mid x)$.

Theorem 7.1 — the Bayes estimator minimises posterior expected loss

Suppose $\Theta \sim \Lambda$, $X \mid \Theta = \theta \sim P_\theta$, and $L(\theta, d) \geq 0$. Assume

  1. $E\, L(\Theta, \delta_0) < \infty$ for some estimator $\delta_0$, and
  2. for almost every $x$ there is a value $\delta_\Lambda(x)$ that minimises $E[L(\Theta, d) \mid X = x]$ over $d$.

Then $\delta_\Lambda$ is a Bayes estimator.

Why the proof is just smoothing

Let $\delta$ be any other estimator. By the definition of $\delta_\Lambda(x)$,

$$E\!\big[L(\Theta, \delta(X)) \mid X = x\big] \;\geq\; E\!\big[L(\Theta, \delta_\Lambda(X)) \mid X = x\big]$$

for almost every $x$. Now take expectations on both sides (smoothing recovers the unconditional expectation):

$$E\, L(\Theta, \delta(X)) \;\geq\; E\, L(\Theta, \delta_\Lambda(X)).$$

That is the entire proof. The trick is to push the optimisation inside the conditional expectation, where it becomes a pointwise problem in $d$.

Restating the punch line

To find the Bayes estimator, do not minimise over functions $\delta$. For each observed $x$, just pick the value $d$ that minimises a single number — the posterior expected loss. Stitch those pointwise minimisers together and you have $\delta_\Lambda$. Optimisation over a function space has collapsed into a pile of one-dimensional minimisations, one per value of $x$.

Pause — the posterior is the prior of the next experiment

Suppose you observe $X_1, X_2, \dots$ in sequence. After $X_1 = x_1$, the posterior $\Lambda(\cdot \mid x_1)$ encodes everything you now believe about $\Theta$. If a second measurement $X_2$ arrives, you can treat $\Lambda(\cdot \mid x_1)$ as the new prior and Bayes-update again. The "posterior becomes the next prior" is not a slogan — it is a literal consequence of how conditioning composes. Information accumulates one observation at a time, with no special bookkeeping required.

Computing the posterior in the dominated case

In nearly every concrete example, the family $\{P_\theta\}$ has densities $p_\theta(x)$ (against some measure $\mu$ on $\mathcal{X}$) and the prior $\Lambda$ has a Lebesgue density $\lambda(\theta)$. Then the joint density of $(\Theta, X)$ is

$$p_\theta(x) \, \lambda(\theta).$$

The marginal density of $X$ is

$$q(x) = \int p_\theta(x) \, \lambda(\theta) \, d\theta,$$

and the posterior density of $\Theta$ given $X = x$ is

$$\lambda(\theta \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x) \, \lambda(\theta)}{q(x)}.$$

This is the famous Bayes-rule formula. Notice that $q(x)$ does not depend on $\theta$ — it is just a normalising constant. In practice this lets us write

$$\lambda(\theta \mid x) \;\propto\; p_\theta(x) \, \lambda(\theta),$$

identify the right-hand side as a recognisable distribution in $\theta$, and read off the normalising constant for free.

7.2 Examples

Squared-error loss: the posterior mean

Consider weighted squared-error loss $L(\theta, d) = w(\theta)(d - g(\theta))^2$ for some weight $w \geq 0$ and quantity of interest $g(\theta)$. The posterior expected loss, expanded as a quadratic in $d$, is

$$d^2\, E[w(\Theta) \mid X = x] \;-\; 2d\, E[w(\Theta) g(\Theta) \mid X = x] \;+\; E[w(\Theta) g(\Theta)^2 \mid X = x].$$

Differentiate in $d$, set to zero, and you get

$$\delta_\Lambda(x) \;=\; \frac{E[w(\Theta) g(\Theta) \mid X = x]}{E[w(\Theta) \mid X = x]}. \tag{7.2}$$

In the special case $w \equiv 1$ this collapses to

$$\delta_\Lambda(X) = E[g(\Theta) \mid X],$$

the posterior mean of $g(\Theta)$. Memorise this: under squared-error loss, the Bayes estimator is the posterior mean. Most of the examples below just ask you to compute that one quantity.

Worked example 7.3 — Beta–Binomial

Let $X \mid \Theta = \theta \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)$ and place a $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior on $\Theta$:

$$\lambda(\theta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} \, \theta^{\alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}, \qquad \theta \in (0,1).$$

The joint density of $X$ and $\Theta$ is proportional to $\theta^{x + \alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{n - x + \beta - 1}$, which is the kernel of a $\text{Beta}(x + \alpha, n - x + \beta)$ density. Hence

$$\Theta \mid X = x \;\sim\; \text{Beta}(x + \alpha, \; n - x + \beta).$$

The update rule is irresistibly clean: add the number of successes $x$ to $\alpha$, and add the number of failures $n - x$ to $\beta$. Under squared-error loss,

$$\delta_\Lambda(X) = E[\Theta \mid X] = \frac{X + \alpha}{n + \alpha + \beta},$$

which rearranges into a weighted average of the maximum-likelihood estimator $X/n$ and the prior mean $\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$:

$$\delta_\Lambda(X) = \frac{n}{n + \alpha + \beta} \cdot \frac{X}{n} \;+\; \frac{\alpha + \beta}{n + \alpha + \beta} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}.$$

As $n \to \infty$ the data swamps the prior; as $n \to 0$ we fall back to prior beliefs. The prior parameters $\alpha, \beta$ act like "pseudo-counts" of imagined prior successes and failures.

What just happened

We started with a Beta prior, multiplied by a Binomial likelihood, and recovered a Beta posterior. Whenever the prior and posterior live in the same parametric family, we call the prior conjugate for the sampling model. Conjugacy is a computational windfall: you never have to evaluate the normalising integral $q(x)$ — you just read off the new parameters.

The normal–normal model with known variance

This is the second canonical example. Suppose $X \mid \Theta = \theta \sim N(\theta, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ known, and place a $N(\mu_0, \tau^2)$ prior on $\Theta$. The joint density is proportional to

$$\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\theta - \mu_0)^2}{2\tau^2}\right).$$

Collecting terms in $\theta$ inside the exponent gives a quadratic in $\theta$, so the posterior is again normal. Completing the square shows

$$\Theta \mid X = x \;\sim\; N\!\left( \frac{\tau^2 x + \sigma^2 \mu_0}{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}, \; \frac{\sigma^2 \tau^2}{\sigma^2 + \tau^2} \right).$$

The posterior mean — and hence the Bayes estimator under squared-error loss — is a precision-weighted average of the data and the prior mean. The posterior precision (inverse variance) is the sum of the data precision $1/\sigma^2$ and the prior precision $1/\tau^2$. Precisions add: a beautifully clean rule that generalises to $n$ i.i.d. observations by replacing $\sigma^2$ with $\sigma^2/n$.

Worked example 7.4 — Negative Binomial, and a warning

Now we run the machinery on a setup where it misbehaves. Let $X$ be the number of failures before the second success in Bernoulli$(\theta)$ trials, so

$$p_\theta(x) = (x + 1)\, \theta^2 (1 - \theta)^x, \qquad x = 0, 1, 2, \dots.$$

Take $\Theta \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$, and try to estimate $g(\Theta) = 1/\Theta$ under squared-error loss. The posterior is

$$\Theta \mid X = x \;\sim\; \text{Beta}(3, x + 1).$$

The posterior mean of $1/\Theta$ works out to $\delta_0(x) = (x + 3)/2$. But the UMVU estimator from Chapter 5 is $\delta_1(x) = (x + 2)/2$, which differs from $\delta_0$ by a constant. So they have the same variance, but $\delta_0$ has constant bias $1/2$, hence

$$R(\theta, \delta_0) = R(\theta, \delta_1) + 1/4.$$

The "Bayes" estimator is uniformly dominated by another estimator! What went wrong is that condition (a) of Theorem 7.1 fails: $E\, L(\Theta, \delta) = \infty$ for every $\delta$, because $1/\Theta$ has infinite prior expectation under Uniform$(0,1)$. When the Bayes risk is everywhere infinite, every estimator achieves "the minimum" (which is $\infty$), and the formal Bayes calculation no longer picks out a good one.

Common pitfall — improper or heavy-tailed priors

An improper prior is one whose total mass is infinite (for instance, the "flat prior" $\lambda(\theta) \equiv 1$ on $\mathbb{R}$). These are tempting because they look noncommittal, but they invalidate the hypotheses of Theorem 7.1. The posterior may still exist as a probability distribution — which lulls you into thinking everything is fine — but the resulting estimator can be inadmissible (a different estimator beats it for every $\theta$). Always check that condition (a) of Theorem 7.1 holds before declaring a "Bayes" estimator is good.

Pause — sequential updating in action

Take the Beta–Binomial. Start with $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$, observe $x_1$ successes in $n_1$ trials, update to $\text{Beta}(\alpha + x_1, \beta + n_1 - x_1)$. Now treat that as your new prior and observe $x_2$ in $n_2$ more trials: you reach $\text{Beta}(\alpha + x_1 + x_2, \beta + n_1 + n_2 - x_1 - x_2)$. Compare this with observing $x_1 + x_2$ successes in $n_1 + n_2$ trials in one shot: same answer. Bayesian updating is order-invariant and chunk-invariant. This is the formal content of "the posterior is the prior of the next experiment".

Conjugate priors for exponential families

The Beta–Binomial and Normal–Normal patterns are not isolated. For a one-parameter canonical exponential family

$$p_\theta(x) = h(x) \, \exp\!\big(\theta T(x) - A(\theta)\big),$$

the family of priors

$$\lambda_{\alpha, \beta}(\theta) \;\propto\; \exp\!\big(\alpha \theta - \beta A(\theta)\big), \qquad \theta \in \Omega,$$

is conjugate: the posterior after observing $X_1, \dots, X_n$ has parameters $(\alpha + \sum T(X_i),\, \beta + n)$. Bayes estimation in any exponential family thus reduces to bookkeeping on two parameters. Under squared-error loss the Bayes estimate of $A'(\Theta)$ — which by Chapter 2 equals the mean of $T(X)$ under $P_\theta$ — works out to a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample mean $\bar T = (T(X_1) + \cdots + T(X_n))/n$. The "weighted average of data and prior" pattern is universal.

Sensitivity, in one paragraph

How much should you worry about prior choice? In conjugate models with enough data, the data eventually dominates and reasonable priors converge to similar answers. But if $n$ is small, if the prior is heavy-tailed, or if you care about tail-probability questions ("what's the chance $\Theta$ exceeds the regulatory threshold?"), the prior matters and you should do a sensitivity analysis: rerun the inference under several plausible priors and see whether the conclusions move. If they do, that's a finding, not a bug.

7.3 Utility theory (optional)

We have been quietly assuming that "expected loss" is the right yardstick for comparing estimators. But why expectation? Why not, say, the median loss, or the worst-case loss? This optional section gives the philosophical answer: utility theory, which derives expected-value reasoning from a short list of axioms about preferences.

Lotteries and preferences

Let $\mathcal{R}$ be a set of possible rewards (money, fame, "no headache today" — anything you might care about). A lottery is a probability distribution on $\mathcal{R}$: gamble $P$ pays out a random reward drawn from $P$.

We compare lotteries with a relation $\preceq$, where $P_1 \preceq P_2$ means "I weakly prefer $P_2$ to $P_1$". For this to be a coherent preference, $\preceq$ should at minimum be a complete ordering:

  • Any two lotteries are comparable.
  • If $P_1 \preceq P_2$ and $P_2 \preceq P_3$, then $P_1 \preceq P_3$.
Definition 7.5 — utility function

A measurable function $U : \mathcal{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a utility function for $\preceq$ if

$$P_1 \preceq P_2 \quad \text{if and only if} \quad E_{P_1} U \leq E_{P_2} U,$$

whenever the expectations exist.

In words: $U$ converts preferences into numbers in a way that makes expected utility the right summary. Two natural questions follow. First, does such a $U$ always exist? Second, is it unique?

When utility functions don't exist

Not every preference has a utility function. Keener's amusing example: suppose $\mathcal{R}$ contains two rewards $a$ and $b$, and let $P_\theta$ be the lottery that pays $a$ with probability $\theta$ and $b$ otherwise. A gambler who loves suspense might rank $P_\theta$ by how close $\theta$ is to $1/2$, preferring the maximally uncertain lottery. There is no utility function compatible with this preference — expected utility is linear in $\theta$ and cannot peak in the middle.

The axioms that force expected utility

Roughly speaking, Keener's axioms A1–A4 say:

  • A1 (substitution). Mixing $P_1$ and $P_2$ with a common third lottery $P$ preserves strict preferences. If you preferred $P_2$ to $P_1$, you still prefer the mixture $\alpha P_2 + (1-\alpha) P$ to $\alpha P_1 + (1-\alpha) P$.
  • A2 (continuity). If $P_1 \prec P \prec P_2$, you can squeeze $P$ between $\alpha P_2 + (1-\alpha) P_1$ for some $\alpha \in (0,1)$, and similarly from above.
  • A3 (measurability). A technical condition ensuring the constructed $U$ is measurable.
  • A4 (reduction of compound lotteries). A two-stage lottery is equivalent to its collapsed one-stage version. There is no intrinsic preference for or against "extra randomisation steps".
Theorem 7.8 — existence of utility

Under axioms A1–A4, there exists a utility function $U$ such that for all bounded lotteries $P_1, P_2$,

$$P_1 \preceq P_2 \quad \text{if and only if} \quad E_{P_1} U \leq E_{P_2} U.$$

$U$ is unique up to positive affine transformations $U \mapsto a + bU$ with $b > 0$.

The construction in one sentence

Fix two reference rewards $s_0 \prec s_1$, set $U(s_0) = 0$ and $U(s_1) = 1$. For any other reward $r$, find the unique $\nu \in [0,1]$ for which the gamble $\nu s_1 + (1-\nu) s_0$ is equivalent to receiving $r$ for sure; define $U(r) = \nu$. Axiom A2 says this $\nu$ exists, A4 says the construction extends consistently to lotteries, and the rest is checking that $\preceq$ now equals expected-utility ordering.

Why we use loss instead of utility

Loss functions $L(\theta, d)$ are just negative utilities — minimising expected loss is maximising expected utility. The utility-theoretic justification means our reliance on $E\, L(\Theta, \delta)$ in (7.1) is not an arbitrary convention. Anyone whose preferences satisfy A1–A4 has no choice but to rank estimators by expected loss for some loss function $L$. The Bayesian framework is then a way of solving for the loss-minimising decision rule once $L$ is named.

Common pitfall — different priors, different answers

Two reasonable analysts can produce different Bayes estimators from the same data simply by choosing different priors. This is not a flaw — it is the framework being honest that subjective inputs matter. But it does mean a Bayesian analysis is incomplete without naming the prior. If a paper reports a "Bayesian estimate" without telling you the prior, treat the number with caution.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 7

The whole chapter, in seven bullets:

  1. A Bayesian model couples a prior $\Theta \sim \Lambda$ with a sampling model $X \mid \Theta \sim P_\theta$. The joint and marginal distributions are determined by these two pieces.
  2. The posterior distribution $\Lambda(\cdot \mid x)$ is the conditional distribution of $\Theta$ given $X = x$. In the dominated case it has density $\lambda(\theta \mid x) \propto p_\theta(x) \lambda(\theta)$.
  3. Theorem 7.1 (the main result). The Bayes estimator $\delta_\Lambda(x)$ is whatever value of $d$ minimises the posterior expected loss $E[L(\Theta, d) \mid X = x]$.
  4. Under squared-error loss, the Bayes estimator is the posterior mean of $g(\Theta)$.
  5. Conjugate priors keep the posterior in the same family as the prior. Beta is conjugate for Binomial; Normal is conjugate for Normal with known variance; every exponential family has a conjugate two-parameter prior family.
  6. The Bayes estimator is typically a weighted average of the data summary and the prior mean, with weights set by sample size and prior precision.
  7. Utility theory justifies expected-loss reasoning from preference axioms. The Bayes framework is the right framework when you accept those axioms.
