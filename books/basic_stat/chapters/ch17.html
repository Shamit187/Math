Chapter 17

Large-Sample Theory for Likelihood Ratio Tests

When the Neyman–Pearson machinery doesn't apply, one trick saves us: twice the log-likelihood ratio is approximately chi-square.

Book pages: 343–366 ~60 min reading time Prereqs: Chapters 9, 12, 13, 16

Why this chapter exists

Up through Chapter 13 we built optimal tests by hand: the Neyman–Pearson lemma for simple hypotheses, then uniformly most powerful tests for one-sided composite hypotheses in monotone-likelihood-ratio families, then unbiased tests for exponential families. Each construction was beautiful, and each came with a long list of requirements on the model and the hypothesis.

Real applied problems rarely satisfy all those requirements. A working statistician needs a hammer that always swings, even if it doesn't always strike with optimal force. The generalized likelihood ratio test (often abbreviated LRT) is that hammer.

The big idea in one sentence

Form the ratio "best likelihood I can achieve under the full model" over "best likelihood I can achieve under the null". If the null is true, that ratio shouldn't be much bigger than one; if the null is false, the ratio should grow. The chapter's central theorem says: take twice its logarithm, and under the null this statistic is approximately $\chi^2$ with degrees of freedom equal to how many dimensions the null forbids.

The cost of this universality is that everything we do is asymptotic. Sizes are only approximately $\alpha$, powers are only approximately computed, and the approximations rely on regularity conditions (smooth densities, interior parameter values, invertible Fisher information). When those conditions fail, the chi-square approximation can be badly wrong. We'll return to those traps at the end.

The chapter also introduces two siblings of the LRT — the Wald test and the score test. Asymptotically they are equivalent to the LRT, but each one has its own computational personality.

17.1 Generalized likelihood ratio tests

Setup and definition

We observe data $X_1, \dots, X_n$ i.i.d. with common density $f_\theta$, where $\theta$ lives in some parameter space $\Omega$. We want to test $$H_0 : \theta \in \Omega_0 \quad \text{versus} \quad H_1 : \theta \in \Omega_1,$$ where $\Omega_0 \cup \Omega_1 = \Omega$ and the two pieces are disjoint. The likelihood is $$L(\theta) = L(\theta \mid X_1, \dots, X_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n f_\theta(X_i).$$

Definition — generalized likelihood ratio statistic

The generalized likelihood ratio statistic is $$\lambda = \lambda(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \frac{\sup_{\theta \in \Omega_1} L(\theta)}{\sup_{\theta \in \Omega_0} L(\theta)}.$$ The likelihood ratio test rejects $H_0$ when $\lambda > k$ for some threshold $k$.

When $H_0$ and $H_1$ are both single points, this is exactly the Neyman–Pearson ratio and the resulting test is most powerful. For composite hypotheses, $\lambda$ is no longer guaranteed to be optimal, but it remains a sensible measure of how much better the data fit the alternative than the null.

A cosmetic but important change

The supremum over $\Omega_1$ in the numerator is awkward because $\Omega_1$ is usually an open set with no nice maximizer. In the situations we care about — where $\Omega_0$ is a smooth lower-dimensional surface inside $\Omega$ — the supremum over the full space $\Omega = \Omega_0 \cup \Omega_1$ equals the supremum over $\Omega_1$ almost always. So Keener replaces the definition with the equivalent $$\lambda = \frac{\sup_{\theta \in \Omega} L(\theta)}{\sup_{\theta \in \Omega_0} L(\theta)} = \frac{L(\hat\theta)}{L(\tilde\theta)} \tag{17.1}$$ where $\hat\theta$ is the unrestricted MLE and $\tilde\theta$ is the MLE constrained to $\Omega_0$. This is the formula we'll use from now on. Note $\lambda \geq 1$ always: the unrestricted maximum can only beat the restricted one.

What $\lambda$ is really measuring

$L(\hat\theta)$ is the highest the likelihood can get if we let $\theta$ be anything. $L(\tilde\theta)$ is the highest it can get if we force $\theta$ to obey $H_0$. The ratio asks: how much likelihood did the null hypothesis cost me? If the null is true, almost nothing; the two maxima will be close. If the null is false, the constraint hurts a lot and the ratio blows up.

Worked example: testing a normal mean

Example 17.1 — the LRT for $\mu = 0$ is the $t$-test

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be a sample from $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\theta = (\mu, \sigma)$. The log-likelihood is $$\ell(\theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(X_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}.$$

Maximizing without constraint gives the familiar MLEs $$\hat\mu = \bar X, \qquad \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2,$$ and substitution yields $L(\hat\mu, \hat\sigma) = e^{-n/2} / (2\pi\hat\sigma^2)^{n/2}$.

Under $H_0 : \mu = 0$, the MLE of $\mu$ is $\tilde\mu = 0$ (no choice) and maximizing over $\sigma$ gives $\tilde\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2$. Then $$\lambda = \frac{L(\hat\theta)}{L(\tilde\theta)} = \frac{\tilde\sigma^n}{\hat\sigma^n} = \left[\frac{\sum X_i^2}{\sum (X_i - \bar X)^2}\right]^{n/2}.$$ Using the identity $\sum X_i^2 = \sum(X_i - \bar X)^2 + n\bar X^2$, $$\lambda = \left[1 + \frac{T^2}{n-1}\right]^{n/2}, \quad T = \frac{\sqrt n\, \bar X}{S}.$$ So $\lambda$ is a strictly increasing function of $|T|$, where $T$ is the usual $t$-statistic. The LRT is exactly the two-sided $t$-test.

This example is the prototype: a familiar test, rediscovered as a likelihood ratio. The chapter's big claim is that we can play this game even when there is no familiar test to fall back on — and the resulting statistic will still have a known approximate distribution.

A second example: correlation

For a sample $(X_i, Y_i)$ from a bivariate normal, the LRT for $H_0: \rho = 0$ versus $H_1: \rho \neq 0$ works out to $$\log \lambda = -\frac{n}{2}\log(1 - \hat\rho^2),$$ where $\hat\rho$ is the sample correlation. Equivalently, the test rejects when $|\hat\rho|$ or the associated $t$-statistic $T = \hat\rho \sqrt{n-2}/\sqrt{1 - \hat\rho^2}$ exceeds a constant. Under $H_0$, $T$ has a $t$-distribution on $n - 2$ degrees of freedom — exactly the classical test, again recovered as an LRT.

A pattern is emerging

In both examples, the LRT collapses to a test we already knew, with an exact distribution we could write down. That's a coincidence of the normal model. For most other models we won't be so lucky — the distribution of $\lambda$ under $H_0$ won't have a clean closed form. What rescues us is Wilks' theorem: a single approximation that works asymptotically across every regular model.

17.2 Asymptotic distribution of $2\log\lambda$

The result we're aiming at

Let $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^r$ be open, and let $\Omega_0$ be a smooth $q$-dimensional submanifold of $\Omega$, with $q < r$. So the null hypothesis imposes $r - q$ constraints on $\theta$.

Wilks' theorem (informal statement)

Under regularity conditions on $f_\theta$ and consistency of both MLEs, if $\theta_0 \in \Omega_0$ then under $P_{\theta_0}$ $$2 \log \lambda_n \;\Rightarrow\; \chi^2_{r - q}.$$ More generally, along contiguous alternatives $\theta_n = \theta_0 + \Delta / \sqrt n$, $$2 \log \lambda_n \;\Rightarrow\; \chi^2_{r - q}(\delta^2),$$ a noncentral chi-square with the same degrees of freedom and noncentrality parameter $$\delta^2 = \Delta' Q_0 \big(P_0 + Q_0 I(\theta_0)^{-1} Q_0\big)^{-1} Q_0 \Delta,$$ where $P_0$ and $Q_0 = I - P_0$ project onto the tangent space of $\Omega_0$ at $\theta_0$ and its orthogonal complement.

Reading the formula in plain English

The degrees of freedom equal the number of restrictions $H_0$ imposes — formally, $r - q$, the codimension of $\Omega_0$ inside $\Omega$. Forget $r$ and $q$ for a moment: it's "the dimension you lose by believing $H_0$". One scalar constraint? One degree of freedom. Two free parameters set to zero? Two degrees of freedom. That's the rule to remember.

The noncentrality parameter $\delta^2$ measures how far the true parameter sits from $\Omega_0$, projected through the Fisher information geometry. If $\Delta$ is along the tangent of $\Omega_0$, then $Q_0 \Delta = 0$ and $\delta^2 = 0$; the local direction doesn't take us out of the null space. Only the orthogonal component of $\Delta$ — the part that genuinely leaves $\Omega_0$ — generates power.

The sketch of why this is true

The full derivation is long, but the architecture is worth understanding. Let $$Z_n = \sqrt n (\hat\theta_n - \theta_0), \qquad Y_n = \sqrt n (\tilde\theta_n - \theta_0).$$ Both are $\sqrt n$-scaled deviations of an MLE from the true parameter $\theta_0 \in \Omega_0$. The unrestricted MLE $\hat\theta_n$ can wander anywhere in $\Omega$, so $Z_n$ asymptotically lives in all of $\mathbb{R}^r$. The restricted MLE $\tilde\theta_n$ is forced to stay on $\Omega_0$, so $Y_n$ asymptotically lives in the tangent space $V_{\theta_0}$.

Three approximations carry the proof:

  1. A Taylor expansion of the score function gives $$\frac{1}{\sqrt n} \nabla \ell_n(\theta_0) = I(\theta_0) Z_n + o_p(1). \tag{17.13}$$
  2. A two-term Taylor expansion of the log-likelihood around $\theta_0$ produces $$2 \log \lambda_n = (Z_n - Y_n)' I(\theta_0) (Z_n - Y_n) + o_p(\|Y_n\|^2) + o_p(1). \tag{17.15}$$
  3. The geometry of $\Omega_0$ at $\theta_0$ (encoded in the projection $P_0$) ties $Y_n$ to $Z_n$ in a specific way: $Y_n$ is essentially the projection of $Z_n$ onto the tangent space, in the Fisher-information inner product.

Combining these, $Z_n - Y_n$ ends up being a vector in the orthogonal complement of the tangent space, of dimension $r - q$, with covariance such that the quadratic form $(Z_n - Y_n)' I(\theta_0) (Z_n - Y_n)$ is asymptotically the squared length of a standard $(r-q)$-dimensional Gaussian. That's a chi-square on $r - q$ degrees of freedom.

Geometric picture

Think of $\Omega$ as a flat sheet and $\Omega_0$ as a curve drawn on it. The unrestricted MLE $\hat\theta_n$ lands somewhere on the sheet near $\theta_0$. The restricted MLE $\tilde\theta_n$ is the closest point on the curve to that landing site, measured in the Fisher-information metric. The vector $\hat\theta_n - \tilde\theta_n$ is therefore (asymptotically) orthogonal to the curve. Its squared length, weighted by the Fisher information, is $2 \log \lambda_n$. And the squared length of a Gaussian vector of dimension $r - q$ is a chi-square on $r - q$ degrees of freedom. That is Wilks' theorem in one mental picture.

What the proof actually requires

Read this list once and don't worry about memorizing it — we'll just need to know what can go wrong:

  • $\Omega$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^r$.
  • $\Omega_0$ is a smooth submanifold of dimension $q < r$.
  • The densities $f_\theta$ are smooth enough for two Taylor expansions of the log-likelihood.
  • The Fisher information $I(\theta)$ is positive definite and continuous in $\theta$.
  • The MLEs $\hat\theta_n$ and $\tilde\theta_n$ are consistent.
  • $\theta_0 \in \Omega_0$ lies in the interior of $\Omega$.
Common pitfall — boundary parameters

The last condition is the one that breaks most often in practice. If $H_0$ forces a parameter onto the boundary of $\Omega$ — for example $H_0 : \sigma^2 = 0$ in a random-effects model, or $H_0 : p = 0$ for a binomial probability — then the MLE under $H_0$ can't wander in all directions, and Wilks' theorem fails. The asymptotic distribution of $2\log\lambda$ becomes a mixture of chi-squares (a "chi-bar-squared"). This is exactly what happens in problem 3 of the chapter (testing $\tau^2 = 0$ in a random-effects model).

The safe diagnostic: if the null forces a parameter to sit at the edge of where it's allowed, the chi-square approximation needs adjustment.

17.3 Examples

Three Poisson means

Let $X_i, Y_i, Z_i$ be independent Poisson observations with rates $\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3$, and test $$H_0 : \theta_1 + \theta_2 = \theta_3 \quad \text{versus} \quad H_1 : \theta_1 + \theta_2 \neq \theta_3.$$ Here $r = 3$ and $\Omega_0$ is the plane defined by one linear constraint, so $q = 2$. Wilks' theorem predicts $2\log\lambda \approx \chi^2_1$ under $H_0$.

Example 17.3 — the calculation

Maximizing without constraint gives the obvious MLEs $\hat\theta_1 = \bar X$, $\hat\theta_2 = \bar Y$, $\hat\theta_3 = \bar Z$. Under $H_0$ we substitute $\theta_3 = \theta_1 + \theta_2$ into the log-likelihood and solve the resulting two normal equations. The solution is $$\tilde\theta_1 = \frac{\bar X}{2}\!\left(\frac{\bar Z + \bar X + \bar Y}{\bar X + \bar Y}\right), \quad \tilde\theta_2 = \frac{\bar Y}{2}\!\left(\frac{\bar Z + \bar X + \bar Y}{\bar X + \bar Y}\right), \quad \tilde\theta_3 = \frac{\bar Z + \bar X + \bar Y}{2}.$$ After simplification (and using $\tilde\theta_1 + \tilde\theta_2 + \tilde\theta_3 = \hat\theta_1 + \hat\theta_2 + \hat\theta_3$), $$2\log\lambda = -2n\left[(\bar X + \bar Y) \log\!\left(\frac{\bar Z + \bar X + \bar Y}{2\bar X + 2\bar Y}\right) + \bar Z \log\!\left(\frac{\bar Z + \bar X + \bar Y}{2\bar Z}\right)\right].$$

Under $H_0$ this is approximately $\chi^2_1$. The book also computes the noncentrality parameter for power calculations: when $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 1$, $\theta_3 = 2.3$, and $n = 100$, one finds $\delta^2 = 2.09$ and the power against this alternative is about $0.31$ at level $5\%$.

Independence in a $2 \times 2$ table

Suppose $(N_{11}, N_{12}, N_{21}, N_{22}) \sim \text{Multinomial}(n; p_{11}, p_{12}, p_{21}, p_{22})$ with the probabilities summing to one. We test $$H_0 : p_{ij} = p_{i+} p_{+j} \quad \text{(rows and columns independent)}.$$ Because the four probabilities sum to one, the full parameter space has dimension $r = 3$. The null hypothesis imposes one nontrivial constraint (e.g. $p_{11} = p_{1+} p_{+1}$), so $q = 2$ and Wilks predicts $\chi^2_1$.

Example 17.4 — the LRT

The unrestricted MLEs are $\hat p_{ij} = N_{ij}/n$. Under independence, the constrained MLEs factorize as $\tilde p_{ij} = \hat p_{i+} \hat p_{+j}$ where the marginals match the observed marginals. Therefore $$2\log\lambda = 2 \sum_{i,j} N_{ij} \log\!\left(\frac{\hat p_{ij}}{\hat p_{i+} \hat p_{+j}}\right).$$ This is the well-known $G^2$ statistic for contingency tables. Under $H_0$ it has approximately a $\chi^2_1$ distribution.

The book uses the result to compute a sample size: to achieve $5\%$ level and $90\%$ power when $p_{11} = p_{22} = 0.3$ and $p_{12} = p_{21} = 0.2$, we need $n \approx 263$.

Pearson's $\chi^2$ falls out as a special case

The classical Pearson statistic is $$T = \sum_{i,j} \frac{(N_{ij} - n\hat p_{i+}\hat p_{+j})^2}{n \hat p_{i+} \hat p_{+j}}.$$ A two-term Taylor expansion of $2 \log \lambda$ around the point where $\hat p_{ij} = \hat p_{i+}\hat p_{+j}$ (which is "approximately true" under $H_0$) gives $$2 \log \lambda \approx T.$$ The first-order term vanishes; the second-order term reproduces Pearson's statistic exactly. So Pearson's $\chi^2$ and the likelihood ratio $\chi^2$ are the same statistic up to higher-order corrections, and they share the same asymptotic distribution.

Why two famous chi-square tests are really one

You learned Pearson's $\chi^2$ as "observed minus expected, squared, over expected". You learned the LRT as "twice the log of the likelihood ratio". These look unrelated. But Taylor-expand one around the null, and you get the other. The two statistics differ by $O_p(1/\sqrt n)$, which vanishes asymptotically. In a $2 \times 2$ table with $n = 263$, the two numbers will agree to roughly two significant figures.

A worked binomial-style test

Mini-example — binomial proportion $p = p_0$

Let $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$. Test $H_0 : p = p_0$ vs $H_1 : p \neq p_0$. Here $r = 1$ and $q = 0$, so the null pins $p$ to a single point and Wilks predicts $\chi^2_1$.

The unrestricted MLE is $\hat p = X/n$. The constrained "MLE" is $\tilde p = p_0$. The log-likelihood ratio is $$\log \lambda = X \log\!\left(\frac{\hat p}{p_0}\right) + (n - X) \log\!\left(\frac{1 - \hat p}{1 - p_0}\right),$$ so $$2 \log \lambda = 2n\left[\hat p \log\!\left(\frac{\hat p}{p_0}\right) + (1 - \hat p) \log\!\left(\frac{1 - \hat p}{1 - p_0}\right)\right].$$ Under $H_0$, $2\log\lambda \;\Rightarrow\; \chi^2_1$, and a Taylor expansion around $\hat p = p_0$ recovers the Pearson statistic $$T = \frac{(X - np_0)^2}{np_0(1 - p_0)} = \frac{n(\hat p - p_0)^2}{p_0(1 - p_0)},$$ which equals the square of the usual $z$-statistic. All three viewpoints converge.

17.4 Wald and score tests

Imagine writing $H_0$ as $$H_0 : g(\theta) = 0$$ for some smooth constraint function $g : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^{r-q}$ with $Dg(\theta)$ of full rank on $\Omega_0$. (For instance, "independence in a $2\times 2$ table" is the single constraint $g(\theta) = \theta_1 - (\theta_1 + \theta_2)(\theta_1 + \theta_3) = 0$.)

Three different "ways the null could fail" suggest three different test statistics.

The Wald test — "$g(\hat\theta_n)$ should be near zero"

If $H_0$ is true, then evaluating the constraint at the unrestricted MLE should give something near zero. By the delta method, $$\sqrt n\, g(\hat\theta_n) \;\Rightarrow\; N\!\left(0,\; Dg(\theta)\, I(\theta)^{-1}\, Dg(\theta)'\right),$$ so the natural quadratic form $$T_W = n \, g(\hat\theta_n)' \big[ Dg(\hat\theta_n)\, I(\hat\theta_n)^{-1}\, Dg(\hat\theta_n)' \big]^{-1} g(\hat\theta_n)$$ converges to $\chi^2_{r - q}$ under $H_0$.

The score test — "$\nabla \ell_n(\tilde\theta_n)$ should be near zero"

Alternative viewpoint: if $H_0$ is true, then $\tilde\theta_n$ already estimates $\theta$ well, and the gradient of the log-likelihood at $\tilde\theta_n$ should be small. Define $$T_S = \frac{1}{n} \nabla \ell_n(\tilde\theta_n)'\, I(\tilde\theta_n)^{-1}\, \nabla \ell_n(\tilde\theta_n).$$ A short Taylor argument shows $$T_S = 2 \log \lambda_n + o_p(1),$$ so under $H_0$ also $T_S \Rightarrow \chi^2_{r - q}$.

Putting them side by side

The trinity

Three statistics for the same null hypothesis, all converging to $\chi^2_{r-q}$:

  • LRT: $2\log\lambda_n = 2[\ell_n(\hat\theta_n) - \ell_n(\tilde\theta_n)]$. Needs both MLEs.
  • Wald: $T_W$ uses only $\hat\theta_n$, the unrestricted MLE.
  • Score: $T_S$ uses only $\tilde\theta_n$, the restricted MLE.

All three are asymptotically equivalent under $H_0$ and under contiguous alternatives; the pairwise differences are $o_p(1)$.

A picture that ties them together

Plot the log-likelihood $\ell(\theta)$ against $\theta$. The graph is a hill, and consider testing $H_0 : \theta = 0$ (just one scalar to fix intuition).

  • The LRT measures the vertical drop in $\ell$ from the peak to the height at $\theta = 0$.
  • The Wald test measures the horizontal distance from $\hat\theta_n$ to $0$ (scaled by Fisher information).
  • The score test measures the slope of $\ell$ at $\theta = 0$ (scaled by Fisher information).

For a perfectly quadratic log-likelihood (the asymptotic limit, by Taylor expansion), drop $=$ distance squared $=$ slope squared, all in appropriate units. Hence the trinity.

They agree asymptotically — but not in finite samples

This is the single most important practical point. In any specific dataset, the three statistics will give three different numbers. Sometimes the LRT rejects when the Wald test does not, or vice versa. The differences vanish as $n \to \infty$, but in real applied work with $n = 30$ or $n = 100$, they can disagree.

When this happens, the LRT is typically the most reliable (its sampling distribution is closer to chi-square in moderate samples), and the Wald test can be especially unstable when the parameter is near the boundary or the Fisher information is poorly estimated. Many statisticians default to the LRT for that reason.

When to reach for which

  • LRT: most reliable in moderate samples. Use when you can compute both MLEs comfortably.
  • Wald: handy when the constrained MLE $\tilde\theta_n$ is hard to compute but the unrestricted fit is easy. Most common in regression output (every reported $z$-statistic in a generalized linear model is a Wald statistic).
  • Score: handy when the unrestricted fit is expensive but the restricted fit is cheap — for instance when testing whether a complicated model improves on a simple one. But the score test's power can be poor at distant alternatives.
Pitfalls of the Wald test

The Wald test is not invariant under reparameterization. Testing $H_0 : \theta = 1$ in a Bernoulli model on the logit scale versus on the probability scale gives different Wald statistics. The LRT is invariant. This is one reason the Wald test sometimes misbehaves on weird parameterizations of the same null.

Also, when $H_0$ pushes a parameter to the boundary, the Wald statistic can be exactly zero by construction (because $g(\hat\theta_n)$ is forced to one side), and the test loses all power. Use the LRT instead, with the corrected boundary distribution.

Bahadur efficiency — a footnote

For a fixed alternative $\theta \in \Omega_1$, all three tests have power tending to one. The rate of convergence is exponential, and a deep result (Bahadur) says the LRT achieves the optimal rate among all reasonable tests in this regime. So the LRT is "Bahadur efficient". In practice, this matters less than the moderate-sample behavior — which is what makes the difference in real applications.

Summary

The chapter, distilled into eight bullets:

  1. The generalized likelihood ratio is $\lambda = L(\hat\theta) / L(\tilde\theta)$ — best unrestricted fit over best fit under $H_0$. Always $\geq 1$.
  2. Wilks' theorem: under regularity, $2 \log \lambda_n \Rightarrow \chi^2_{r-q}$ under $H_0$, where $r - q$ is the number of constraints $H_0$ imposes (the codimension of $\Omega_0$ in $\Omega$).
  3. Under contiguous alternatives $\theta_n = \theta_0 + \Delta/\sqrt n$, the limit becomes the noncentral chi-square $\chi^2_{r-q}(\delta^2)$, with $\delta^2$ a quadratic form in $\Delta$ involving the Fisher information.
  4. The Wald test uses only $\hat\theta_n$; the score test uses only $\tilde\theta_n$. Both also converge to $\chi^2_{r-q}$ under $H_0$.
  5. All three statistics are asymptotically equivalent: pairwise differences are $o_p(1)$. In finite samples they disagree, and the LRT is usually the most reliable.
  6. Pearson's chi-square statistic is a Taylor approximation to $2 \log \lambda$ — same asymptotic distribution.
  7. The classical $t$-test, $z$-test for a proportion, and $G^2$ for contingency tables are all special cases of the LRT.
  8. Pitfalls: the chi-square approximation requires interior parameter values, smooth densities, and positive-definite Fisher information. Boundary nulls (variance components, mixture weights) need a corrected distribution. Wald tests are not reparameterization-invariant.

You should be able to answer, without looking back:

  • Why does the degrees-of-freedom equal $r - q$ rather than $r$ or $q$ alone? What does "the dimension you lose" mean?
  • Sketch the log-likelihood as a hill. Mark on it the three quantities measured by the LRT, the Wald test, and the score test.
  • Why are the three tests asymptotically equivalent? In what sense can they disagree in finite samples?
  • State one situation in which Wilks' theorem fails, and explain why.
  • How does the noncentrality parameter $\delta^2$ depend on the direction of $\Delta$ relative to the tangent space of $\Omega_0$? What does that direction-dependence mean for the power of the test?

If any of these feel shaky, return to Section 17.2 and re-trace the geometric picture: $\hat\theta_n - \tilde\theta_n$ is asymptotically a Gaussian vector orthogonal to $\Omega_0$, and Wilks' theorem is the statement that its squared length, in the Fisher-information metric, is a chi-square.
