Chapter 9

Estimating Equations and Maximum Likelihood

The longest chapter in the book — and the one that ties together every asymptotic result you have seen so far. Read it slowly; it is worth it.

Book pages: 151–194 ~90 min reading time Prereqs: Chapters 4, 7, 8

Why this chapter is the keystone

Up to now you have met estimators one at a time: the sample mean, the MLE for a Bernoulli proportion, least squares, the method of moments. Each came with its own little asymptotic argument. Chapter 9 reveals that nearly all of them are instances of a single template:

$$\text{the estimator } \hat\theta_n \text{ solves } \sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi(X_i; \hat\theta_n) = 0.$$

Different choices of $\psi$ give different estimators. When $\psi = \nabla_\theta \log f_\theta$, the solution is the MLE. When $\psi(x, t) = x - t$, the solution is the sample mean. When $\psi(x, t) = \mathrm{sign}(x - t)$, the solution is the sample median. The method of moments, Huber's robust estimator, the Bayes estimator under regularity — all sit inside this frame.

The big idea in one sentence

If $\hat\theta_n$ solves $\sum \psi(X_i; \hat\theta_n) = 0$ and the average $\bar\psi_n(t)$ converges uniformly to its expectation $\lambda(t) = E\psi(X; t)$, then $\hat\theta_n$ is consistent for the root $c$ of $\lambda$, and a single Taylor expansion gives its asymptotic normal distribution.

The chapter follows that idea in nine sections. Section 9.1 builds the technical engine — a law of large numbers that holds uniformly in $t$. Sections 9.2–9.3 apply it to maximum likelihood. Sections 9.4–9.5 turn limiting distributions into usable confidence intervals. Section 9.6 introduces the EM algorithm for cases where the MLE has no closed form. Section 9.7 lifts everything into $\mathbb{R}^p$. Section 9.8 generalises beyond the likelihood (Huber's M-estimators). Section 9.9 sketches how the same template survives when the data are no longer i.i.d.

9.1 Weak law for random functions

What is wrong with pointwise convergence?

The ordinary weak law says: for each fixed $t$, the average $\bar W_n(t) = n^{-1}\sum W_i(t)$ converges in probability to $E W(t)$. That looks like enough — but it is not. When we plug a random argument $\hat\theta_n$ into $\bar W_n$, pointwise convergence is too weak to conclude $\bar W_n(\hat\theta_n) \to E W(\hat\theta_n)$. We need convergence that is uniform over $t$.

Set-up

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d., let $K \subset \mathbb{R}^p$ be compact, and let $h(t, x)$ be continuous in $t$ for every $x$. Define the random functions $$W_i(t) = h(t, X_i), \qquad t \in K.$$ Each $W_i$ lives in $C(K)$, the space of continuous functions on $K$, equipped with the supremum norm $$\|w\|_\infty = \sup_{t \in K} |w(t)|.$$

Why $C(K)$?

$C(K)$ is a complete normed linear space — a Banach space. Distances are measured by the worst-case gap between two functions. "Uniformly close" means small in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$. The whole point of working in $C(K)$ is that this norm gives us a single number measuring closeness over the entire compact set $K$ at once.

The uniform weak law

Theorem 9.2

If $W, W_1, W_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. random functions in $C(K)$ with mean $\mu(t) = E W(t)$ and $E\|W\|_\infty < \infty$, then $$\|\bar W_n - \mu\|_\infty \xrightarrow{p} 0.$$

The strategy of the proof is worth absorbing because the same logic recurs everywhere in Chapter 9. Compactness of $K$ lets us cover it by finitely many small balls. Continuity of each $W_i$ lets us bound the variation of $W_i$ inside each ball. The ordinary weak law handles the finitely many ball-centres. Glue the pieces together and uniform convergence falls out.

Why uniform convergence is the gold standard

Theorem 9.4 — three consequences

Suppose $G_n \in C(K)$, $K$ compact, and $\|G_n - g\|_\infty \xrightarrow{p} 0$ with $g$ deterministic.

  1. Plug-in: if $t_n \xrightarrow{p} t^*$, then $G_n(t_n) \xrightarrow{p} g(t^*)$.
  2. Argmax: if $g$ has a unique maximiser $t^*$ and $t_n$ maximises $G_n$, then $t_n \xrightarrow{p} t^*$.
  3. Root: if $K \subset \mathbb{R}$ and $g(t) = 0$ has a unique root $t^*$, and $t_n$ solves $G_n(t_n) = 0$, then $t_n \xrightarrow{p} t^*$.
Read these three slowly

Assertion (1) says we may substitute random arguments into uniformly convergent functions. Assertion (2) is the consistency theorem for MLE in disguise. Assertion (3) is the consistency theorem for M-estimators. The entire chapter is going to be applications of these three facts.

Common pitfall

Pointwise convergence is not enough. Problem 9.1 in the book constructs $G_n(t) = nZ_n(1-t)t^n - t$ where $G_n(t) \xrightarrow{p} -t$ for every fixed $t$, yet the maximiser of $G_n$ does not converge to the maximiser of the limit. The lesson: when you let $t$ depend on $n$, only uniform convergence saves you.

9.2 Consistency of the maximum likelihood estimator

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. with density $f_\theta$, $\theta \in \Omega$. The log-likelihood from the first $n$ observations is $$\ell_n(\omega) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log f_\omega(X_i).$$ We write $\omega$ for the dummy argument and reserve $\theta$ for the true value. The MLE $\hat\theta_n$ maximises $\ell_n$.

Definition 9.7 — Kullback–Leibler information

$$I(\theta, \omega) = E_\theta \log \!\left[ \frac{f_\theta(X)}{f_\omega(X)} \right].$$ It is the expected log-likelihood-ratio between the true model and a candidate $\omega$.

Why this quantity matters

Jensen's inequality gives $I(\theta, \omega) \geq 0$ with equality iff $P_\theta = P_\omega$. So as a function of $\omega$, $-I(\theta, \omega)$ has a strict maximum at $\omega = \theta$. If we can show the random function $\bar W_n(\omega) = n^{-1}\ell_n(\omega) - n^{-1}\ell_n(\theta)$ converges uniformly to $-I(\theta, \omega)$, then by Theorem 9.4(2) the maximiser of $\bar W_n$ — which is the MLE — must converge to $\theta$.

Theorem 9.9 — Wald consistency

If $\Omega$ is compact, $f_\omega(x)$ is continuous in $\omega$ for a.e. $x$, $P_\omega \neq P_\theta$ for $\omega \neq \theta$, and $E_\theta \|W\|_\infty < \infty$ where $W(\omega) = \log[f_\omega(X)/f_\theta(X)]$, then under $P_\theta$, $\hat\theta_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta$.

Theorem 9.11 extends this to $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^p$ provided the density tails off at infinity in a controlled way. The fundamental engine — uniform LLN plus uniqueness of the maximum — is unchanged.

Common pitfall — MLE can fail to exist

Compactness and continuity hide real-world traps. For the three-parameter lognormal $X = \gamma + e^Z$ with $Z \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the profile log-likelihood diverges to $+\infty$ as $\gamma \uparrow X_{(1)}$ — so the MLE of $\gamma$ does not exist (Problem 9.5). For uniform $(0, \theta)$, the MLE is $X_{(n)}$ and the regularity assumption "support independent of $\theta$" fails. In such non-regular cases the theory of Sections 9.2–9.3 simply does not apply.

9.3 Limiting distribution for the MLE

We now ask the second question: how fast does $\hat\theta_n$ converge, and to what limit law? The answer is the celebrated asymptotic normality theorem.

Theorem 9.14 — asymptotic normality of the MLE

Under regularity (continuous second derivative of $\log f_\theta$, Fisher information finite and reachable through either $E[(W')^2]$ or $-E[W'']$, integrable local Hessian, and consistency), for any $\theta$ in the interior of $\Omega$,

$$\sqrt{n}\, (\hat\theta_n - \theta) \;\Rightarrow\; N\!\left(0, \tfrac{1}{I(\theta)}\right).$$

The proof, slowly

Because $\hat\theta_n$ is consistent and interior, with probability tending to one it solves the score equation $\ell_n'(\hat\theta_n) = 0$. Equivalently $\bar W_n'(\hat\theta_n) = 0$, where $\bar W_n = n^{-1}\ell_n$. Taylor-expand $\bar W_n'$ around $\theta$:

$$0 = \bar W_n'(\hat\theta_n) = \bar W_n'(\theta) + \bar W_n''(\tilde\theta_n)(\hat\theta_n - \theta),$$

where $\tilde\theta_n$ is between $\theta$ and $\hat\theta_n$. Solving and multiplying by $\sqrt n$,

$$\sqrt n (\hat\theta_n - \theta) = \frac{\sqrt n\, \bar W_n'(\theta)}{-\bar W_n''(\tilde\theta_n)}. \tag{9.1}$$

The numerator and denominator have separate fates.

  • Numerator. $\sqrt n \bar W_n'(\theta) = n^{-1/2} \sum_i \partial_\theta \log f_\theta(X_i)$. The summands are i.i.d., mean zero (a consequence of differentiating under the integral), and have variance $I(\theta)$. The CLT gives $\sqrt n \bar W_n'(\theta) \Rightarrow N(0, I(\theta))$.
  • Denominator. $\bar W_n''(\tilde\theta_n)$. The uniform LLN of Section 9.1 gives $\bar W_n'' \to E W''$ uniformly on a neighbourhood of $\theta$. Combine with $\tilde\theta_n \to \theta$ via Theorem 9.4(1): $\bar W_n''(\tilde\theta_n) \xrightarrow{p} E W''(\theta) = -I(\theta)$.

Slutsky's theorem (Theorem 8.13) then gives the ratio $\Rightarrow N(0, I(\theta))/I(\theta) = N(0, 1/I(\theta))$.

Pause — why is the MLE asymptotically efficient?

Recall the Cramér–Rao bound from Chapter 4: any unbiased estimator of $\theta$ has variance at least $1/(n I(\theta))$. Theorem 9.14 says the MLE attains exactly that variance asymptotically. That is what "asymptotic efficiency" means: in the limit, the MLE saturates the information bound. No regular estimator can do strictly better.

The deeper reason: the score function $\ell_n'(\theta)$ has variance $n I(\theta)$ (Fisher's identity), and the MLE is, to first order, the linearisation of the inverse of this score. The Newton step from $\theta$ to $\hat\theta_n$ has length $\propto \ell_n'(\theta)/I(\theta)$, scaling its variance down to $1/(n I(\theta))$.

Worked example — Bernoulli

Worked example — MLE for Bernoulli

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$, $p \in (0,1)$. The log-likelihood is $\ell_n(p) = (\sum X_i)\log p + (n - \sum X_i)\log(1-p)$. Differentiating and setting equal to zero gives $\hat p_n = \bar X_n$. The Fisher information is $$I(p) = \frac{1}{p(1-p)},$$ obtained either by $-E[\partial_p^2 \log f_p(X)]$ or directly from $\mathrm{Var}(X)$. Theorem 9.14 then delivers $$\sqrt n (\hat p_n - p) \;\Rightarrow\; N\!\big(0, p(1-p)\big),$$ which is also exactly the CLT applied to $\bar X_n$ — a healthy consistency check.

Worked example — Normal mean

Worked example — MLE for $N(\mu, \sigma_0^2)$ with known variance

$\log f_\mu(x) = -\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma_0^2} + \text{const}$. The MLE is $\hat\mu_n = \bar X_n$. The Fisher information is $I(\mu) = 1/\sigma_0^2$, so $\sqrt n(\hat\mu_n - \mu) \Rightarrow N(0, \sigma_0^2)$. Again, the CLT delivers this directly — but the point of Theorem 9.14 is that we did not need to be clever; we just turned the crank.

Worked example — Poisson

Worked example — MLE for Poisson$(\theta)$

$\ell_n(\theta) = (\sum X_i)\log\theta - n\theta + \text{const}$. The score is $\ell_n'(\theta) = n\bar X_n/\theta - n$, so $\hat\theta_n = \bar X_n$. The Fisher information is $I(\theta) = 1/\theta$ (compute $-E[-X/\theta^2] = 1/\theta$). Therefore $\sqrt n(\bar X_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N(0, \theta)$.

Common pitfall — uniqueness of the root

The score equation $\ell_n'(\theta) = 0$ may have multiple solutions. Theorem 9.4(3) requires a unique root of the limiting function — but for finite $n$ there can be several roots, and the global maximum of $\ell_n$ may not be the one nearest to $\theta$. In practice one solves the score equation numerically and verifies it is the global maximum, not merely a critical point.

9.4 Confidence intervals

A point estimator tells you a guess; a confidence interval tells you how much to trust the guess.

Definition 9.17 — confidence interval

Statistics $\delta_0, \delta_1$ give a $1-\alpha$ confidence interval for $g(\theta)$ if $$P_\theta\big(g(\theta) \in (\delta_0, \delta_1)\big) \geq 1 - \alpha \quad \text{for all } \theta \in \Omega.$$ More generally a random set $S(X)$ is a $1-\alpha$ confidence region if $P_\theta(g(\theta) \in S) \geq 1 - \alpha$ for all $\theta$.

Reading the statement carefully

The probability is taken over the data $X$, not over $\theta$. Once you observe $X = x$, the interval $(\delta_0(x), \delta_1(x))$ is a fixed set of real numbers; the parameter $\theta$ is fixed too, though unknown. The "$1 - \alpha$" is a long-run frequency property of the procedure, not a posterior probability for $\theta$.

Pivots

A pivot is a function of the data and the parameter whose distribution does not depend on the parameter. Pivots are the engine that turns probability statements about random variables into confidence statements about parameters.

Example 9.19 — $t$-interval for the normal mean

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)$. From Section 4.3, $\bar X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ and $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$ are independent. The ratio $$T = \frac{\bar X - \mu}{S/\sqrt n} \sim t_{n-1}$$ is a pivot. Inverting $-t_{\alpha/2, n-1} < T < t_{\alpha/2, n-1}$ gives the classical $t$-interval $$\left(\bar X - t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\, \frac{S}{\sqrt n}, \; \bar X + t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\, \frac{S}{\sqrt n}\right),$$ which has exact coverage $1 - \alpha$.

Pivots also exist for $\sigma^2$ (using $V = (n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$), for variance ratios $\sigma_x/\sigma_y$ in two-sample problems (using $F$ distributions, Problem 9.8), and for any location/scale family (Problem 9.11). Where exact pivots exist, asymptotics is not needed.

9.5 Asymptotic confidence intervals

Most parametric problems do not admit an exact pivot. The MLE asymptotic normality of Theorem 9.14 gives an approximate pivot

$$\sqrt{n I(\theta)}\,(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \;\Rightarrow\; N(0, 1), \tag{9.3}$$

whose limiting distribution is parameter-free. This single fact spawns three different intervals.

Form 1 — implicit region using $I(\theta)$

$$S = \left\{ \theta : \sqrt{n I(\theta)}\, |\hat\theta_n - \theta| < z_{\alpha/2} \right\}. \tag{9.4}$$

This set may not even be an interval, and inverting the inequality may be algebraically painful.

Form 2 — plug-in using $I(\hat\theta_n)$

By continuity, $I(\hat\theta_n)/I(\theta) \xrightarrow{p} 1$, so we can substitute the estimate:

$$\left(\hat\theta_n - \frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n I(\hat\theta_n)}}, \; \hat\theta_n + \frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{n I(\hat\theta_n)}}\right). \tag{9.5}$$

This is the textbook "Wald interval". It always is an interval; closed form; easy.

Form 3 — observed information

Argue that confidence intervals should depend only on the actual shape of the likelihood, not on population averages. Replacing the expected information $I(\hat\theta_n)$ by the curvature at the MLE, $-\ell_n''(\hat\theta_n)$,

$$\left(\hat\theta_n - \frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{-\ell_n''(\hat\theta_n)}}, \; \hat\theta_n + \frac{z_{\alpha/2}}{\sqrt{-\ell_n''(\hat\theta_n)}}\right). \tag{9.7}$$

The denominator $-\ell_n''(\hat\theta_n)$ is the observed Fisher information.

Form 4 — profile / likelihood-ratio

By a second Taylor expansion, $2\ell_n(\hat\theta_n) - 2\ell_n(\theta) \approx \big[\sqrt{-\ell_n''(\theta^*_n)}(\theta - \hat\theta_n)\big]^2 \Rightarrow \chi^2_1$. Inverting,

$$S = \left\{ \theta : 2\ell_n(\hat\theta_n) - 2\ell_n(\theta) < z_{\alpha/2}^2 \right\}. \tag{9.8}$$

This is the profile likelihood region. It uses the full curve of $\ell_n$ — not just its peak and second derivative — and tends to give better small-sample coverage in non-quadratic models.

Worked example 9.20 — Poisson revisited

With $X_1, \dots, X_n \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Poisson}(\theta)$, $\hat\theta = \bar X$, $I(\theta) = 1/\theta$, and $-\ell_n''(\hat\theta) = n\bar X/\hat\theta^2 = n/\bar X = nI(\hat\theta)$. So forms 2 and 3 coincide: $$(\bar X - z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\bar X/n}, \; \bar X + z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\bar X/n}).$$ Note this can go negative when $\bar X$ is small — a famous defect of the Wald interval. The profile form (9.8) stays inside $(0, \infty)$ but its endpoints must be found numerically.

When do all four agree?

For an exponential family in canonical form, the log-likelihood is exactly quadratic in the canonical parameter, so all four intervals coincide. In non-exponential families they diverge, and the profile interval is usually the most accurate at moderate $n$.

9.6 EM algorithm — estimation from incomplete data

The EM algorithm (Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977) computes MLEs when the data are incomplete in some way — censored, rounded, missing-at-random, or augmented latent variables. The "full data" $X$ has density $h(x) e^{\eta T(x) - A(\eta)}$ from an exponential family; the observed data are $Y = g(X)$ where $g$ is many-to-one, so $X$ cannot be recovered from $Y$.

The algorithm

Start from a guess $\hat\eta_0$. Iterate two steps until convergence:

  • E-step. Impute the sufficient statistic: $$T_{k+1} = E_{\hat\eta_k}[T(X) \mid Y].$$
  • M-step. Maximise as if $T_{k+1}$ were observed: $$\hat\eta_{k+1} = \psi(T_{k+1}),$$ where $\psi$ is the inverse of $A'$.
Why this works

At any fixed point $\tilde\eta = \psi(e(Y, \tilde\eta))$, the derivative of the observed-data log-likelihood $\log f_\eta(Y)$ vanishes: differentiating $f_\eta(Y) = \int_{\mathcal{X}(Y)} h e^{\eta T - A}\, d\mu$ gives $\partial_\eta \log f_\eta(Y) = e(Y, \eta) - A'(\eta)$, which is zero at $\tilde\eta$. So EM fixed points are critical points of the observed log-likelihood. A theorem of Dempster–Laird–Rubin (extended by Wu, 1983) shows each iteration cannot decrease $\log f_\eta(Y)$.

Example 9.23 — rounding

$X_1, \dots, X_n \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Exp}(\eta)$, but we only observe $Y_i = \lfloor X_i \rfloor$. The full-data sufficient statistic is $T = -\sum X_i$, and the conditional expectation works out to $$E_\eta[X_i \mid Y_i = y] = y + \frac{e^\eta - 1 - \eta}{\eta(e^\eta - 1)}.$$ The EM recursion becomes $$\hat\eta_{j+1} = -\frac{n}{T_{j+1}}, \qquad T_{j+1} = -n\!\left[\bar Y + \frac{e^{\hat\eta_j} - 1 - \hat\eta_j}{\hat\eta_j (e^{\hat\eta_j} - 1)}\right].$$ Here the marginal $Y_i$ is geometric with $p = 1 - e^{-\eta}$, so we can verify the answer in closed form: $\hat\eta = \log(1 + 1/\bar Y)$.

Common pitfall — local maxima and slow convergence

EM is monotone in $\log f_\eta(Y)$ but is not guaranteed to find the global maximum. If the likelihood has multiple modes, EM may converge to a local maximum; the standard remedy is to restart from several initial values. Convergence is also typically only linear — the error shrinks by a constant factor each iteration. Newton–Raphson on $\log f_\eta(Y)$ would converge quadratically, but is less stable and needs second derivatives of the observed-data likelihood.

9.7 Limiting distributions in higher dimensions

In multivariate problems $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$, the Fisher information is a $p \times p$ matrix

$$I(\theta) = \mathrm{Cov}_\theta\!\big(\nabla_\theta \log f_\theta(X)\big) = -E_\theta\, \nabla^2_\theta \log f_\theta(X).$$

Multivariate normal — the limit distribution

The multivariate normal $N(\mu, \Sigma)$ has moment generating function $M_X(t) = \exp(t'\mu + t'\Sigma t/2)$. Built as $X = \mu + AZ$ for $Z$ a vector of i.i.d. standard normals and $AA' = \Sigma$, it inherits these properties:

  • $EX = \mu$, $\mathrm{Cov}(X) = \Sigma$.
  • Linear images stay normal: $b + AX \sim N(b + A\mu, A\Sigma A')$.
  • When $\Sigma$ is positive definite, $X$ has Lebesgue density $(2\pi)^{-p/2}(\det\Sigma)^{-1/2}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12(x-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\big)$.
Multivariate CLT (Theorem 9.27)

If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. random vectors with mean $\mu$ and covariance $\Sigma$, then $\sqrt n(\bar X - \mu) \Rightarrow N(0, \Sigma)$.

Asymptotic normality of the MLE in $\mathbb{R}^p$

The univariate proof transcribes almost verbatim with vectors and matrices. With $\hat\theta_n$ consistent and interior, Taylor expansion of the score gives

$$\sqrt n(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \approx \left[-\tfrac{1}{n}\nabla^2 \ell_n(\theta)\right]^{-1} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}\nabla \ell_n(\theta). \tag{9.12}$$

The CLT handles the score $\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\nabla \ell_n(\theta) \Rightarrow N(0, I(\theta))$. The uniform LLN handles the Hessian $-\frac{1}{n}\nabla^2 \ell_n(\theta) \xrightarrow{p} I(\theta)$. Continuity of $A \mapsto A^{-1}$ (for nonsingular matrices) plus Theorem 9.30 give

$$\sqrt n(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \;\Rightarrow\; N\!\big(0, I(\theta)^{-1}\big).$$

The multivariate delta method

Proposition 9.31

If $g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is differentiable and $\sqrt n(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N(0, I(\theta)^{-1})$, then $$\sqrt n\big(g(\hat\theta_n) - g(\theta)\big) \;\Rightarrow\; N\!\big(0, \nabla g(\theta)' I(\theta)^{-1} \nabla g(\theta)\big).$$ Vector-valued $g$ gives covariance $D g(\theta)\, I(\theta)^{-1}\, Dg(\theta)'$.

This is how confidence intervals for derived parameters such as $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$, $\log(\theta)$, or odds ratios get their asymptotic variances.

9.8 M-estimators for a location parameter

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. from some distribution $Q$, and let $\rho$ be convex with $\rho(x) \to \infty$ as $|x| \to \infty$. The M-estimator $T_n$ minimises $$H(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \rho(X_i - t).$$ Equivalently, with $\psi = \rho'$, $T_n$ solves the estimating equation $$\bar W_n(t) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \psi(X_i - t) = 0.$$

Three familiar estimators are M-estimators
  • $\rho(x) = x^2$, $\psi(x) = 2x$ gives $T_n = \bar X_n$, the sample mean.
  • $\rho(x) = |x|$, $\psi(x) = \mathrm{sign}(x)$ gives $T_n = \mathrm{median}(X_1, \dots, X_n)$.
  • If $Q$ has log-concave density $f(x - \theta)$ and $\rho = -\log f$, $T_n$ is the MLE for $\theta$.

Consistency and asymptotic normality

Define $\lambda(t) = E\psi(X - t)$. Convexity of $\rho$ makes $\psi$ nondecreasing, so $\lambda$ is nonincreasing; under mild integrability $\lambda$ has a unique root $c$. Theorem 9.4(3) applied to the uniformly convergent average $\bar W_n$ gives $T_n \xrightarrow{p} c$.

For asymptotic normality, Taylor-expand $\bar W_n$ around $c$: $0 = \bar W_n(T_n) = \bar W_n(c) + (T_n - c)\bar W_n'(t_n^*)$, so

$$\sqrt n(T_n - c) = -\frac{\sqrt n \bar W_n(c)}{\bar W_n'(t_n^*)}.$$

The numerator is asymptotically $N(0, E\psi(X-c)^2)$ by the CLT; the denominator converges in probability to $E\psi'(X - c) = \lambda'(c)$. Therefore

$$\sqrt n(T_n - c) \;\Rightarrow\; N\!\left(0, \; \frac{E\psi(X - c)^2}{\lambda'(c)^2}\right).$$
Pause — same template, different $\psi$

Notice how the proof is structurally identical to the MLE proof in Section 9.3. Both estimators solve $\bar W_n = 0$ (M-estimators by definition, MLEs because they sit at the peak). Both inherit asymptotic normality from a Taylor expansion plus the uniform LLN. The asymptotic variance is always $E\psi^2 / (\lambda')^2$. The MLE just happens to have $E\psi^2 = \lambda' = I(\theta)$, making both factors equal and producing the efficient variance $1/I(\theta)$.

Huber's M-estimator

When the model is $N(\theta, 1)$, the sample mean is fully efficient and the median is wasteful. But if the model is contaminated, $X \sim (1 - \epsilon) N(\theta, 1) + \epsilon Q^*$ with $Q^*$ symmetric about $\theta$, then $\mathrm{Var}(X)$ can be enormous and $\bar X_n$ becomes terrible. The sample median is unaffected but throws away information when the contamination is small.

Worked example — Huber's saddle-point estimator

Huber (1964) asked: what $\psi$ minimises the worst-case asymptotic variance over the class $\mathcal{C}_\epsilon$ of contaminated distributions? The answer (Theorem 9.34) is the function $$\psi_0(t) = \begin{cases} t, & |t| \leq k, \\ k\, \mathrm{sign}(t), & |t| > k, \end{cases}$$ with corresponding convex loss $\rho_0(t) = \tfrac12 t^2$ for $|t|\leq k$ and $\rho_0(t) = k|t| - \tfrac12 k^2$ for $|t| > k$. The constant $k$ solves $$\frac{1}{1 - \epsilon} = P(|Z| < k) + \frac{2\phi(k)}{k}, \qquad Z \sim N(0,1).$$ The estimator behaves like the mean for "good" observations and like the median for outliers — the best of both worlds. It is the canonical example of a robust estimator.

9.9 Models with dependent observations

All of the asymptotic theory so far has rested on the i.i.d. assumption. Real data — time series, Markov chains, spatial fields — are not i.i.d. The good news: the basic argument template survives, provided we upgrade the two main probability tools.

Stationarity, ergodicity, and the ergodic theorem

Definition — strictly stationary process

A process $\{X_n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is strictly stationary if for every $k \geq 1$ and every $n$, $(X_1, \dots, X_k) \sim (X_{n+1}, \dots, X_{n+k})$. It is ergodic if every shift-invariant event has probability 0 or 1.

Theorem 9.38 — ergodic theorem

If $X$ is stationary ergodic and $E|g(X)| < \infty$, then $$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} g(T^j X) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} E g(X).$$

The ergodic theorem replaces the strong law: it is exactly as strong, but covers dependent data. Consistency of the MLE for time-series models follows from arguments structurally identical to those in Section 9.2, with $\ell_n(\theta)/n \to E\big[\log f_\theta(X_0 \mid X_{-1}, X_{-2}, \dots)\big]$ via the ergodic theorem, and this expectation maximised uniquely at the true $\theta$ by the same Kullback–Leibler argument.

The martingale CLT

For asymptotic normality we need a CLT for dependent summands. The crucial structural observation: the score function for a sequentially factored likelihood is a martingale. Let $Y_j = \partial_\theta \log f_\theta(X_j \mid X_1, \dots, X_{j-1})$. Then $E_\theta[Y_j \mid X_1, \dots, X_{j-1}] = 0$, so $\ell_n'(\theta) = \sum_{j=1}^n Y_j$ is a mean-zero martingale.

Theorem 9.40 — martingale CLT

If $M_n$ is a mean-zero martingale with conditional variances $s_j^2$ satisfying $\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n s_j^2 \xrightarrow{p} \sigma^2$ and a Lindeberg condition, then $M_n/\sqrt n \Rightarrow N(0, \sigma^2)$.

With this tool, the Section 9.3 proof transcribes to dependent data: $\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\ell_n'(\theta) \Rightarrow N(0, I(\theta))$ by the martingale CLT (where now $I(\theta) = E_\theta[-\partial_\theta^2 \log f_\theta(X_0 \mid X_{-1}, \dots)]$ is the per-observation Fisher information), and $-\frac{1}{n}\ell_n''(\theta) \xrightarrow{p} I(\theta)$ by the ergodic theorem. The final conclusion is unchanged:

$$\sqrt n(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \;\Rightarrow\; N\!\left(0, \tfrac{1}{I(\theta)}\right).$$
The lesson of Section 9.9

Asymptotic statistics is held up by two probability theorems: the LLN and the CLT. When you change the dependence structure, you swap out these two theorems for stronger versions (ergodic theorem and martingale CLT) — but everything you proved in between is unchanged. The Taylor expansion, the uniform convergence argument, the inversion of the information matrix — all of it survives.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 9

The longest chapter, in ten bullets:

  1. Many estimators (MLE, method of moments, M-estimators, Huber, Bayes posterior modes) are roots of $\sum \psi(X_i; \hat\theta) = 0$.
  2. A uniform weak law of large numbers (Theorem 9.2) is the technical tool that lets us substitute random arguments into averages — pointwise convergence is not enough.
  3. The MLE is consistent because $-I(\theta, \omega)$, the negative Kullback–Leibler information, has a unique maximum at the true $\theta$ (Wald 1949; Theorems 9.9, 9.11).
  4. The MLE is asymptotically normal with variance $1/I(\theta)$ (Theorem 9.14). The proof is a Taylor expansion of the score around $\theta$, plus the CLT for the numerator and the uniform LLN for the denominator.
  5. The MLE is therefore asymptotically efficient — it saturates the Cramér–Rao bound in the limit.
  6. Four flavours of confidence interval result: implicit using $I(\theta)$ (9.4), plug-in $I(\hat\theta_n)$ (9.5), observed information $-\ell_n''(\hat\theta_n)$ (9.7), and the likelihood-ratio profile region (9.8).
  7. When the MLE has no closed form, the EM algorithm alternates an E-step (impute sufficient statistics) and an M-step (maximise as if data were complete). Each iteration cannot decrease the observed likelihood, but convergence is only linear and only to a local maximum.
  8. In $p$ dimensions, every result above lifts verbatim with vectors and matrices. The asymptotic variance becomes $I(\theta)^{-1}$, and the multivariate delta method propagates it to functions $g(\hat\theta_n)$.
  9. M-estimators (Huber 1964) use $\psi$ functions other than the score to trade some efficiency for robustness against contamination.
  10. For dependent data, swap the LLN for the ergodic theorem and the CLT for the martingale CLT. The asymptotic distribution of the MLE is unchanged: $N(0, 1/I(\theta))$.
