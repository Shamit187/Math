Chapter 19

Bootstrap Methods

A computer simulation that pretends the data are the population — and the surprising amount of inference you can do with that one trick.

Book pages: 391–403 ~40 min reading time Prereqs: empirical CDF, plug-in estimators, basic CLT

Why does the bootstrap even work?

Here is the problem the bootstrap is trying to solve. You collect a sample $X_1, \dots, X_n$ i.i.d. from some unknown distribution $Q$. You compute an estimator $\hat\theta = \hat\theta(X)$ of some parameter $\theta = \theta(Q)$. You want to know how accurate $\hat\theta$ is — that is, you want the distribution of the error $\hat\theta - \theta$.

But the error distribution depends on $Q$, which is the very thing you don't know. If you knew $Q$, you wouldn't be doing statistics.

There are two classical ways around this. First: derive an asymptotic distribution analytically — like the central limit theorem giving $\sqrt{n}(\bar X_n - \mu) \Rightarrow N(0, \sigma^2)$. Second: stick with the few special parametric models where the finite-sample distribution is computable, like $t$ for a normal mean.

The bootstrap is a third way, due to Efron (1979): use the computer. Take whatever simulation you would have run if you knew $Q$, but run it with $Q$ replaced by the empirical distribution $\hat Q$ — the distribution that puts mass $1/n$ on each observed data point. The bootstrap bets that the answer you get is close to the one you would have gotten with the real $Q$.

The one-line slogan

The empirical distribution $\hat Q$ is to the true distribution $Q$ as the bootstrap sample $X^*$ is to the observed sample $X$.

If that analogy holds well enough, then the variability of $\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta$ across bootstrap resamples mimics the variability of $\hat\theta - \theta$ across hypothetical real samples. We can simulate the first; we can never see the second. That swap is the bootstrap.

19.1 Introduction — the empirical distribution and resampling

The empirical distribution $\hat Q$

Given i.i.d. data $X_1, \dots, X_n$ from $Q$, the empirical distribution is

$$\hat Q = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta_{X_i},$$

where $\delta_x$ is the point mass at $x$: $\delta_x(\{x\}) = 1$ and $\delta_x(B) = 0$ for any $B$ not containing $x$. So $\hat Q$ is a discrete probability measure that places mass $1/n$ on each observed point. For any set $A$,

$$\hat Q(A) = \frac{1}{n} \#\{i \leq n : X_i \in A\}.$$

This is the obvious nonparametric estimate of $Q$. By the strong law of large numbers, $\hat Q(A) \to Q(A)$ almost surely for any fixed set $A$ — even uniformly in $A$ over many classes (this is the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem when $A$ ranges over half-lines). So $\hat Q$ converges to $Q$, and reasonable functionals of $\hat Q$ should converge to the same functionals of $Q$.

Definition — bootstrap sample

A bootstrap sample $X^* = (X^*_1, \dots, X^*_n)$ is drawn conditionally i.i.d. from $\hat Q$ given the original data: $$X^* \mid X \;\sim\; \hat Q^n.$$ Since $\hat Q$ puts mass $1/n$ on each $X_i$, drawing from $\hat Q$ is exactly the same as drawing with replacement from $\{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$.

That last sentence is the implementation. A bootstrap sample is just $n$ draws with replacement from your data. Some original observations will appear multiple times in $X^*$; some won't appear at all. That's fine — that's the whole point. The variability between bootstrap samples is supposed to mimic the variability between independent real samples from $Q$.

The plug-in principle

Most of the bootstrap can be understood through a single piece of mathematics called the plug-in principle. Suppose $\theta$ is some functional of $Q$ — that is, $\theta = \theta(Q)$ for some rule $\theta(\cdot)$ that takes a distribution and returns a number. The plug-in estimator is

$$\hat\theta = \theta(\hat Q).$$

Examples make this concrete:

  • The mean: $\theta(Q) = \int x \, dQ(x)$. Plug-in: $\theta(\hat Q) = \bar X_n$.
  • The variance: $\theta(Q) = \int (x-\mu)^2 \, dQ$. Plug-in: $\theta(\hat Q) = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar X_n)^2$.
  • The median: $\theta(Q) = Q^{-1}(1/2)$. Plug-in: the sample median.
  • The bias of $\hat\theta$ at $Q$: $b(Q) = E_Q[\hat\theta - \theta]$. Plug-in: $\hat b = b(\hat Q)$.

That last bullet is where the bootstrap really lives. Whatever feature of the sampling distribution you want — bias, variance, a quantile, a coverage probability — write it as a functional of $Q$ and then plug in $\hat Q$.

Why plug-in is the soul of the bootstrap

We typically can't write a closed-form expression for $b(\hat Q)$ even after the plug-in. But $b(\hat Q)$ is, by definition, the expected error of $\hat\theta$ when the data come from $\hat Q$. And we can sample from $\hat Q$ — it's just resampling with replacement. So we estimate the unknown $b(\hat Q)$ by Monte Carlo. The bootstrap is the plug-in principle plus simulation as the calculator.

Bootstrap estimation in three lines

The general recipe, used by every example in the chapter:

  1. Pick the quantity of interest about the sampling distribution: $\phi(Q) = $ bias, variance, quantile, etc.
  2. Plug in: $\hat\phi = \phi(\hat Q)$.
  3. Approximate $\hat\phi$ by simulation. Draw $B$ bootstrap samples $X^*_1, \dots, X^*_B$; compute $\hat\theta^*_b = \hat\theta(X^*_b)$ for each. Approximate $\hat\phi$ by the corresponding sample summary of the $\hat\theta^*_b$.

The number of replications $B$ is a computational choice, not a statistical one. Larger $B$ reduces Monte Carlo noise but doesn't change what you're estimating. Typical values are $B \in [200, 10{,}000]$ depending on the task.

Example: bootstrap estimate of bias

The true bias is $b(Q) = E_Q[\hat\theta] - \theta$. Plug in $\hat Q$:

$$\hat b = b(\hat Q) = E[\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta \mid X].$$

In words: pretend $\hat Q$ is the truth, so $\hat\theta$ plays the role of $\theta$ (since $\hat\theta = \theta(\hat Q)$), and $\hat\theta^*$ plays the role of $\hat\theta$ (since it is computed from a sample drawn from $\hat Q$). Approximating by Monte Carlo with $B$ replicates:

$$\hat b \approx \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^{B} \big(\hat\theta^*_b - \hat\theta\big).$$

The bias-corrected estimator is $\hat\theta - \hat b$. This typically has smaller bias than $\hat\theta$, and Section 19.2 makes that "typically" rigorous for one example.

Example: bootstrap standard error

An even more basic application: the standard error of $\hat\theta$ is

$$\operatorname{se}(Q) = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}_Q(\hat\theta)}.$$

Plug in to get the bootstrap standard error,

$$\widehat{\operatorname{se}} = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta^* \mid X)} \approx \sqrt{\frac{1}{B-1} \sum_{b=1}^B \big(\hat\theta^*_b - \overline{\hat\theta^*}\big)^2}.$$

This is the single most-used bootstrap quantity in practice: a finite-sample standard error for any estimator, however complicated, without doing any calculus.

Worked example — bootstrap standard error of the median

Suppose $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are i.i.d. from some unknown $Q$ and you want to report a sample median $\hat m$ with a standard error. There is no easy closed-form $\operatorname{Var}(\hat m)$ unless you assume a density at the median (and even then it's an asymptotic formula involving the unknown $f(m)$). So you bootstrap.

Concrete procedure:

  1. Compute $\hat m$ from the data, the original sample median.
  2. For $b = 1, \dots, B$ (say $B = 1000$):
    • Draw $n$ indices $i_1, \dots, i_n$ uniformly with replacement from $\{1, \dots, n\}$.
    • Form the bootstrap sample $X^*_b = (X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_n})$.
    • Compute the bootstrap median $\hat m^*_b = \operatorname{median}(X^*_b)$.
  3. Report $$\widehat{\operatorname{se}}(\hat m) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\big(\hat m^*_b - \overline{\hat m^*}\big)^2}.$$

That's it. No assumption about the density. The bootstrap standard error is a usable, defensible number that you can stick next to $\hat m$ in your report.

A subtlety worth noticing: because $\hat Q$ is discrete with $n$ atoms, the bootstrap median $\hat m^*$ takes only $O(n)$ distinct values (essentially one per order statistic of the original sample). The empirical distribution of $\hat m^*$ is highly discrete. For estimators that depend smoothly on $Q$, this discreteness washes out as $n$ grows; for the median, smoothness of $Q$ near $m$ rescues the approximation. We'll see in Section 19.6 that for non-smooth functionals, the bootstrap can be in real trouble.

Pause — why is this not circular?

It looks like cheating. We are using the data to invent a population, then drawing samples from that invented population, then claiming the variability of those samples tells us something about variability under the real (unknown) population.

The point is that $\hat Q \to Q$ as $n \to \infty$, and for "nice" functionals $\phi$ — those that are continuous in $Q$ in an appropriate sense — we have $\phi(\hat Q) \to \phi(Q)$. So our plug-in estimate of, say, the standard error of $\hat\theta$ converges to the truth. The bootstrap is not magic; it's the plug-in principle applied to an unusually rich functional (the entire sampling distribution of $\hat\theta$), with simulation handling the calculation.

Example: bootstrap confidence intervals (first pass)

If $q = q(Q)$ is the upper $\alpha$-th quantile of the distribution of $|\hat\theta - \theta|$, then

$$P\!\left(\theta \in [\hat\theta - q,\, \hat\theta + q]\right) = 1 - \alpha.$$

Plug-in: $\hat q$ is the upper $\alpha$-th quantile of $|\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta|$ given $X$. Approximate $\hat q$ by simulation as the upper-$\alpha$ sample quantile of $|\hat\theta^*_b - \hat\theta|$ across $b = 1, \dots, B$. The resulting interval

$$[\hat\theta - \hat q,\, \hat\theta + \hat q]$$

is an approximate $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval. Two-sided, symmetric. Section 19.5 will refine this with percentile and BCa intervals.

Studentizing — and why it usually helps

In many problems, $(\hat\theta - \theta)/\hat\tau$ is approximately standard normal, where $\hat\tau$ is an estimate of the standard error of $\hat\theta$. If we apply the bootstrap to the studentized error

$$\frac{|\hat\theta - \theta|}{\hat\tau},$$

rather than the raw error $|\hat\theta - \theta|$, the quantile we are estimating depends much more weakly on $Q$ (it's nearly $z_{\alpha/2}$ in well-behaved cases). Statistical quantities that depend only weakly on $Q$ are easier to estimate from data than ones that depend strongly. This intuition — "studentize first, then bootstrap" — is borne out theoretically in Section 19.4: studentizing improves the order of accuracy of the bootstrap confidence interval.

19.2 Bias reduction — a calculation you can carry out by hand

We pause the abstract discussion and do one example explicitly. Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. from $Q$ with mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$, and third central moment $\gamma = E(X_i - \mu)^3$. Consider estimating $\theta = \mu^3$ by the plug-in estimator

$$\hat\theta = \bar X_n^3.$$

This estimator is biased — averaging a non-linear function of $\bar X_n$ doesn't give $\mu^3$. To find the bias, expand:

$$\bar X^3 = (\mu + (\bar X - \mu))^3 = \mu^3 + 3\mu^2(\bar X - \mu) + 3\mu(\bar X - \mu)^2 + (\bar X - \mu)^3.$$

Using $E(\bar X - \mu) = 0$, $E(\bar X - \mu)^2 = \sigma^2/n$, and $E(\bar X - \mu)^3 = \gamma/n^2$ (third cumulant adds across i.i.d. terms),

$$E \bar X^3 = \mu^3 + \frac{3\mu\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{\gamma}{n^2},$$

and so the bias is

$$b(Q) = E\hat\theta - \theta = \frac{3\mu\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{\gamma}{n^2}.$$

The bootstrap bias estimate substitutes $\hat Q$ everywhere:

$$\hat b = b(\hat Q) = \frac{3\bar X \hat\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{\hat\gamma}{n^2},$$

with $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2$ and $\hat\gamma = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar X)^3$. The bias-reduced estimator is then

$$\hat\theta - \hat b = \bar X^3 - \frac{3\bar X \hat\sigma^2}{n} - \frac{\hat\gamma}{n^2}.$$

The chapter then performs a somewhat involved bookkeeping calculation to find the mean of $\hat\theta - \hat b$. The key intermediate results, in case you want to verify them:

$$E[\bar X \hat\sigma^2] = \mu\sigma^2 + \frac{1}{n}(\gamma - \mu\sigma^2) - \frac{\gamma}{n^2},$$ $$E[\hat\gamma] = \gamma\left(1 - \frac{3}{n} + \frac{2}{n^2}\right).$$

Plugging in and collecting terms,

$$E[\hat\theta - \hat b] = \mu^3 + \frac{3}{n^2}(\mu\sigma^2 - \gamma) + \frac{6\gamma}{n^3} - \frac{2\gamma}{n^4}.$$

Subtract $\theta = \mu^3$ to get the new bias: it is of order $1/n^2$, compared with order $1/n$ for the original $\hat\theta$.

What just happened

We did the same calculation twice: once with the truth $Q$, once with $\hat Q$. The bootstrap "knew" everything about the leading $1/n$ bias term because that term depends only on $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, which $\hat Q$ estimates consistently. The error that remains is at the next order — $1/n^2$. This is the typical story for the bootstrap: it picks off leading-order corrections that ordinary asymptotics leaves uncomputed.

An alternative to bootstrap bias correction is the jackknife: compute $\hat\theta_{-i}$ by leaving out observation $i$, then use the pseudo-values $\tilde\theta_i = n\hat\theta - (n-1)\hat\theta_{-i}$ to estimate bias and variance. The jackknife is closely related to the bootstrap and is the subject of Problem 19.2.

19.3 Parametric bootstrap confidence intervals

Up to here we have used the empirical distribution $\hat Q$, treating $Q$ as completely unknown. This is the nonparametric bootstrap. There is a parallel construction for parametric models.

Definition — parametric bootstrap

Suppose $Q \in \{Q_\lambda : \lambda \in \Lambda\}$ is known to lie in a parametric family, with $\hat\lambda$ the maximum likelihood estimator from the data. Use $\hat Q = Q_{\hat\lambda}$ in place of the empirical distribution. A parametric bootstrap sample is drawn i.i.d. from $Q_{\hat\lambda}$: $$X^* \mid X \;\sim\; Q_{\hat\lambda}^{\,n}.$$

Everything else is the same. The bootstrap estimator of a quantile $q = q(\lambda)$ is $\hat q$, the upper $\alpha$-th quantile of $|\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta|$ given $X$ — either computed in closed form when the model permits, or by simulation when it doesn't.

Worked case: the normal mean

Take $X_1, \dots, X_n$ i.i.d. $N(\theta, \sigma^2)$, with both $\theta$ and $\sigma^2$ unknown. The MLE is $\hat\theta = \bar X$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2$. The parametric bootstrap draws $X^*_1, \dots, X^*_n$ i.i.d. from $N(\bar X, \hat\sigma^2)$. Then

$$\hat\theta^* \mid X \,\sim\, N\!\left(\bar X,\, \hat\sigma^2/n\right),$$

so the upper $\alpha/2$ quantile of $|\hat\theta^* - \bar X|$ is simply $z_{\alpha/2}\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$. The bootstrap confidence interval is

$$\left(\bar X - z_{\alpha/2}\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt n,\; \bar X + z_{\alpha/2}\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt n\right).$$

Notice this is not the textbook $t$-interval: it uses $z$ rather than $t$, and uses the MLE $\hat\sigma^2$ rather than $S^2 = n\hat\sigma^2/(n-1)$. The true coverage is $1 - \alpha - z_{\alpha/2}\phi(z_{\alpha/2})/n + o(1/n)$ — close to nominal but not exact.

Now try the studentized version: bootstrap the distribution of $|\hat\theta - \theta|/\hat\tau$ where $\hat\tau = \hat\sigma/\sqrt n$. Some calculation (see the chapter) shows

$$\tilde q(\lambda) = \frac{\sqrt n\, t_{\alpha/2, n-1}}{\sqrt{n-1}},$$

which is independent of $\lambda$ — so the bootstrap estimates it perfectly. The resulting studentized bootstrap interval is

$$\left(\bar X - t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\,S/\sqrt n,\; \bar X + t_{\alpha/2, n-1}\,S/\sqrt n\right),$$

the exact $t$-interval with coverage exactly $1 - \alpha$. Studentizing turned a slightly-off bootstrap interval into the exact textbook one.

Pause — what made studentizing work?

The quantity $|\hat\theta - \theta|/\hat\tau$ is a pivot in this normal model: its distribution doesn't depend on $\lambda$ at all. The bootstrap quantile of a pivot is the true quantile, exactly. In general models, perfect pivots don't exist, but quantities that are approximately pivotal (those whose distribution depends only weakly on the unknown $Q$) are exactly the ones the bootstrap handles best. This is the deeper reason "studentize first" works.

19.4 Nonparametric accuracy for averages — how fast does the bootstrap learn?

Take $\hat\theta = \bar X_n$ estimating $\theta = \mu = EX_i$. We could approximate the error distribution by the CLT:

$$Z_n = \frac{\sqrt n(\hat\theta - \theta)}{\sigma} \Rightarrow N(0,1).$$

How accurate is this approximation? The Edgeworth expansion answers: for nonlattice $Q$ with $E|X_i|^3 < \infty$,

$$P(Z_n \leq x) = \Phi(x) - \frac{\gamma(x^2 - 1)}{6\sigma^3 \sqrt n}\,\phi(x) + o(1/\sqrt n),$$

uniformly in $x$. So the CLT misses by $O(1/\sqrt n)$, and the correction term involves the third moment $\gamma$ — which is exactly the kind of higher moment the bootstrap can pick up.

Singh (1981) showed:

Theorem 19.4 (Singh)

Let $Z_n^* = \sqrt n(\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta)/\hat\sigma_n$. Under the same conditions, $$P(Z_n^* \leq x \mid X) = \Phi(x) - \frac{\gamma(x^2 - 1)}{6\sigma^3 \sqrt n}\,\phi(x) + o_p(1/\sqrt n).$$

The first two terms in the bootstrap expansion match the truth exactly — including the $1/\sqrt n$ correction. The remainder is $o_p(1/\sqrt n)$. So the bootstrap matches the truth to higher order than the CLT does.

Why the bootstrap beats normal approximation

The bootstrap automatically uses the empirical third moment $\hat\gamma_n$. The CLT throws away that information by replacing everything with a normal. So whenever the underlying distribution is even slightly skewed, the bootstrap quietly tracks the skewness and the CLT doesn't.

Hall (1992) extended this to studentized statistics and confidence intervals. The headline:

  • Symmetric two-sided bootstrap CI without studentizing: coverage error $O(1/n)$.
  • Symmetric two-sided bootstrap CI with studentizing: coverage error $O(1/n^2)$.
  • Asymptotic normal-theory CI: coverage error $O(1/\sqrt n)$.

Two orders of magnitude better, just from running a resampling loop. This is one of the strongest justifications for using the bootstrap as a default tool when the analyst can afford the compute.

The smoothness condition under which this all works is delicate. Bickel and Freedman (1981) studied von Mises functionals — those $\theta(\cdot)$ that are Gateaux differentiable with the derivative representable as an integral — and proved the bootstrap is consistent for them. They also gave counterexamples; see Section 19.6.

19.5 Bootstrap confidence intervals — percentile and BCa

Section 19.1 gave one form of bootstrap confidence interval, based on quantiles of $|\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta|$. There are several other constructions worth knowing.

The basic (or "pivotal") interval

Bootstrap the distribution of $\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta$. Let $\hat q_L$ and $\hat q_U$ be the lower-$\alpha/2$ and upper-$\alpha/2$ quantiles of this bootstrap distribution. The basic bootstrap interval is

$$\big(\hat\theta - \hat q_U,\; \hat\theta - \hat q_L\big).$$

The reasoning is straightforward: pretend $\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta$ has the same distribution as $\hat\theta - \theta$, then solve for $\theta$. Note the quantiles flip: the upper bootstrap quantile gives the lower endpoint and vice versa.

The percentile interval

Order the bootstrap replicates $\hat\theta^*_{(1)} \leq \cdots \leq \hat\theta^*_{(B)}$. The percentile interval simply reads off the $\alpha/2$ and $1-\alpha/2$ sample quantiles:

$$\big(\hat\theta^*_{(\lfloor B\alpha/2 \rfloor)},\; \hat\theta^*_{(\lceil B(1-\alpha/2)\rceil)}\big).$$

This is the easiest interval to explain ("middle 95% of bootstrap replicates") and what most software defaults to. It is range-respecting: if $\theta$ must be positive, the percentile interval is positive too, automatically.

Common pitfall — percentile vs. basic

Percentile and basic intervals are not the same and can disagree noticeably when the bootstrap distribution is skewed. The percentile interval is symmetric in $\hat\theta^*$-space; the basic interval is symmetric in error-space. Many software packages use one or the other without saying which.

As a rule of thumb: if the bootstrap distribution is roughly symmetric around $\hat\theta$, they agree. If it's skewed, neither is great, and you should reach for BCa or studentize.

The BCa interval — bias-corrected and accelerated

Efron's BCa interval repairs the percentile interval in the presence of bias and skewness. It uses two scalar adjustments:

  • A bias correction $\hat z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\#\{b : \hat\theta^*_b \leq \hat\theta\}/B\right)$ — measures how far the bootstrap median sits from $\hat\theta$.
  • An acceleration $\hat a$ — measures how quickly the standard error of $\hat\theta$ changes with $\theta$, typically computed from jackknife pseudo-values: $$\hat a = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (\bar{\hat\theta}_{-\cdot} - \hat\theta_{-i})^3}{6\big[\sum_{i=1}^n (\bar{\hat\theta}_{-\cdot} - \hat\theta_{-i})^2\big]^{3/2}}.$$

Given $\hat z_0$ and $\hat a$, define

$$\alpha_1 = \Phi\!\left(\hat z_0 + \frac{\hat z_0 + z_{\alpha/2}}{1 - \hat a(\hat z_0 + z_{\alpha/2})}\right),\qquad \alpha_2 = \Phi\!\left(\hat z_0 + \frac{\hat z_0 + z_{1-\alpha/2}}{1 - \hat a(\hat z_0 + z_{1-\alpha/2})}\right).$$

The BCa interval is $(\hat\theta^*_{(\alpha_1)}, \hat\theta^*_{(\alpha_2)})$ — the same percentile-style read-off but at adjusted percentile levels. The point is: when $\hat z_0 = \hat a = 0$, BCa reduces to the percentile interval. Otherwise the adjustments correct for bias and skewness, and BCa achieves second-order accuracy: coverage error $O(1/n)$, an improvement over the percentile and basic intervals which are first-order ($O(1/\sqrt n)$).

When does BCa matter?

Small samples, skewed estimators, parameters near a boundary — these are the situations where the bootstrap distribution is far from symmetric and where BCa pays off most. For large $n$ with smooth estimators, every reasonable bootstrap interval gives more or less the same answer; BCa earns its keep in the awkward cases.

The studentized (or "bootstrap-$t$") interval

Compute $T^*_b = (\hat\theta^*_b - \hat\theta)/\hat\tau^*_b$ for each bootstrap replicate, where $\hat\tau^*_b$ is an estimate of the standard error of $\hat\theta$ computed from the $b$-th bootstrap sample. Let $\hat t_{\alpha/2}$ and $\hat t_{1-\alpha/2}$ be the corresponding sample quantiles. The studentized interval is

$$\big(\hat\theta - \hat t_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat\tau,\; \hat\theta - \hat t_{\alpha/2}\,\hat\tau\big).$$

This requires a standard error estimate at each bootstrap iteration — sometimes that's another inner bootstrap (a "double bootstrap"), which gets computationally heavy. When you have a fast $\hat\tau$ available (a delta-method formula, for example), the studentized interval typically gives the best coverage of any single-resampling-pass bootstrap method.

19.6 Where the bootstrap fails

The bootstrap is not unconditionally consistent. Three classes of failure are worth knowing.

Non-smooth functionals: maximum of a uniform

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. $\text{Uniform}(0, \theta)$, and let $\hat\theta = \max_i X_i = X_{(n)}$ be the MLE. The exact distribution of the error is

$$P(n(\theta - \hat\theta)/\theta \leq x) \to 1 - e^{-x} \quad \text{for } x \geq 0.$$

Now consider the nonparametric bootstrap. Conditional on $X$, $\hat\theta^* = \max_i X^*_i$, and the bootstrap sample is drawn with replacement from $\{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$. The bootstrap maximum can never exceed $X_{(n)} = \hat\theta$, so $\hat\theta^* \leq \hat\theta$ always. Moreover,

$$P(\hat\theta^* = \hat\theta \mid X) = 1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \to 1 - 1/e \approx 0.632.$$

So with probability about 63% the bootstrap reports zero error. The bootstrap distribution of $\hat\theta - \hat\theta^*$ does not converge to the true error distribution — it puts a permanent atom at $0$. Bootstrap confidence intervals for $\theta$ undercover badly.

Common pitfall — boundary parameters

The bootstrap fails for the maximum of a uniform because the functional $\theta(Q) = \sup\{x : Q(x) < 1\}$ is not smooth in $Q$ — small perturbations of $Q$ near the upper endpoint cause discontinuous changes in $\theta$. Any estimator that depends critically on the tail of the distribution (extremes, ranges, modes of multi-modal distributions) is suspect. The right fix is either (a) parametric bootstrap if you trust the model, (b) subsampling, or (c) the $m$-out-of-$n$ bootstrap, which draws bootstrap samples of size $m < n$.

Dependent data

Everything in this chapter has assumed $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are i.i.d. If your data are a time series, a spatial process, or otherwise dependent, naive resampling with replacement destroys the dependence structure. The bootstrap empirical distribution treats the marginals correctly but acts as if observations were independent.

The fix is some form of block bootstrap: resample blocks of consecutive observations rather than individual observations, so the within-block dependence is preserved. The block length must be chosen — too short and dependence is lost, too long and you don't have enough blocks.

Small samples

The bootstrap's appeal is that it doesn't require asymptotic approximations, but it absolutely does require $\hat Q$ to be a reasonable stand-in for $Q$. With $n = 5$, $\hat Q$ supports only $5$ points and can be a very poor representation of a continuous $Q$. In these cases the bootstrap may be no better — and sometimes worse — than a parametric approach with a defensible model.

Common pitfall — "the bootstrap doesn't need assumptions"

This is a slogan, not a theorem. The bootstrap needs i.i.d. data, $\hat\theta$ that is a smooth functional of $Q$, and enough sample size for $\hat Q \approx Q$. When any of these break, the bootstrap can be silently wrong — and the user gets back beautifully formatted output that looks just as authoritative as if it were correct.

Heavy tails and infinite variance

If $\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \infty$, then $\sqrt n(\bar X - \mu)$ does not converge to a normal. The CLT-based asymptotics break, and so does the bootstrap. Athreya (1987) showed that the bootstrap distribution of $\bar X$ in the heavy-tailed case does not stabilize at all — it has a random limit that depends on the particular data realization. If you have heavy tails, you need either a different estimator (the median, say) or a different resampling scheme (e.g., $m$-out-of-$n$).

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 19

The whole chapter, in seven bullets:

  1. The empirical distribution $\hat Q = \frac{1}{n}\sum \delta_{X_i}$ is the natural nonparametric estimator of $Q$.
  2. A bootstrap sample $X^*$ is drawn i.i.d. from $\hat Q$ — equivalently, with replacement from the data.
  3. The plug-in principle: to estimate any feature $\phi(Q)$ of the sampling distribution, use $\phi(\hat Q)$. Monte Carlo with $B$ bootstrap resamples handles the calculation when $\phi(\hat Q)$ has no closed form.
  4. The parametric bootstrap uses $\hat Q = Q_{\hat\lambda}$ for a parametric family. The recipe is otherwise identical.
  5. Confidence intervals: percentile, basic, BCa, studentized. Studentizing improves the order of accuracy from $O(1/\sqrt n)$ to $O(1/n)$ or better.
  6. For smooth functionals (von Mises functionals), the bootstrap is consistent and typically matches the truth to higher order than the CLT.
  7. The bootstrap fails for non-smooth functionals (max of a uniform), for dependent data without blocking, for heavy-tailed data, and is unreliable at very small $n$.
