Chapter 20

Sequential Methods

When the sample size is itself a random variable — and what that does to the inference machinery you spent nineteen chapters building.

Book pages: 405–430 ~50 min reading time Prereqs: Chs. 1, 5, 7, 8, 12

Why be sequential?

Every inference procedure you have met so far assumes a fixed sample size $n$, chosen before looking at the data. You write down a confidence interval, a test, a posterior — and the sample size is a constant. Now we relax that. In a sequential experiment, the data arrive one at a time and the experimenter is allowed to decide, after each observation, whether to keep collecting or to stop. The sample size $N$ becomes a random variable.

There are three reasons to care:

  • Efficiency. If you are testing whether a drug works and after twenty patients the answer is already obvious, why pay to enrol another two hundred? Sequential tests can detect the truth with far fewer observations on average.
  • Fixed-precision goals. Some objectives are not achievable with any predetermined $n$ — for example, "give me a confidence interval of width $\leq 0.01$ no matter what the variance turns out to be". This requires looking at the data to decide when enough is enough.
  • Adaptive design. In clinical trials, dose-finding studies, and "multi-armed bandit" problems, the choice of what to do next depends on what you have already seen. Stopping is just the simplest instance.
The whole chapter in one sentence

Once $N$ is random, almost every classical sampling distribution becomes wrong, the likelihood somehow becomes right, and a new optimization problem appears: choose the stopping rule itself.

The chapter opens with a small horror story. It is worth lingering over.

Example 20.1 — Sampling to a foregone conclusion

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. $N(\mu, 1)$, and let $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$. The classical level-$\alpha$ two-sided test of $H_0 : \mu = 0$ rejects when $|S_n| > z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{n}$. Now imagine a researcher who runs this test repeatedly, after each new observation, and stops the moment it rejects: $$N = \inf\{ n \geq 1 : |S_n| > z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{n} \}.$$

A short argument using the law of large numbers and independence of disjoint blocks $S_{2^{j+1}} - S_{2^j}$ shows that $P(N = \infty) = 0$ even when $\mu = 0$. So the researcher is guaranteed, with probability one, to eventually reject $H_0$ — regardless of whether $H_0$ is true. The nominal level $\alpha$ has been silently inflated to $100\%$.

Common pitfall

This is not a quirk of the normal model. Any fixed-$n$ test, monitored continuously and stopped at the first rejection, will eventually reject under the null. The technical name is the law of the iterated logarithm talking, but the moral is simpler: peeking and stopping early invalidates classical inference. You may not use a fixed-$n$ critical value with a data-dependent sample size.

Pause

Read Example 20.1 again. The researcher did nothing dishonest — they ran the same level-$\alpha$ test the textbook prescribes, just more often. The mistake is structural: the sampling distribution of $S_N/\sqrt{N}$ is not $N(0,1)$ when $N$ is the random stopping time defined above. There exists no $n$ for which the boundary is sharp; it is always re-crossed eventually.

Now ask yourself: if a colleague tells you "we collected data until the effect was significant," what should you do with their $p$-value? The honest answer is: politely refuse it, and ask for the protocol.

Surprise: the likelihood is unaffected

Here is the philosophical fault line. The sampling distribution of $S_N/\sqrt N$ depends violently on the stopping rule. But the likelihood function after stopping does not. Whether you collected $X_1, \dots, X_{10}$ because you planned to all along, or because some adaptive rule led you to stop at $n=10$, $$L(\mu) = \prod_{i=1}^{10} \phi(X_i - \mu)$$ is the same function of $\mu$. We prove this carefully in Section 20.2 (Wald's fundamental identity), but the consequence is huge:

  • Frequentist inference — based on the sampling distribution of a statistic — requires you to declare your stopping rule.
  • Bayesian inference — based on the likelihood times the prior — does not care about the stopping rule at all.

This is the cleanest example in the book of the two inferential philosophies pulling in opposite directions on the same data. Many design problems are therefore mathematically more tractable in the Bayesian framework, and Section 20.3 exploits this.

20.1 Fixed-width confidence intervals

Suppose $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. from a one-parameter exponential family with sufficient statistic $T(X)$. By the delta method, the MLE $\hat\lambda_n$ of some smooth parameter $\lambda = g(\theta)$ satisfies $$\sqrt n (\hat\lambda_n - \lambda) \Rightarrow N(0, \nu^2(\theta)),$$ and the standard $1-\alpha$ asymptotic confidence interval is $$\hat\lambda_n \pm z_{\alpha/2} \, \nu(\hat\theta_n) / \sqrt n. \tag{20.1}$$ Its half-width is $z_{\alpha/2} \nu(\hat\theta_n)/\sqrt n$. The trouble: $\nu(\theta)$ is unknown, so for any fixed $n$ the width is unpredictable. If we want a guaranteed width $\leq w$, we cannot get there with a fixed-$n$ design.

The sequential fix

Keep sampling until the half-width drops below $w/2$. Formally, $$N = N_w = \inf\!\left\{ n : w^2 n \geq 4 z_{\alpha/2}^2 \, \nu^2(\hat\theta_n) \right\}. \tag{20.2}$$ By construction the width of $\hat\lambda_N \pm z_{\alpha/2} \nu(\hat\theta_N)/\sqrt N$ is at most $w$. The question is: does it still have coverage $1 - \alpha$?

With a fixed $n$ the answer follows from the CLT. With $N$ a random stopping time we need a new tool: a CLT in which the number of summands is itself random.

Definition 20.2 — uniformly continuous in probability (u.c.i.p.)

Random variables $W_n$, $n \geq 1$, are uniformly continuous in probability if for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $$P\!\left\{ \max_{0 \leq k \leq n\delta} |W_{n+k} - W_n| \geq \epsilon \right\} < \epsilon, \quad \text{for all } n \geq 1.$$

Translation

"u.c.i.p." just means: a tiny relative perturbation of the index $n$ cannot move $W_n$ very much, uniformly in $n$. This is the property a sequence needs in order to be insensitive to whether you stop at the deterministic index $n_w$ or the random index $N_w$, provided the two are close.

Theorem 20.3 — Anscombe's CLT

If $N_w$ are positive-integer-valued random variables with $w^2 N_w \xrightarrow{p} c \in (0, \infty)$ as $w \downarrow 0$, if $n_w = \lfloor c/w^2 \rfloor$, and if $W_n$ is u.c.i.p., then $$W_{N_w} - W_{n_w} \xrightarrow{p} 0.$$ In particular, if $W_n \Rightarrow W$, then also $W_{N_w} \Rightarrow W$.

The proof in the book uses Kolmogorov's maximal inequality (Lemma 20.4): $$P\!\left( \max_{1 \leq k \leq n} |S_k| \geq c \right) \leq \frac{n \sigma_Y^2}{c^2}.$$ This is the workhorse for showing that normalized partial sums $W_n = \sqrt{n} \bar Y_n$ are u.c.i.p.

Why this is the right tool

Anscombe is saying: if your statistic doesn't wiggle too much locally, you can replace its random index with a nearby deterministic index without changing the limit. The CLT you already know transfers wholesale to the sequential setting, as long as $N_w \to \infty$ in the prescribed way.

Applying Anscombe to $W_n = \sqrt n (\hat\lambda_n - \lambda)/\nu(\hat\theta_n)$ — together with the strong law to guarantee $\hat\theta_N \to \theta$ a.s. — gives $$\frac{\sqrt N (\hat\lambda_N - \lambda)}{\nu(\hat\theta_N)} \Rightarrow N(0,1)$$ as $w \downarrow 0$, and therefore the sequential interval (20.3) has asymptotic coverage $1 - \alpha$. Width guaranteed at most $w$ by construction; coverage guaranteed in the limit by Anscombe.

Pause

Notice that the procedure is asymptotic. For any finite $w$ the coverage is only approximate, and small-sample under-coverage is real. The standard improvement is a "two-stage" design (Stein 1945, Problem 20.1): a fixed first stage of size $N_1$ to estimate the variance, then a second stage with $N_2$ chosen as a deterministic function of the first-stage sample variance, after which the $t$-statistic has an exact $t_{N_1 - 1}$ distribution.

20.2 Stopping times and likelihoods

In Chapter 5 we admitted that a sequential experiment was awkward to write down as a random vector — its dimension varies. The right tool is the language of filtrations: increasing $\sigma$-fields representing "information observed so far".

A $\sigma$-field is a list of yes/no questions you can answer

The toy example from the book is the cleanest illustration. Toss a coin twice; the outcome space is $E = \{TT, TH, HT, HH\}$. Let $X$ be the number of heads. Knowing $X$ tells you whether the event $\{X = 1\} = \{HT, TH\}$ occurred, but not whether the first toss was heads. The collection of all events you can resolve by observing $X$ is $$\sigma(X) = \big\{\emptyset, \{TT\}, \{HT, TH\}, \{HH\}, \dots, E\big\},$$ a $\sigma$-field of eight sets. "Observing $X$" and "knowing which events in $\sigma(X)$ occurred" carry the same information.

Filtration and natural filtration

A filtration is an increasing sequence of $\sigma$-fields $\mathcal{F}_0 \subset \mathcal{F}_1 \subset \mathcal{F}_2 \subset \cdots$. The natural filtration for a sequence $X_1, X_2, \dots$ is $$\mathcal{F}_n = \sigma(X_1, \dots, X_n), \quad \text{with } \mathcal{F}_0 = \{\emptyset, E\}.$$ $\mathcal{F}_n$ is "everything you can know after watching the first $n$ observations".

The crucial restriction on the sample size

A sequential design cannot be clairvoyant. You cannot say "I will collect two observations if and only if $X_5 > 10$." The decision to stop after $n$ observations must depend only on the first $n$ observations — i.e., on $\mathcal{F}_n$.

Definition 20.5 — stopping time

A random variable $N$ taking values in $\{0, 1, 2, \dots, \infty\}$ is a stopping time with respect to a filtration $\mathcal{F}_n$ if $$\{N = n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n \quad \text{for all } n \geq 0.$$

Re-reading in plain English

The event "stop now" must be decidable from the data already collected. No peeking into the future. This single constraint is what tames sequential experiments — it allows us to define a $\sigma$-field $\mathcal{F}_N$ of "information available at time of stopping" and to do real probability theory on it.

The $\sigma$-field at the stopping time is $$\mathcal{F}_N = \big\{ B \in \mathcal{F} : B \cap \{N = n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n \text{ for every } n \big\}.$$ Inside it live exactly the random variables that depend only on $(X_1, \dots, X_N)$. For instance $\bar X_N$ is $\mathcal{F}_N$-measurable, but $X_{N+1}$ is not.

Wald's fundamental identity

This is the technical heart of why likelihoods are unaffected by stopping. Suppose two probability measures $P_0$ and $P_1$ give the data $X_1, \dots, X_n$ joint densities $f_{0n}$ and $f_{1n}$. Let $$L_n = L_n(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \frac{f_{1n}(X_1, \dots, X_n)}{f_{0n}(X_1, \dots, X_n)}$$ be the sequential likelihood ratio.

Theorem 20.6 — Wald's fundamental identity

If $f_{1n} = 0$ whenever $f_{0n} = 0$, and if $P_0(N < \infty) = P_1(N < \infty) = 1$, then for every $B \in \mathcal{F}_N$, $$P_1(B) = E_0\!\left[ 1_B \, L_N \right].$$

What this is saying

Restricted to $\mathcal{F}_N$, the measure $P_1$ has density $L_N$ with respect to $P_0$. The likelihood ratio "at the stopping time" is the same product you would write down for a fixed sample size — it does not pick up an extra factor for the stopping decision. This is because $\{N = n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n$, so on this event $L_N$ collapses to $L_n$, and the proof is a partition argument: $$P_1(B) = \sum_n P_1(B \cap \{N = n\}) = \sum_n E_0\!\left[ 1_{B \cap \{N=n\}} L_n \right] = E_0[1_B L_N].$$

Specializing to i.i.d. observations with marginal density $f_\theta$, the likelihood after stopping at $N$ is just $\prod_{i=1}^N f_\theta(X_i)$. Bayesian inference based on this likelihood is identical to what you would do with a deterministic $n$. The whole stopping rule disappears from the posterior.

Common pitfall

"The likelihood is the same" does not mean "all inference is the same". The likelihood is a function of $\theta$ given the data. The sampling distribution of, say, $\hat\theta_N$ — which is what a frequentist uses to build a CI — depends on the joint law of $(N, X_1, \dots, X_N)$ and is generally not what it would be at a fixed $n$. The two camps disagree on what to do with the same likelihood.

20.3 Optimal stopping

We now treat the stopping rule as a decision to be optimized. The setup: data $X_1, X_2, \dots$ i.i.d. from $Q_\theta$ given $\Theta = \theta$; cost $c > 0$ per observation; a loss $L(\Theta)$ for accepting the wrong hypothesis. We want to choose both an inferential action $A$ (which hypothesis to accept) and a stopping time $N$ to minimize $$E[c N + \rho_N], \tag{20.5}$$ where $\rho_N$ is the posterior inferential risk after observing $\mathcal{F}_N$.

The optimal action

For testing $H_0 : \Theta \in \Omega_0$ versus $H_1 : \Theta \in \Omega_1$, Theorem 20.7 says: given $\mathcal{F}_N$, accept the hypothesis with smaller posterior expected loss. The resulting posterior inferential risk is $$\rho_N = \min\!\left\{ E[L(\Theta) 1_{\Theta \in \Omega_1} \mid \mathcal{F}_N], \ E[L(\Theta) 1_{\Theta \in \Omega_0} \mid \mathcal{F}_N] \right\}.$$ This is just the Bayes rule with a sequential filtration in place of a fixed one — same idea as Chapter 7, new index.

A worked example: Bernoulli with backwards induction

The textbook example fixes the framework. Let $X_i$ be Bernoulli($\Theta$), test $H_0 : \Theta \leq 1/2$ vs. $H_1 : \Theta > 1/2$, with prior $\Theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ and loss $L(\Theta) = K|\Theta - 1/2|$. The conjugate posterior after observing $\mathcal{F}_n$ is $\text{Beta}(\alpha_n, \beta_n)$ with $\alpha_n = \alpha + S_n$ and $\beta_n = \beta + n - S_n$, where $S_n$ counts successes.

A short calculation gives $$\rho_n = \tfrac{1}{2} K \, E[|\Theta - 1/2| \mid \mathcal{F}_n] + \tfrac{1}{4} K - \tfrac{1}{2} K \frac{\max\{\alpha_n, \beta_n\}}{\alpha_n + \beta_n}.$$ The first two terms have constant expectation. So minimizing (20.5) is equivalent to maximizing $$\tfrac{1}{2} K \, E\!\left[ \frac{\max\{\alpha_N, \beta_N\}}{\alpha_N + \beta_N} \right] - c E N. \tag{20.6}$$ A nice interpretation: think of $c$ as the cost per observation and $K/2$ as the prize for correctly guessing $X_{N+1}$. Equation (20.6) is the expected net winnings.

The Markov structure that makes it tractable

Given the posterior parameters $(\alpha_n, \beta_n)$, future observations are conditionally independent of the past — they evolve like a Bayesian player with prior $\text{Beta}(\alpha_n, \beta_n)$. The sequence $(\alpha_n, \beta_n)$ is therefore a Markov chain. So the value function depends on the data only through the current $(\alpha, \beta)$.

The Bellman equation for the value function

Let $V(\alpha, \beta)$ be the optimal expected net winnings starting from posterior $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$. Bellman's principle of optimality gives $$V(\alpha, \beta) = \max\!\left\{ \tfrac{1}{2} K \frac{\max\{\alpha, \beta\}}{\alpha + \beta}, \ \frac{\alpha V(\alpha + 1, \beta) + \beta V(\alpha, \beta + 1)}{\alpha + \beta} - c \right\}. \tag{20.7}$$

Backwards induction

For $n$ large, sampling cost overwhelms the value of one more observation and we may set $V(\alpha_n, \beta_n) = \tfrac{1}{2} K \max\{\alpha_n, \beta_n\}/(\alpha + \beta + n)$. Starting from this boundary, we recompute $V$ at $n-1$, then $n-2$, and so on back to $V(\alpha, \beta)$ via (20.7). This backwards induction is the standard dynamic-programming algorithm.

With $\alpha = \beta = 1$ (uniform prior), $K = 200$, $c = 1$, Keener reports an explicit continuation region: the rule continues sampling as long as $(\alpha_n, \beta_n)$ lies in a finite set of 19 lattice points around the diagonal. The expected sample size is $E N = 3.13$ and the expected inferential risk is $3.55$ — compared with $5.0$ for the best fixed-$n = 3$ design. The sequential design is 29% more efficient on inferential risk for only $0.13$ extra observations on average.

Pause

The continuation region is symmetric in $(\alpha_n, \beta_n)$ — which makes sense, because the prior and the loss are symmetric about $\Theta = 1/2$. You stop as soon as either $\alpha_n$ or $\beta_n$ pulls noticeably ahead; otherwise the posterior is still indecisive and an extra coin flip is cheap relative to the inferential cost of being wrong.

20.4 The Sequential Probability Ratio Test

The crown jewel of sequential analysis is Wald's sequential probability ratio test (SPRT), published in 1947. It tests two simple hypotheses — $H_0 : \theta = 0$ vs. $H_1 : \theta = 1$ — with i.i.d. observations from $Q_\theta$ with density $q_\theta$. The likelihood ratio after $n$ observations is $$L_n = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{q_1(X_i)}{q_0(X_i)}, \qquad L_0 = 1.$$

The SPRT

Fix two thresholds $A < 1 < B$. At each stage:

  • If $L_n \in (A, B)$: take another observation.
  • If $L_n \geq B$: stop and reject $H_0$.
  • If $L_n \leq A$: stop and accept $H_0$.

The sample size is the stopping time $$N = \inf\{ n : L_n \notin (A, B) \}.$$

Why this is the right shape

Recall Neyman–Pearson: the most powerful fixed-$n$ test rejects when the likelihood ratio is large. The SPRT applies the same logic sequentially: keep accumulating evidence in the form of $\log L_n$, which is a random walk with negative mean under $H_0$ and positive mean under $H_1$. Stop as soon as the walk crosses a threshold on either side. The expected time to cross is short when the evidence is decisive and long when the data are near the boundary $\theta \approx 1/2$ — which is exactly when you should keep collecting.

Worked example — Bernoulli SPRT

SPRT for two Bernoulli proportions

Suppose $X_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and we test $H_0 : p = p_0$ vs. $H_1 : p = p_1$ with $p_1 > p_0$. Then $$\frac{q_1(X_i)}{q_0(X_i)} = \left(\frac{p_1}{p_0}\right)^{X_i} \left(\frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_0}\right)^{1 - X_i},$$ so taking logs, $$\log L_n = S_n \log\!\frac{p_1}{p_0} + (n - S_n) \log\!\frac{1 - p_1}{1 - p_0},$$ where $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$. The SPRT thus monitors the random walk $\log L_n$ and stops when it leaves $(\log A, \log B)$. To choose $A$ and $B$ for target error probabilities $\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_1$, Wald's approximate identities give $$A \approx \frac{\alpha_1}{1 - \alpha_0}, \qquad B \approx \frac{1 - \alpha_1}{\alpha_0},$$ known as the Wald boundaries. These are slightly conservative (the inequalities are exact upper/lower bounds because of "overshoot" past the boundary), but in practice they are excellent.

Concrete numbers: $p_0 = 0.1$, $p_1 = 0.3$, $\alpha_0 = \alpha_1 = 0.05$. Then $A = 0.05/0.95 \approx 0.053$ and $B = 0.95/0.05 = 19$, so $\log A \approx -2.94$, $\log B \approx 2.94$. Each $X_i = 1$ contributes $\log(0.3/0.1) = \log 3 \approx 1.10$ to $\log L_n$; each $X_i = 0$ contributes $\log(0.7/0.9) \approx -0.25$. A test that would need $n \approx 100$ to achieve these errors at fixed $n$ typically resolves in 25–40 observations with the SPRT. That is the efficiency gain Wald promised.

The optimality theorem

Why is the SPRT special? Because it minimizes both $E_0 N$ and $E_1 N$ simultaneously among all sequential tests with the same or better error probabilities. This is the Wald–Wolfowitz theorem.

Theorem 20.9 — Wald–Wolfowitz optimality

Let the SPRT with thresholds $\tilde A < 1 < \tilde B$ have error probabilities $\tilde\alpha_0, \tilde\alpha_1$ and sample size $\tilde N$. For any competing sequential test $(N, \varphi)$ with error probabilities $\alpha_0 \leq \tilde\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_1 \leq \tilde\alpha_1$, $$E_0 N \geq E_0 \tilde N \quad \text{and} \quad E_1 N \geq E_1 \tilde N.$$

The shape of the proof

Recast as a Bayesian problem with prior $\pi = P(\Theta = 1)$, loss $L(0)$ and $L(1)$ for incorrect actions, and cost $c$ per observation. Section 20.3 shows the optimal procedure has the form "stop when the posterior $\pi_n = P(\Theta = 1 \mid \mathcal{F}_n)$ leaves an interval $(\pi_-, \pi_+)$". Since $\pi_n$ is a monotone function of $L_n$, $$\pi_n = \frac{\pi L_n}{1 - \pi + \pi L_n},$$ this is equivalent to "stop when $L_n$ leaves $(A, B)$" — a SPRT. The technical lemma is that every choice of $(A, B)$ for the SPRT corresponds to some $(c, \omega)$ in the Bayesian problem, so the SPRT is optimal for every prior. A risk inequality that holds for every $\pi \in (0, 1)$ forces $E_0 \tilde N \leq E_0 N$ and $E_1 \tilde N \leq E_1 N$ separately.

Common pitfall

The SPRT is optimal for simple vs. simple. For composite hypotheses (e.g. $\theta \leq \theta_0$ vs. $\theta > \theta_0$) optimality requires more care — see one-sided SPRTs (Problem 20.8) and "power-one" tests. The expected sample size $E_\theta N$ at parameter values between $H_0$ and $H_1$ can be considerably larger than at the design points, and may even be infinite. The SPRT's optimality property is precisely at $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = 1$, not everywhere.

20.5 Sequential design — beyond stopping

The final section pushes past optional stopping. The new feature: the experimenter chooses the treatment or input for the next observation, not just whether to take it.

Stochastic approximation (Robbins–Monro)

Given a regression function $f(x) = E[Y \mid X = x]$ with unknown shape, find the root $\theta$ with $f(\theta) = t$. The classical approach — fix a design, fit a parametric model — is wasteful if you only care about one feature. Robbins and Monro (1951) propose the adaptive recursion $$X_{n+1} = X_n - a_n (Y_n - t), \quad n \geq 1, \tag{20.12}$$ with step sizes $a_n > 0$ satisfying $\sum a_n = \infty$ and $\sum a_n^2 < \infty$. Intuition: if $Y_n > t$ the response was too high, so move $X$ down; if $Y_n < t$, move $X$ up. The condition $\sum a_n = \infty$ ensures we can travel arbitrarily far from any starting point; $\sum a_n^2 < \infty$ controls noise accumulation.

Theorem 20.12 — Robbins–Monro convergence

If $f$ is continuous and strictly increasing, $\sum a_n^2 < \infty$, $\sum a_n = \infty$, and $\sup_x [\sigma(x) + |f(x)|]/(1 + |x|) < \infty$, then $X_n \to \theta$ almost surely.

With the canonical choice $a_n = c/n$ and $2c > 1/f'(\theta)$, $$\sqrt n (X_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N\!\left( 0, \frac{c^2 \sigma^2(\theta)}{2 c f'(\theta) - 1} \right),$$ and the variance is minimized by $c = 1/f'(\theta)$. The procedure is asymptotically as good as the MLE — without fitting a model.

Example 20.11 — bioassay and the ED50

The ED50 is the dose $\theta$ at which the probability of response equals $1/2$. Set $Y \in \{0, 1\}$ indicating response, $t = 1/2$, and apply (20.12). Each subject moves the dose toward the unknown median effective dose. After many trials, $X_n$ concentrates around the ED50 — without ever knowing the dose-response curve.

Bandit problems

At each stage choose one of $k$ "arms"; each pull of arm $a$ yields a reward governed by an unknown $\Theta_a$. The Bayesian formulation puts independent priors $\pi_0(a)$ on each $\Theta_a$ and asks for an allocation policy maximizing the geometric-discounted reward $$V = E \sum_{n=0}^\infty \beta^n r(A_n, \Theta_{A_n}),$$ with discount factor $\beta \in (0, 1)$. This is a famously hard problem — the optimal policy must trade off exploration (pull arms about which you know little) against exploitation (pull arms that look best so far).

Theorem 20.13 — Gittins

There exists, for each arm $a$ in isolation, a function $\nu(a, \pi)$ — the Gittins index — depending only on $a$'s own reward and posterior, such that the policy "always pull the arm with the largest index" is optimal for the $k$-armed bandit.

Why the index theorem is astonishing

Naively, the optimal policy for arm $a$ should depend on what the other arms look like — why explore $a$ if some other arm is more promising? Gittins (1979) showed that you can summarize each arm by a single number $\nu(a, \pi)$ computed in a one-arm sub-problem against a "fixed-reward" comparison arm $\lambda$. The index is the critical $\lambda$ at which immediate stopping becomes optimal: $$\nu(a, \pi_0) = \inf\!\{ \lambda : (1 - \beta) H(a, \pi_0, \lambda) = \lambda \}.$$ The original problem of size $k$ decouples into $k$ stopping problems. This is one of the most remarkable decomposition theorems in stochastic control.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 20, and what you learned in the book

Chapter 20, in eight bullets:

  1. A sequential experiment has a sample size $N$ that is itself a random variable, decided adaptively from the data.
  2. The sampling distribution of standard statistics is generally wrong under data-dependent stopping — Example 20.1 shows you can guarantee rejection of a true null just by peeking.
  3. The likelihood function, by contrast, is unaffected by the stopping rule. This is Wald's fundamental identity (Theorem 20.6) — and the reason Bayesian inference sails through unchanged.
  4. A stopping time is a random time $N$ with $\{N = n\} \in \mathcal{F}_n$. The filtration $\mathcal{F}_n$ formalizes "information available after $n$ observations".
  5. Anscombe's CLT lets us substitute a random sample size $N_w$ for a deterministic $n_w$ provided $w^2 N_w \to c$ — the basis for sequential fixed-width confidence intervals.
  6. Optimal stopping via Bayesian backwards induction balances inferential risk $\rho_N$ against sampling cost $cE N$; the Bellman equation (20.7) gives a recursive algorithm.
  7. The SPRT for simple vs. simple testing, with thresholds $A < 1 < B$ on the likelihood ratio, simultaneously minimizes both $E_0 N$ and $E_1 N$ subject to error-probability constraints (Wald–Wolfowitz, Theorem 20.9).
  8. Beyond stopping: stochastic approximation finds roots of regression functions adaptively, and the Gittins index reduces $k$-armed bandit problems to $k$ separate one-arm stopping problems.

You started in Chapter 1 with measures and $\sigma$-fields — abstract scaffolding that seemed to have nothing to do with data. You ended here, in Chapter 20, with a filtration $\mathcal{F}_n$ representing "information observed so far" and a stopping time $N$ that tells you when you have collected enough of it. The whole arc of the book is the slow population of that scaffolding with statistical content: distributions, sufficiency, estimation, testing, asymptotics, decision theory, the linear model, and now sequential design.

The single most important takeaway is not any one theorem — it is the habit of asking, for every procedure, two questions: what is its sampling distribution, and what is its likelihood. The chapters from 1 through 19 built up the language to answer these. Chapter 20 made vivid how starkly they can disagree.

Good luck. The papers you read from now on will assume you know all of this — quietly, in the background, the way Chapter 1 assumed you knew calculus. That assumption is now justified.
