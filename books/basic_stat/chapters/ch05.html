Chapter 5

Curved Exponential Families

What happens when an exponential-family parameter is forced to live on a curve — and why that breaks completeness, but rarely breaks sufficiency.

Book pages: 85–99 ~35 min reading time Prereqs: Chapter 2 (exponential families), Chapter 4 (UMVU)

Why "curved"? A picture before the math

Chapter 2 introduced the canonical exponential family $$p_\eta(x) = \exp\big(\eta \cdot T(x) - A(\eta)\big) \, h(x),$$ with natural parameter $\eta \in \Xi \subseteq \mathbb{R}^s$. The whole point of that machinery is that the natural parameter is a free Euclidean variable: you can move $\eta$ in any direction inside $\Xi$ and you stay inside the family. That freedom is what made Chapter 4's UMVU theory work — Lehmann–Scheffé needs completeness, and a full rank exponential family gives it for free.

Now we ask a question the book has been carefully postponing. What if the modeller, for some scientific reason, insists that the parameter must satisfy a constraint? Maybe two variances are known to be equal. Maybe a mean and a standard deviation are linked. Maybe the sample size itself was decided sequentially, so the family quietly picks up an extra parameter relating the stopping time to the data.

All of these scenarios share one geometric picture: instead of roaming freely over an $s$-dimensional parameter space $\Xi$, the parameter is now pinned to a lower-dimensional subset $\tilde\eta(\Omega) \subseteq \Xi$. There are two qualitatively different things that can happen.

  • The subset is an affine slice of $\Xi$ — a flat linear constraint. In this case the model collapses cleanly into a smaller, full rank exponential family.
  • The subset is a genuinely curved submanifold — a curve, a parabola, a sphere inside $\Xi$ that does not lie in any hyperplane. This is the case the chapter is named after: a curved exponential family.
The geometric picture

Think of $\Xi$ as a flat $s$-dimensional space. A linear constraint slices off a flat plane. The family stays exponential, just with fewer parameters. A nonlinear constraint slices off a curve or a curved surface — and that curvature is precisely what stops the smaller model from being a full exponential family in its own right.

Common pitfall

"Curved exponential family" does not mean "not an exponential family". A curved exponential family is an exponential family — its densities still factor as $\exp(\eta \cdot T - A) \, h$. What it lacks is full rank. The image $\tilde\eta(\Omega)$ in the natural parameter space is lower-dimensional than $\Xi$, and it bends. So the family is exponential but "constrained", not "broken".

5.1 Constrained families

The setup

Start with a full rank $s$-parameter canonical exponential family $$\mathcal{P} = \{P_\eta : \eta \in \Xi\}$$ with complete sufficient statistic $T$. (From Chapter 2: full rank means $\Xi$ contains an open set in $\mathbb{R}^s$, and $T$ does not lie in any affine subspace.) Now consider a submodel $\mathcal{P}_0$ parameterized by a new parameter $\theta \in \Omega$: $$\mathcal{P}_0 = \{P_{\tilde\eta(\theta)} : \theta \in \Omega\},$$ where $\tilde\eta : \Omega \to \Xi$ is the embedding that says "for each $\theta$, use natural parameter $\tilde\eta(\theta)$".

Usually $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^r$ with $r < s$. So we are restricting the natural parameter to live in an $r$-dimensional image $\tilde\eta(\Omega) \subseteq \Xi$.

Definition — the two cases

Let $\mathcal{P}_0 \subsetneq \mathcal{P}$ as above.

  1. If the points of $\tilde\eta(\Omega)$ satisfy a nontrivial linear constraint in $\mathbb{R}^s$, then $\mathcal{P}_0$ is itself a $q$-parameter full rank exponential family for some $q < s$. The original $T$ remains sufficient but is no longer minimal.
  2. If the points of $\tilde\eta(\Omega)$ do not satisfy any linear constraint, then $\mathcal{P}_0$ is called a curved exponential family. Here $T$ is minimal sufficient but typically not complete.

Why a linear constraint collapses the family

Suppose $\tilde\eta(\Omega)$ sits inside a hyperplane $\{\eta : a \cdot \eta = c\}$ for some $a \in \mathbb{R}^s$ and constant $c$. Then for every $\theta \in \Omega$, $$\eta \cdot T(x) = (\text{component along } a)\,\underbrace{(a \cdot T(x))}_{\text{fixed combo}} + (\text{free components}) \cdot (\text{other combos of } T).$$ The constrained combination $a \cdot T$ behaves like a constant in the exponent (up to the fixed multiplier $c$) and can be absorbed into the baseline. What is left is a genuine $q$-parameter exponential family in the remaining free directions. No curvature, no loss of completeness — the framework of Chapter 4 still applies, just with fewer effective parameters.

Why a curved (nonlinear) constraint is different

If $\tilde\eta(\Omega)$ is not contained in any hyperplane, no linear combination $a \cdot T$ is redundant. The statistic $T$ has nowhere to collapse, so it remains minimal sufficient. But curvature manufactures something new: certain functions $g(T)$ end up having zero mean for every $\theta \in \Omega$, even though they are not zero as random variables. By the definition of completeness — "$E_\theta g(T) = 0$ for all $\theta$ implies $g = 0$ a.s." — the existence of such a $g$ is exactly a witness that $T$ is not complete. Curvature creates "extra" identities that the full family did not have.

The intuition in one sentence

When the parameter set bends, the moments of $T$ get tied together by relations that hold along the entire curve. Each such relation is a nontrivial function of $T$ with mean zero — and that destroys completeness, even when sufficiency survives.

Example 5.1 — the normal family with linked moments

Take $X_1, \dots, X_n \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Chapter 2 (Example 2.3) gave the canonical natural parameter and complete sufficient statistic $$\eta = \left( \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}, \, -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \right), \qquad T = \left( \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \, \sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2 \right).$$ This is a full rank two-parameter family. Now look at two constrained submodels.

Worked example — $\mu = \sigma^2 = \theta$ (linear case)

Set $\mu = \sigma^2 = \theta$, with $\theta > 0$. Then $$\tilde\eta(\theta) = \left( 1, \, -\frac{1}{2\theta} \right).$$ Look at the picture: the first coordinate is locked to $1$, while the second sweeps out a ray. The image is the horizontal line $\eta_1 = 1$ — a linear constraint. By case 1 of the definition, $\mathcal{P}_0$ is a one-parameter exponential family. Indeed, plugging $\tilde\eta$ into the density and absorbing the constant term, the submodel turns out to be a full rank one-parameter exponential family with canonical statistic $\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^2$ alone. The old $T = (\sum X_i, \sum X_i^2)$ is still sufficient but no longer minimal — the first coordinate is just informational redundancy.

Worked example — $\sigma = |\mu|$, i.e. $X_i \sim N(\theta, \theta^2)$ (curved case)

Now constrain the variance to be the square of the mean: $\sigma^2 = \theta^2$ with $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $$\tilde\eta(\theta) = \left( \frac{1}{\theta}, \, -\frac{1}{2\theta^2} \right).$$ Eliminating $\theta$ gives the relation $\eta_2 = -\tfrac12 \eta_1^2$. The image is a parabola in the natural parameter plane — not a line. This is the textbook example of a curved exponential family.

Sufficiency survives: $T = (\sum X_i, \sum X_i^2)$ is still minimal sufficient for the submodel (the chapter cites Example 3.12). But completeness fails. To see it, compute the moments of $T$ on the curve, writing $T_1 = \sum X_i$ and $T_2 = \sum X_i^2$:

$E_\theta T_1 = n\theta$ and $\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T_1) = n\theta^2$, so $$E_\theta T_1^2 = (E_\theta T_1)^2 + \operatorname{Var}_\theta(T_1) = n^2 \theta^2 + n \theta^2.$$ Meanwhile $E_\theta T_2 = n E_\theta X_i^2 = n(\theta^2 + \theta^2) = 2 n \theta^2$. Combining, $$E_\theta\!\left( 2 T_1^2 - (n+1) T_2 \right) = 2(n^2 + n)\theta^2 - 2n(n+1)\theta^2 = 0$$ for every $\theta$. So the function $g(T) = 2 T_1^2 - (n+1) T_2$ is a nontrivial function of $T$ with mean zero everywhere on $\Omega$. Hence $T$ is not complete (unless $n = 1$, where $g$ happens to vanish).

Pause — why did this work?

The mean and variance of the normal distribution are tied to different coordinates of $T$. In the unrestricted family those two coordinates can be tuned independently, so no polynomial in $T$ can have mean zero across the whole parameter space. The constraint $\sigma^2 = \theta^2$ ties them together algebraically — and a polynomial identity in $\theta$ becomes available, manifesting as an identity in moments. Curvature literally creates the zero-mean function.

The deeper moral: each "extra" algebraic relation between $\tilde\eta$'s coordinates corresponds to an "extra" zero-mean function of $T$ — and each such function obstructs completeness.

Example 5.2 — two normal samples, three subfamilies

Let $X_1, \dots, X_m \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} N(\mu_x, \sigma_x^2)$ and $Y_1, \dots, Y_n \stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim} N(\mu_y, \sigma_y^2)$ be independent samples. The joint distributions form a four-parameter exponential family with $$T = \left( \sum X_i, \, \sum Y_j, \, \sum X_i^2, \, \sum Y_j^2 \right), \qquad \eta = \left( \frac{\mu_x}{\sigma_x^2}, \, \frac{\mu_y}{\sigma_y^2}, \, -\frac{1}{2\sigma_x^2}, \, -\frac{1}{2\sigma_y^2} \right).$$ Equivalently we can summarise via $(\bar X, \bar Y, S_x^2, S_y^2)$.

Three submodels make the linear-vs-curved distinction concrete:

  • Equal variances $\sigma_x^2 = \sigma_y^2$. The natural-parameter constraint is $\eta_3 = \eta_4$ — a hyperplane. By case 1, the submodel is a full rank three-parameter exponential family with complete sufficient statistic $(T_1, T_2, T_3 + T_4)$, equivalent to $(\bar X, \bar Y, S_p^2)$ where $$S_p^2 = \frac{(m-1) S_x^2 + (n-1) S_y^2}{n + m - 2}$$ is the pooled sample variance. UMVU machinery applies.
  • Equal means $\mu_x = \mu_y$. The natural-parameter constraint relates $\eta_1, \eta_2, \eta_3, \eta_4$ nonlinearly (it forces $\eta_1 / \eta_3 = \eta_2 / \eta_4$), so this is a curved exponential family. The full $T$ remains minimal sufficient but is not complete: under the constraint $E_\theta(\bar X - \bar Y) = 0$ for all parameter values, so $\bar X - \bar Y$ is a nonzero function of $T$ with mean zero.
  • Equal variances and equal means — a further submodel of the equal-variance one. This is itself a linearly constrained submodel of the previous case and stays full-rank exponential.
Reading the structure

Equal variances is a constraint between the two "$-1/(2\sigma^2)$" coordinates of $\eta$ — both of which sit in the same linear "scale" subspace. The constraint is a hyperplane there. Equal means is a constraint that involves ratios of $\eta$'s coordinates — mixing location and scale — and ratios produce curves, not lines.

5.2 Sequential experiments

What changes when sampling is sequential

In a classical experiment the sample size $n$ is fixed before any data arrive. In a sequential experiment the decision to keep sampling, stop, or switch populations is made on the fly, using whatever data have come in so far. Two motivations:

  • Efficiency. Stopping early once the answer is clear saves data; collecting more once it isn't shrinks variance. Quantified through decision-theoretic risk, with a sampling cost added to the loss function.
  • Feasibility. Some targets cannot be estimated unbiasedly without a sequential rule, as the next example shows.

Example 5.3 — estimating a population size

A lake contains $M$ fish; $M$ is the unknown of interest. A capture–recapture protocol tags $k$ fish, returns them, and then resamples; each recaptured fish is independently tagged with probability $\theta = k / M$. So estimating $M$ amounts to estimating $1/\theta$.

Code the recapture results as Bernoulli $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ with success probability $\theta$. With a fixed sample size $N = n$, the joint density is $$\theta^{T(x)} (1 - \theta)^{n - T(x)}, \qquad T(x) = x_1 + \cdots + x_n,$$ a one-parameter exponential family with $T \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, \theta)$. Then $T/n$ is UMVU for $\theta$ — but the parameter we care about is $1/\theta$, and the following obstruction kills any unbiased estimator.

Why no unbiased estimator of $1/\theta$ exists

Any candidate $\delta(T)$ depends on $T \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}$, so it is bounded: $E_\theta \delta(T) \leq \max_k \delta(k)$. But $1/\theta$ is unbounded as $\theta \to 0$. So no fixed-$n$ binomial estimator can be unbiased for $1/\theta$. The deeper symptom: when $\theta$ is much smaller than $1/n$, $T = 0$ with very high probability, and from a string of zeros we cannot tell whether $\theta$ is tiny or merely small.

The fix is inverse binomial sampling: keep collecting Bernoulli draws until $m$ ones have appeared. Now $m$ is fixed and $N$ is random, the reverse of the previous setup. Let $Y_i$ be the number of failures between the $(i-1)$st and $i$th success. Then $Y_1, \dots, Y_m$ are i.i.d. geometric with mass function $$P_\theta(Y_i = y) = (1 - \theta)^y \theta = \exp\!\left( y \log(1 - \theta) + \log \theta \right), \quad y = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$ This is a one-parameter exponential family with natural parameter $\eta = \log(1 - \theta)$ and $A(\eta) = -\log(1 - e^\eta)$. Sufficient statistic $T = \sum_{i=1}^m Y_i = N - m$ has negative binomial distribution; $E_\theta T = m/\theta - m$, so $$\widehat{1/\theta} = \frac{T + m}{m} = \frac{N}{m}$$ is unbiased — and UMVU, since the family is full rank.

Pause — why does this work?

Fixed-$n$ binomial sampling capped the possible values of the sufficient statistic, and that cap capped any unbiased estimator. Inverse binomial sampling leaves $N$ unbounded above. The parameter we cannot reach with bounded data ($1/\theta$ at small $\theta$) becomes reachable once the sample size itself can grow with the difficulty of the problem.

The general result on sequential densities

A stopping time $N$ is a random sample size that depends only on past data, never on the future: $\{N = n\} = \{(X_1, \dots, X_n) \in A_n\}$ for some sequence of sets $A_n$. The total observed data are informally written $(N, X_1, \dots, X_N)$, with $P_\theta(N < \infty) = 1$.

Theorem 5.4 — densities under a stopping time

If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with common density $f_\theta$ and $N$ is a stopping time with $P_\theta(N < \infty) = 1$, then the joint density of the observed data is $$\prod_{i=1}^n f_\theta(x_i). \tag{5.1}$$ When $f_\theta$ comes from an exponential family $f_\theta(x) = e^{\eta(\theta) \cdot T(x) - B(\theta)} h(x)$, the joint density becomes $$\exp\!\left[ \eta(\theta) \cdot \sum_{i=1}^n T(x_i) - n B(\theta) \right] \prod_{i=1}^n h(x_i), \tag{5.2}$$ an exponential family with canonical parameters $\eta_1(\theta), \dots, \eta_s(\theta), -B(\theta)$ and sufficient statistic $\big( \sum_{i=1}^N T(X_i), \, N \big)$.

The key observation

Notice that the sequential likelihood has the same algebraic form as the fixed-$n$ likelihood. The price of using a stopping rule is hidden, not in the likelihood, but in the parameter set: equation (5.2) has $s + 1$ natural parameters $\eta_1(\theta), \dots, \eta_s(\theta), -B(\theta)$, all of which are functions of the same $r$-dimensional $\theta$. Generically the image $\tilde\eta(\Omega)$ is an $r$-dimensional curved submanifold of an $(s+1)$-dimensional natural-parameter space.

In other words: sequential experiments typically produce curved exponential families.

Likelihood vs. sampling distribution

The likelihood is unaffected by optional stopping — equation (5.1) doesn't even mention the stopping rule. But the sampling distribution of common estimators is affected. For example, $(X_1 + \cdots + X_N) / N$ is generally a biased estimator of $E_\theta X_1$ when $N$ is data-dependent. So Bayesian inference ignores the stopping rule; frequentist bias and variance do not.

The inverse-binomial example above is unusual: there $\sum_{i=1}^N X_i$ was constrained to equal $m$ exactly, which collapsed the extra dimension and left a full rank one-parameter family. In most sequential schemes nothing forces such a cancellation, and the family ends up curved.

5.3 Multinomial distribution and contingency tables

The multinomial as a full rank exponential family

Run $n$ independent trials, each yielding one of $s + 1$ outcomes $a_0, \dots, a_s$ with probabilities $p_0, \dots, p_s$ (where $\sum_j p_j = 1$). Let $X_j$ be the number of trials with outcome $a_j$, and stack them into $X = (X_0, X_1, \dots, X_s)$. Then $$P(X_0 = x_0, \dots, X_s = x_s) = \binom{n}{x_0, \dots, x_s} \prod_{j=0}^s p_j^{x_j}, \qquad \sum_j x_j = n,$$ the multinomial distribution, written $X \sim \mathrm{Multinomial}(p_0, \dots, p_s; n)$.

Writing the joint mass function of the underlying trial labels in exponential-family form is the natural starting point. Using the constraint $\sum_j X_j = n$ to eliminate $X_0$, $$P(Y_1 = y_1, \dots, Y_n = y_n) = \exp\!\left[ \sum_{i=1}^s x_i \log(p_i / p_0) + n \log p_0 \right],$$ a full rank $s$-parameter exponential family with natural parameters $\eta_i = \log(p_i / p_0)$ (log-odds against the baseline outcome $a_0$) and canonical complete sufficient statistic $(X_1, \dots, X_s)$. Marginally each $X_j \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, p_j)$, so $X_j / n$ is UMVU for $p_j$.

Why exclude $X_0$?

Treating all $s + 1$ probabilities as free parameters would give a family of rank $s$ embedded in a natural-parameter space of dimension $s + 1$ — failing to be full rank because of the constraint $\sum p_j = 1$. Picking a "reference cell" $a_0$ and using log-odds-vs-baseline parameters $\log(p_i / p_0)$ removes the redundancy and makes the family full rank. This is the same logic behind softmax / multinomial-logit parameterizations in modern machine learning.

Estimating products of probabilities

Estimating a single $p_j$ is easy. Estimating a product $p_j p_k$ ($j \neq k$) is more delicate because $X_j$ and $X_k$ are dependent (they jointly sum with the others to $n$). Take the indicator $\delta = 1\{Y_1 = a_j, Y_2 = a_k\}$ as a starting unbiased estimator (since $E_\theta \delta = p_j p_k$), then Rao–Blackwellise on the complete sufficient $X$: $$E[\delta \mid X = x] = \frac{x_j x_k}{n(n-1)}.$$ Hence the UMVU estimator of $p_j p_k$ is $X_j X_k / (n^2 - n)$. This is a small but instructive computation — it is the kind of pattern you will use repeatedly in contingency-table inference.

Example 5.5 — two-way contingency tables under independence

Suppose each trial records two characteristics: characteristic $A$ with levels $A_1, \dots, A_I$ and characteristic $B$ with levels $B_1, \dots, B_J$. Let $N_{ij}$ count trials with combination $A_i B_j$ and $p_{ij}$ be its probability. Marginal counts and probabilities are $$N_{i+} = \sum_j N_{ij}, \quad N_{+j} = \sum_i N_{ij}, \quad p_{i+} = \sum_j p_{ij}, \quad p_{+j} = \sum_i p_{ij}.$$

The independence model assumes $p_{ij} = p_{i+} p_{+j}$ for all $i, j$. Substituting this constraint into the multinomial mass function and collecting terms gives $$\binom{n}{n_{11}, \dots, n_{IJ}} \exp\!\left[ \sum_{i=2}^I n_{i+} \log\!\frac{p_{i+}}{p_{1+}} + \sum_{j=2}^J n_{+j} \log\!\frac{p_{+j}}{p_{+1}} + n \log(p_{1+} p_{+1}) \right].$$ This is a full rank $(I + J - 2)$-parameter exponential family with canonical sufficient statistic $(N_{2+}, \dots, N_{I+}, N_{+2}, \dots, N_{+J})$ — equivalently the row and column totals. Under independence, $N_{i+} \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, p_{i+})$ and $N_{+j} \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, p_{+j})$ are independent, so $$\widehat{p_{ij}} = \widehat{p_{i+}} \, \widehat{p_{+j}} = \frac{N_{i+} N_{+j}}{n^2}$$ is UMVU for $p_{ij}$ in the independence model.

Pause — what just happened geometrically?

The unrestricted multinomial on an $I \times J$ table is an $(IJ - 1)$-parameter exponential family. The independence constraint $p_{ij} = p_{i+} p_{+j}$ is nonlinear in the $p$'s, yet the resulting submodel turned out to be a full rank $(I + J - 2)$-parameter exponential family — not curved.

The reason: reparameterize by natural log-odds. Independence is equivalent to saying $\log p_{ij} = (\text{row term})_i + (\text{column term})_j$, which is additive — i.e. affine in a chosen basis of $\eta$-space. A constraint that looks curved in one parameterization can be linear in another. "Curved" really means "not linear in any parameterization of the natural parameter". Independence is genuinely linear once you switch to log-odds.

Example 5.6 — conditional independence

With three characteristics $A$, $B$, $C$ and counts $N_{ijk}$, suppose $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $C$: $$p_{ijk} = p_{++k} \cdot \frac{p_{i+k}}{p_{++k}} \cdot \frac{p_{+jk}}{p_{++k}}.$$ A similar log-odds reparameterization shows that this gives a full rank $\big( K(I + J - 1) - 1 \big)$-parameter exponential family with sufficient statistics $\{N_{++k}\}_k$, $\{N_{i+k}\}_{i,k}$, $\{N_{+jk}\}_{j,k}$ — the within-$C$ row and column totals. Again the constraint is multiplicative in $p$ but linear in log-odds, so the model remains full rank.

When is a constraint genuinely curved?

In contingency-table models built from independence or conditional independence assumptions, the constraints are always multiplicative in the cell probabilities — equivalently additive in the log-cell-probabilities — which is the right parameterization for the natural parameter. So they collapse to full rank. By contrast, the equal-means two-sample normal problem of Example 5.2 imposed a constraint involving ratios $\mu/\sigma^2$ between location and scale coordinates — and those mix non-trivially in $\eta$-space, producing genuine curvature.

When the multinomial does get curved

Not every constraint on cell probabilities is additive in log-odds. Two examples from the chapter's problems hint at where curvature appears:

  • Marginal symmetry: in a square table, requiring $p_{i+} = p_{+i}$ for all $i$. This mixes row and column probabilities in a way that is not log-additive, and generally produces a curved exponential family.
  • Dependence parameter linked to a covariate: in a stack of $2 \times 2$ tables where the cell probabilities depend on $\theta_k$ and $\log\!\big[(1 + \theta_k)/(1 - \theta_k)\big]$ is forced to be a linear function of a known covariate $x_k$. The covariate ties cell probabilities across tables in a nonlinear way; the resulting family is exponential but curved.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 5

One chapter, four take-home messages:

  1. A curved exponential family is an exponential family whose natural parameter is restricted to a subset of $\Xi$ that bends — does not lie in any hyperplane. It is still an exponential family; it is just not full rank.
  2. If the constraint on $\eta$ is linear, the submodel collapses to a smaller full-rank exponential family — $T$ is no longer minimal but the Chapter 4 UMVU machinery still applies to the reduced statistic.
  3. If the constraint is nonlinear, $T$ remains minimal sufficient, but it is generally not complete: the curvature produces nontrivial functions $g(T)$ with mean zero for every $\theta$. Without completeness, Lehmann–Scheffé does not deliver UMVU estimators automatically.
  4. Sequential experiments add an extra natural parameter $-B(\theta)$ to the exponent and so almost always yield curved families. Multinomial models under independence or conditional independence look multiplicative but are linear in log-odds — full rank, not curved. Other multinomial constraints (marginal symmetry, covariate-linked dependence) can be genuinely curved.

  • What property of $T$ is typically lost when the constraint is nonlinear, and which survives?
  • In the $N(\theta, \theta^2)$ example, write down a function $g(T)$ with $E_\theta g(T) = 0$ for all $\theta$.
  • Why does inverse binomial sampling allow unbiased estimation of $1/\theta$, while binomial sampling does not?
  • Why is the multinomial independence model full rank rather than curved, even though $p_{ij} = p_{i+} p_{+j}$ looks multiplicative?
