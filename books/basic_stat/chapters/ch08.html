Chapter 8

Large-Sample Theory

Three modes of convergence, one central limit theorem, and the vocabulary that runs every asymptotic argument in statistics.

Book pages: 129–149 ~50 min reading time Prereqs: Ch 1–7, calculus, a little real analysis

Why does statistics need large-sample theory?

Up to now, the book has been about exact properties. An estimator is unbiased or not; sufficient or not; minimum variance or not. These exact properties are wonderful when you can get them, but the world is not always so kind. Two estimators may both be reasonable, neither one exactly unbiased, both with intractable distributions for finite $n$. How are we supposed to choose?

The answer that runs nearly every statistics paper since 1900: ask what happens as the sample size grows. If two estimators both converge to the truth, look at the shape and scale of their errors after suitable magnification. Whichever has the smaller asymptotic variance wins. This is a deal: we trade exactness for tractability.

The promise of asymptotics

A handful of limit theorems — the law of large numbers, the central limit theorem, the delta method, Slutsky's theorem — covers the vast majority of inference problems. The cost is that every conclusion comes with the asterisk "for $n$ large enough". Most of this chapter is about learning what that asterisk means precisely.

This is also where readers most often get confused. We are going to define three different notions of convergence for random variables, and the differences between them are subtle. We will go slowly. By the end you should be able to look at a symbol like $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} Y$ or $Y_n \Rightarrow Y$ and know exactly what is being claimed — and just as importantly, what is not.

8.1 Convergence in Probability

The intuition first

Imagine a sequence of measurements $Y_1, Y_2, \dots$ — say, repeated estimates of some true value $Y$. We want to say "the estimates get close to $Y$". But each $Y_n$ is random, so "close" is itself a probabilistic statement. The simplest version is:

Pick a tolerance $\epsilon > 0$. The probability that $Y_n$ misses $Y$ by at least $\epsilon$ should shrink to zero.

That is the entire content of convergence in probability. Now in symbols.

Definition 8.1 — convergence in probability

A sequence of random variables $Y_n$ converges in probability to $Y$, written $$Y_n \xrightarrow{p} Y,$$ if for every $\epsilon > 0$, $$P\big(\,|Y_n - Y| \geq \epsilon\,\big) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

Reading the definition

Two quantifiers: "for every $\epsilon$" comes first, then "as $n \to \infty$". So we are not saying "pick $n$ large enough and then $Y_n = Y$". We are saying that for any tolerance the user names, eventually $Y_n$ lands within that tolerance of $Y$ with arbitrarily high probability.

In particular, $Y_n$ does not need to equal $Y$ ever. It only needs to be close most of the time.

Chebyshev — the one-line route to convergence in probability

Almost every elementary proof of convergence in probability uses one inequality.

Theorem 8.2 — Chebyshev's inequality

For any random variable $X$ and any constant $a > 0$, $$P\big(\,|X| \geq a\,\big) \leq \frac{E X^2}{a^2}.$$

The proof fits on one line. For every outcome, $$\mathbf{1}\{|X| \geq a\} \leq \frac{X^2}{a^2}$$ because the right-hand side is $\geq 1$ exactly when $|X| \geq a$, and is $\geq 0$ always. Take expectations of both sides.

Now plug this into the definition. If $E(Y_n - Y)^2 \to 0$, then for every $\epsilon > 0$, $$P\big(\,|Y_n - Y| \geq \epsilon\,\big) \leq \frac{E(Y_n - Y)^2}{\epsilon^2} \to 0,$$ so $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} Y$. This is Keener's Proposition 8.3. In words: mean-square convergence implies convergence in probability.

The weak law of large numbers

Worked example — WLLN for the sample mean

Suppose $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$. Let $\bar{X}_n = (X_1 + \cdots + X_n)/n$. Then $$E(\bar{X}_n - \mu)^2 = \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \to 0,$$ and so $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$.

This is the weak law of large numbers: averages converge in probability to the population mean. Note that the proof took two lines once we had Chebyshev — that is the power of mean-square convergence as a shortcut.

Two pedagogical notes. First, the WLLN actually holds whenever $E|X_i| < \infty$, even if $\sigma^2 = \infty$ — but proving that requires more work. Second, the WLLN says nothing about how fast convergence happens. For that we will need the central limit theorem in Section 8.2.

Continuous functions preserve convergence in probability

Proposition 8.5 — continuous mapping (in probability)

If $f$ is continuous at $c$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} c$, then $f(Y_n) \xrightarrow{p} f(c)$.

The argument is the kind of $\epsilon$–$\delta$ trick you have seen in real analysis. Continuity at $c$ means: given any $\epsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta_\epsilon > 0$ such that $|y - c| < \delta_\epsilon$ implies $|f(y) - f(c)| < \epsilon$. Contrapositively, $|f(y) - f(c)| \geq \epsilon$ implies $|y - c| \geq \delta_\epsilon$. Hence $$P\big(\,|f(Y_n) - f(c)| \geq \epsilon\,\big) \leq P\big(\,|Y_n - c| \geq \delta_\epsilon\,\big) \to 0.$$

This proposition, applied component-wise to random vectors, immediately gives us:

If $X_n \xrightarrow{p} a$ and $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} b$, then $X_n + Y_n \xrightarrow{p} a + b$ and $X_n Y_n \xrightarrow{p} ab$.

Addition and multiplication are continuous functions $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$, so the result follows by treating $(X_n, Y_n)$ as a random vector.

Consistent estimators

When we have a parametric family indexed by $\theta \in \Omega$, we write $\xrightarrow{P_\theta}$ to remind ourselves which probability measure is in play.

Definition 8.6 — consistency

A sequence of estimators $\delta_n$ is consistent for $g(\theta)$ if for every $\theta \in \Omega$, $$\delta_n \xrightarrow{P_\theta} g(\theta) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

Consistency is the bare minimum we ask of an estimator. If your estimator is not consistent, more data does not help — it converges to the wrong thing, or to nothing at all.

By Chebyshev again, a sufficient condition is that the mean-squared error vanishes: $$R(\theta, \delta_n) = E_\theta (\delta_n - g(\theta))^2 = b_n(\theta)^2 + \operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta_n) \to 0,$$ where $b_n(\theta) = E_\theta \delta_n - g(\theta)$ is the bias. So: bias and variance both shrinking to zero implies consistency. This is the workhorse check.

Pause

Re-read the definition once more. "For every $\epsilon > 0$, $P(|Y_n - Y| \geq \epsilon) \to 0$" is one of those statements that looks simple but encodes two layers of "for all". The user picks $\epsilon$; then $n$ goes to infinity. The probability shrinks at a rate that may depend on $\epsilon$ — small $\epsilon$ takes longer.

8.2 Convergence in Distribution

What convergence in probability misses

Suppose $\delta_n$ is consistent for $g(\theta)$, so $\delta_n - g(\theta) \xrightarrow{p} 0$. Great — but how fast? And what does the distribution of the error look like, after we zoom in by the right factor? Convergence in probability cannot answer these. For that we need a notion of convergence that tracks the whole shape of the distribution.

Definition 8.7 — convergence in distribution

A sequence of random variables $Y_n$, with CDFs $H_n$, converges in distribution (or "in law") to $Y$ with CDF $H$, written $$Y_n \Rightarrow Y \quad \text{or} \quad Y_n \Rightarrow P_Y,$$ if $$H_n(y) \to H(y) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$ at every $y$ where $H$ is continuous.

Why "only at continuity points"?

Example 8.8 — the degenerate sequence

Let $Y_n = 1/n$ deterministically, and let $Y = 0$. Intuitively $Y_n \to Y$ in every reasonable sense. The CDFs are step functions: $$H_n(y) = \mathbf{1}\{1/n \leq y\}, \qquad H(y) = \mathbf{1}\{0 \leq y\}.$$ For $y > 0$: $H_n(y) = 1$ eventually, so $H_n(y) \to 1 = H(y)$. For $y < 0$: $H_n(y) = 0$, $H(y) = 0$. But at $y = 0$: $H_n(0) = 0$ for all $n$, while $H(0) = 1$.

Convergence fails at $y = 0$ — which is exactly the discontinuity point of $H$. If we demanded convergence there, we would have to declare that $1/n$ does not converge to $0$, which is absurd. So we only ask for convergence at continuity points.

An equivalent definition via expectations

Theorem 8.9 — Portmanteau (the useful direction)

$Y_n \Rightarrow Y$ if and only if $E f(Y_n) \to E f(Y)$ for every bounded continuous function $f$.

This is often taken as the real definition, because it generalizes cleanly to random vectors and even random functions. For our purposes, the most immediate payoff is:

Continuous mapping (in distribution). If $g$ is continuous and $Y_n \Rightarrow Y$, then $g(Y_n) \Rightarrow g(Y)$. The proof is a one-liner using Theorem 8.9: if $f$ is bounded continuous, so is $f \circ g$, so $E(f \circ g)(Y_n) \to E(f \circ g)(Y)$, which is exactly what the equivalent definition demands for $g(Y_n) \Rightarrow g(Y)$.

The central limit theorem

Theorem 8.12 — Central Limit Theorem

Suppose $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$. Let $\bar{X}_n = (X_1 + \cdots + X_n)/n$. Then $$\sqrt{n}\,(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \Rightarrow N(0, \sigma^2).$$

Two things to notice. First, the scaling is $\sqrt{n}$ — not $n$, not $n^2$. This is the right rate for averages, and the CLT is the reason. Second, the limit is normal regardless of the original distribution of the $X_i$ (as long as $\sigma^2 < \infty$). This universality is what makes the CLT so absurdly useful.

As a quick application: if $H_n$ is the CDF of $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)$, then $$P\!\left( \mu - \frac{a}{\sqrt{n}} < \bar{X}_n \leq \mu + \frac{a}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = H_n(a) - H_n(-a) \to \Phi(a/\sigma) - \Phi(-a/\sigma).$$ Compare to what the WLLN gives us: only that the probability $\to 1$ as the window widens. The CLT is sharper by a whole order of magnitude.

Refined error bounds exist. The Berry–Esseen theorem bounds the difference between $P(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \leq x)$ and $\Phi(x/\sigma)$ by a constant times $E|X_1 - \mu|^3 / (\sigma^3 \sqrt{n})$, uniformly in $x$. So the CLT approximation has $O(1/\sqrt{n})$ error.

Slutsky's theorem — the calculus of mixed convergence

Theorem 8.13 — Slutsky

If $Y_n \Rightarrow Y$, $A_n \xrightarrow{p} a$, and $B_n \xrightarrow{p} b$, then $$A_n + B_n Y_n \Rightarrow a + b\,Y.$$

Slutsky's theorem is your bookkeeping tool whenever you have a "main" term converging in distribution and "side" terms converging in probability to constants. It is what lets us replace consistent estimators of nuisance parameters with their limits inside CLT-type statements — a move that happens in nearly every applied asymptotic argument.

The delta method

What if our statistic is a smooth function of an average, not the average itself? Taylor expand.

Proposition 8.14 — the delta method

Under the conditions of the CLT, if $f$ is differentiable at $\mu$, then $$\sqrt{n}\,\big(f(\bar{X}_n) - f(\mu)\big) \Rightarrow N\!\big(0,\, [f'(\mu)]^2 \sigma^2 \big).$$

The sketch (with a continuous derivative for simplicity): by the mean value theorem, $f(\bar{X}_n) = f(\mu) + f'(\mu_n)(\bar{X}_n - \mu)$ for some $\mu_n$ between $\bar{X}_n$ and $\mu$. Since $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$, also $\mu_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$, hence $f'(\mu_n) \xrightarrow{p} f'(\mu)$ by continuity. Then Slutsky gives $$\sqrt{n}\,(f(\bar{X}_n) - f(\mu)) = f'(\mu_n) \cdot \sqrt{n}\,(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \Rightarrow f'(\mu) \cdot Z,$$ with $Z \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$.

Pause — the three modes side by side

Let us pause and be explicit. We have so far defined two notions:

  • In probability: $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} Y$ means $P(|Y_n - Y| \geq \epsilon) \to 0$ for every $\epsilon > 0$. The variables $Y_n, Y$ must live on the same probability space — we are comparing their values.
  • In distribution: $Y_n \Rightarrow Y$ means the CDFs $H_n$ converge to $H$ at continuity points. The $Y_n$ and $Y$ need not even live on the same space — we are only comparing laws.

A third mode (almost sure) is coming in Section 8.7. The hierarchy will be: $$\text{a.s.} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{in probability} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{in distribution.}$$ Strict implications — none of the reverse arrows hold in general.

Common pitfall

Convergence in distribution does NOT imply convergence in probability. Suppose $Y \sim N(0,1)$ and let $Y_n = -Y$ for every $n$. Then $Y_n \sim N(0,1)$ for every $n$ (because the normal is symmetric), so trivially $Y_n \Rightarrow Y$ — they share a distribution. But $|Y_n - Y| = 2|Y|$, which does not go to zero in probability at all. The CDFs agree exactly, yet the variables can be far apart on every outcome.

Moral: convergence in distribution says nothing about joint behavior of $Y_n$ and $Y$. It only constrains their marginals.

The one exception is when the limit is a constant: $Y_n \Rightarrow c$ does imply $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} c$. Constants have no "joint" structure to lose.

Uniform integrability — when expectations also converge

Convergence in distribution gives $E f(Y_n) \to E f(Y)$ for bounded continuous $f$. If $f$ is unbounded, this can fail. The fix is a moment-type condition.

Definition 8.15 — uniform integrability

Random variables $X_n$ are uniformly integrable if $$\sup_{n \geq 1} E\!\left[\, |X_n| \, \mathbf{1}\{|X_n| \geq t\} \,\right] \to 0 \quad \text{as } t \to \infty.$$

In plain language: no matter how far out into the tail you go, eventually the tail mass is small uniformly across the sequence. A bounded moment $\sup_n E|X_n|^{1+\epsilon} < \infty$ is enough to guarantee uniform integrability. The headline consequence (Theorem 8.16): if $X_n \Rightarrow X$ and $\{X_n\}$ is uniformly integrable, then $E X_n \to E X$.

8.3 Maximum Likelihood Estimation

The construction

Let $X$ be data with density $p_\theta$. View this density, evaluated at the observed data and read as a function of $\theta$, as the likelihood: $$L(\theta) = p_\theta(X).$$ The maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) $\hat\theta = \hat\theta(X)$ is the value of $\theta$ that maximizes $L$. Equivalently — and almost always more conveniently — it maximizes the log-likelihood $\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta)$.

Two reasons this works in practice. First, logarithms turn products into sums, so $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log p_\theta(X_i)$ when we have an i.i.d. sample. Second, the maximum of $\log$ is achieved at the same place as the maximum of the original, since $\log$ is strictly increasing.

A useful invariance fact: the MLE of $g(\theta)$ is $g(\hat\theta)$. This means once you have the MLE for a parameter, the MLE of any reparameterization comes for free.

Example 8.17 — MLE in an exponential family

For a canonical one-parameter exponential family $$p_\eta(x) = \exp\{\eta T(x) - A(\eta)\}\, h(x),$$ with an i.i.d. sample of size $n$, the log-likelihood is $$\ell(\eta) = \eta \sum_{i=1}^n T(X_i) - n A(\eta) + \text{const}.$$ Setting $\ell'(\eta) = 0$ gives $\sum T(X_i) - n A'(\eta) = 0$, i.e. $A'(\hat\eta) = \bar{T}$ where $\bar{T} = n^{-1} \sum T(X_i)$. With $\psi = (A')^{-1}$, the MLE is $$\hat\eta = \psi(\bar{T}).$$ Since $\ell''(\eta) = -A''(\eta) = -\operatorname{Var}_\eta(T) < 0$, the log-likelihood is strictly concave — there is a unique maximum.

Asymptotic distribution via the delta method

In the exponential-family example, $\bar{T}$ is an average of i.i.d. variables, so the CLT applies: $\sqrt{n}(\bar{T} - A'(\eta)) \Rightarrow N(0, A''(\eta))$. And $\hat\eta = \psi(\bar{T})$ is a smooth function of an average. The delta method then gives $$\sqrt{n}\,(\hat\eta - \eta) \Rightarrow N\!\left( 0,\, \frac{1}{A''(\eta)} \right).$$ But $A''(\eta)$ is precisely the Fisher information from a single observation. So the asymptotic variance of $\hat\eta$ equals the inverse Fisher information — the Cramér–Rao lower bound. In this asymptotic sense, the MLE is efficient: it achieves the best variance any unbiased estimator could hope for. A general theory of asymptotic efficiency is taken up in Chapters 9 and 16.

8.4 Medians and Percentiles

Why study the median asymptotically?

The sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ is one summary of central tendency; the sample median $\tilde{X}_n$ is another. The mean is sensitive to outliers; the median is not. Both should be in the toolkit. We have the CLT for $\bar{X}_n$. What is the analogous statement for $\tilde{X}_n$?

Arrange $X_1, \dots, X_n$ in increasing order: $X_{(1)} \leq X_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq X_{(n)}$. These are the order statistics. The sample median is the middle one (or the average of the two middle ones for even $n$).

Worked example — asymptotic normality of the sample median

Suppose $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with CDF $F$. Assume $F$ has a unique median $\theta$, so $F(\theta) = 1/2$, and that $F'(\theta)$ exists, is finite, and positive. Then we will show $$\sqrt{n}\,(\tilde{X}_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N\!\left( 0,\, \frac{1}{4\,[F'(\theta)]^2} \right).$$

The trick. The event $\{\tilde{X}_n \leq \theta + a/\sqrt{n}\}$ is the same as the event $\{S_n \geq m\}$, where $$S_n = \#\{ i \leq n : X_i \leq \theta + a/\sqrt{n}\}$$ counts how many observations fell below the threshold. Why? Because the sample median is the $m$-th order statistic; the median lies below a threshold $t$ iff at least $m$ observations lie below $t$.

Then $S_n \sim \text{Binomial}\big(n,\, p_n\big)$ with $p_n = F(\theta + a/\sqrt{n})$. A binomial is a sum of i.i.d. Bernoullis, so the CLT applies: $$\frac{S_n - n p_n}{\sqrt{n}} \approx N(0,\, p_n(1-p_n)).$$ Working through the algebra and using $F(\theta) = 1/2$, $F'(\theta) > 0$, and $\sqrt{F(\theta+a/\sqrt{n})(1 - F(\theta+a/\sqrt{n}))} \to 1/2$, $$P\big(\sqrt{n}(\tilde{X}_n - \theta) \leq a\big) \to \Phi(2 a F'(\theta)).$$ The right side is the CDF of $N(0,\, 1/(4 [F'(\theta)]^2))$ evaluated at $a$. Done.

The same argument generalizes to any quantile (Theorem 8.18): $$\sqrt{n}\,(\tilde\theta_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N\!\left( 0,\, \frac{\gamma(1-\gamma)}{[F'(\theta)]^2}\right),$$ where $\theta$ is the population $\gamma$-quantile and $\tilde\theta_n$ is the sample $\gamma$-quantile. The variance scales with $\gamma(1-\gamma)$ — largest in the middle (where the median sits), smaller in the tails.

8.5 Asymptotic Relative Efficiency

Comparing two estimators of the same thing

Suppose two estimator sequences $\hat\theta_n$ and $\tilde\theta_n$ both satisfy $$\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N(0, \sigma^2_{\hat\theta}), \qquad \sqrt{n}(\tilde\theta_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N(0, \sigma^2_{\tilde\theta}).$$ Define the asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE) of $\hat\theta$ with respect to $\tilde\theta$ as $$\operatorname{ARE} = \frac{\sigma^2_{\tilde\theta}}{\sigma^2_{\hat\theta}}.$$

The interpretation: ARE is the ratio of sample sizes needed for the two estimators to have comparable error distributions. If ARE $> 1$, $\hat\theta$ is more efficient (needs fewer samples). If ARE $< 1$, $\tilde\theta$ is more efficient.

Mean vs. median under a symmetric location family

Suppose the data are i.i.d. from a density $f(x - \theta)$ with $f$ symmetric about zero. Then both the mean and median estimate $\theta$. By the CLT and Theorem 8.18: $$\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N(0, \sigma^2), \qquad \sqrt{n}(\tilde{X}_n - \theta) \Rightarrow N\!\left(0, \frac{1}{4 f^2(0)}\right),$$ where $\sigma^2 = \int x^2 f(x)\, dx$. The ARE of mean to median is $$\operatorname{ARE} = \frac{1}{4 \sigma^2 f^2(0)}.$$

Normal data: $f(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$, so $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $1/(4 f^2(0)) = \pi/2$. The mean's variance is $1$, the median's is $\pi/2 \approx 1.57$. Mean wins, ARE $= \pi/2 \approx 1.57$ — roughly $57\%$ more observations are needed if you use the median instead of the mean. (Unsurprising: the mean is UMVU under normality.)

Laplace data: $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-|x|}$, so $\sigma^2 = 2$ and $1/(4 f^2(0)) = 1$. Now the median's variance is half the mean's, and the median is the MLE. ARE of mean to median is $1/2$. The median wins.

The lesson

No estimator dominates universally. The "right" estimator depends on the noise distribution. Normality favors means; heavy tails favor medians. ARE is the language we use to make this trade-off precise.

8.6 Scales of Magnitude

Big-O and little-o for constants

Asymptotic statements get long fast. Notation helps.

Definition 8.20 — orders of magnitude (deterministic)

For sequences $a_n, b_n$:

  • $a_n = o(b_n)$ means $a_n / b_n \to 0$ — "$a_n$ is of strictly smaller order than $b_n$".
  • $a_n = O(b_n)$ means $|a_n / b_n|$ is bounded — "$a_n$ is at most the order of $b_n$".
  • $a_n \sim b_n$ means $a_n / b_n \to 1$ — "$a_n$ is asymptotic to $b_n$".

As shorthand: $a_n = o(1)$ just means $a_n \to 0$, and $a_n = O(1)$ just means $a_n$ is bounded.

These symbols sit happily inside equations. For instance, the Taylor expansion $$f(x + \epsilon) = f(x) + \epsilon f'(x) + \tfrac{1}{2} \epsilon^2 f''(x) + o(\epsilon^2) \quad \text{as } \epsilon \to 0$$ says the remainder shrinks faster than $\epsilon^2$. If a third derivative exists, the remainder is actually $O(\epsilon^3)$ — a stronger claim.

Stochastic versions: $o_p$ and $O_p$

Definition 8.21 — orders of magnitude (stochastic)

For random variables $X_n$ and constants $b_n$:

  • $X_n = o_p(b_n)$ means $X_n / b_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$.
  • $X_n = O_p(1)$ means $\sup_n P(|X_n| > K) \to 0$ as $K \to \infty$ — the distributions of $X_n$ are tight.
  • $X_n = O_p(b_n)$ means $X_n / b_n = O_p(1)$.

Tightness ($O_p(1)$) is a probabilistic version of "bounded": for any $\epsilon$, all the mass of all the $X_n$ can be packed inside a single compact interval $[-K, K]$ except for at most $\epsilon$. Convergence in distribution implies tightness, so $X_n \Rightarrow X$ gives $X_n = O_p(1)$ for free.

The CLT then reads, very compactly: $$\bar{X}_n = \mu + O_p(1/\sqrt{n}).$$ This single equation packages the WLLN (the $O_p(1/\sqrt n)$ term vanishes, so $\bar{X}_n \to \mu$ in probability) and the CLT scaling (errors are $\sqrt n$-small, in a tightness sense) into one line.

These symbols obey a useful calculus: products multiply orders, $X_n = O_p(a_n)$ and $Y_n = O_p(b_n)$ give $X_n Y_n = O_p(a_n b_n)$. And if $f(\epsilon) = o(\epsilon^\alpha)$ and $X_n = O_p(a_n)$ with $a_n \to 0$, then $f(X_n) = o_p(a_n^\alpha)$. This is the cleanest route to a rigorous delta method proof.

8.7 Almost Sure Convergence

The third (and strongest) mode

The two earlier modes — in probability and in distribution — are both probabilistic statements about how often things happen. Almost sure convergence is a statement about each individual outcome.

Definition 8.25 — almost sure convergence

Random variables $Y_1, Y_2, \dots$ defined on a common probability space converge almost surely to $Y$ if $$P\big(\, Y_n \to Y \,\big) = 1.$$

Read that carefully. Inside the probability is the event "$Y_n \to Y$" — the set of outcomes $\omega$ such that the actual numerical sequence $Y_1(\omega), Y_2(\omega), \dots$ converges to $Y(\omega)$ in the ordinary sense. We are asking that this event have probability one.

How does this differ from convergence in probability?

Convergence in probability says: at each $n$, the probability that $Y_n$ misses $Y$ by $\epsilon$ is small. Almost sure convergence is stronger: with probability one, you can pick $N$ large enough that $|Y_n - Y| < \epsilon$ for every $n \geq N$. The "for every" is the key — it is a statement about tails of the whole sequence, not about one $n$ at a time.

Formally, set $M_n = \sup_{k \geq n} |Y_k - Y|$. Then $Y_n \to Y$ pointwise iff $M_n \to 0$. So $Y_n \to Y$ almost surely iff $M_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$. The technical content of almost-sure convergence is convergence in probability of the supremum from $n$ onward.

A worked contrast

Let $Y_n \sim \text{Bernoulli}(1/n)$. Then $P(Y_n = 1) = 1/n \to 0$, so $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} 0$ trivially. But almost sure convergence depends on the joint distribution. If the $Y_n$ are independent, the Borel–Cantelli lemma applies: $\sum P(Y_n = 1) = \sum 1/n = \infty$, so infinitely many $Y_n = 1$ happen with probability one. Almost sure convergence fails.

But if instead $Y_n = \mathbf{1}\{U \leq 1/n\}$ for a single uniform $U$, then once $1/n < U$ we have $Y_n = 0$ forever, and almost sure convergence holds.

Same marginals; different joints; different almost-sure behavior.

The strong law of large numbers

Theorem 8.27 — Strong Law of Large Numbers

If $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are i.i.d. with finite mean $\mu = E X_i$, then $$\bar{X}_n \to \mu \quad \text{almost surely as } n \to \infty.$$

The WLLN gave us $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$ in two lines using Chebyshev. The SLLN is stronger — and a great deal harder to prove. Conceptually, the WLLN says "for any fixed large $n$, $\bar{X}_n$ is probably near $\mu$"; the SLLN says "if you sit there and watch the sequence, you will see it converge in the ordinary sense, with probability one".

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 8

The chapter in eight bullets:

  1. Convergence in probability: $Y_n \xrightarrow{p} Y$ iff for every $\epsilon$, $P(|Y_n - Y| \geq \epsilon) \to 0$. Mean-square convergence implies it (Chebyshev).
  2. Weak law of large numbers: $\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{p} \mu$ when the $X_i$ are i.i.d. with finite mean. Consistency of estimators is just convergence in probability to the truth.
  3. Convergence in distribution: $Y_n \Rightarrow Y$ iff the CDFs converge at continuity points; equivalently, $E f(Y_n) \to E f(Y)$ for bounded continuous $f$.
  4. Central limit theorem: $\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \Rightarrow N(0, \sigma^2)$. The scaling rate is $\sqrt{n}$, the limit is universal.
  5. Slutsky and the delta method: the calculus for combining $\Rightarrow$ with $\xrightarrow{p}$. A smooth function $f$ of an average has $\sqrt{n}(f(\bar{X}_n) - f(\mu)) \Rightarrow N(0, [f'(\mu)]^2 \sigma^2)$.
  6. MLE and quantiles: MLEs in exponential families are smooth functions of an average and inherit asymptotic normality with variance equal to the inverse Fisher information. Sample quantiles are asymptotically normal with variance $\gamma(1-\gamma)/[F'(\theta)]^2$.
  7. Asymptotic relative efficiency: the ratio of asymptotic variances of two competing estimators. Means beat medians under normality; medians beat means under Laplace.
  8. Scales of magnitude: $o(\cdot)$, $O(\cdot)$, $o_p(\cdot)$, $O_p(\cdot)$ — the shorthand that compresses asymptotic statements. Almost sure convergence is the strongest mode: convergence pointwise on a probability-one set of outcomes.
