Chapter 11

Empirical Bayes and Shrinkage Estimators

The chapter where statistics gets weird: the obvious estimator is provably worse than a contraption that mixes your data with itself.

Book pages: 205–218 ~45 min reading time Prereqs: Bayes rules, normal distributions, decision theory basics

A startling claim

You measure $p$ independent things. The $i$-th measurement comes back as $X_i \sim N(\theta_i, 1)$, and you want to estimate each $\theta_i$. The natural answer is $\hat\theta_i = X_i$: it's unbiased, it's the MLE, it's what everyone does.

In 1961, James and Stein proved that when $p \geq 3$, this estimator is provably worse — under squared error loss summed across all coordinates — than an estimator that shrinks every $X_i$ toward zero by a data-dependent amount. Worse for every $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$, not just on average. The improvement isn't tiny either; near the origin it cuts the risk by a factor of roughly $p/2$.

This is Stein's paradox, and it's the central event of the chapter. We will set it up via empirical Bayes (a pragmatic compromise between fully Bayesian and fully frequentist thinking), prove the risk identity that makes the magic work, then back out into the broader framework of decision theory and ask what it means for an estimator to be "admissible".

Intuition — borrowing strength

Each $X_i$ tells you about $\theta_i$. But under the squared-error loss summed across all coordinates, the other $X_j$'s also tell you something — namely, how big $\theta$'s tend to be overall. The James–Stein estimator does the unintuitive thing of using $X_j$'s far from $X_i$ to improve the estimate of $\theta_i$. Statisticians call this borrowing strength. It feels wrong. It is correct.

11.1 Empirical Bayes Estimation

The setup

Suppose we measure $p$ objects with i.i.d. $N(0, 1)$ errors. Our data are $X_1, \dots, X_p$, independent, with $$X_i \sim N(\theta_i, 1), \qquad i = 1, \dots, p.$$ Write $X = (X_1, \dots, X_p)'$ and $\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_p)'$. We estimate $\theta_i$ by $\delta_i(X)$ and use compound squared error loss: $$L(\theta, \delta) = \sum_{i=1}^p \bigl( \theta_i - \delta_i(X) \bigr)^2 = \| \delta(X) - \theta \|^2.$$ The risk function is $R(\theta, \delta) = E_\theta \| \delta(X) - \theta \|^2$.

Pause — read this twice

Notice carefully: $\delta_i(X)$ is allowed to depend on all the $X_j$'s, not just $X_i$. That sounds strange — why should $X_2$ tell us anything about $\theta_1$? They're independent! Hold that question. The chapter will not just permit cross-dependence, it will require it for the magic to work.

The Bayesian formulation

Pretend, for a moment, that $\theta$ is itself random. Let $\Theta_1, \dots, \Theta_p$ be i.i.d. $N(0, \tau^2)$ a priori, and given $\Theta = \theta$, let $X_1, \dots, X_p$ be independent with $X_i \sim N(\theta_i, 1)$. Then the joint density of $(X, \Theta)$ is $$\frac{1}{(2\pi\tau)^p} \exp\!\left[ -\tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^p (x_i - \theta_i)^2 - \frac{1}{2\tau^2} \sum_{i=1}^p \theta_i^2 \right].$$

The trick now is the same one you've seen a dozen times in Bayesian calculations: complete the square in $\theta_i$. A direct computation gives $$(x_i - \theta_i)^2 + \frac{\theta_i^2}{\tau^2} = \left( 1 + \frac{1}{\tau^2} \right) \left( \theta_i - \frac{x_i}{1 + 1/\tau^2} \right)^2 + \frac{x_i^2}{1 + \tau^2}.$$

Integrating out $\theta$ leaves the marginal density of $X$: a product of $N(0, 1 + \tau^2)$ densities. So in the Bayesian model, marginally, $X_1, \dots, X_p$ are i.i.d.\ $N(0, 1 + \tau^2)$.

Dividing the joint by the marginal gives the posterior: the $\Theta_i$ are conditionally independent given $X$, with $$\Theta_i \,\big|\, X = x \;\sim\; N\!\left( \frac{x_i}{1 + 1/\tau^2}, \; \frac{\tau^2}{1 + \tau^2} \right).$$

So $E[\Theta_i \mid X] = X_i / (1 + 1/\tau^2)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\Theta_i \mid X) = \tau^2 / (1 + \tau^2)$. The Bayes estimator under squared error loss is the posterior mean, which we now slow down to inspect.

The Bayes shrinkage formula

Under the $N(0, \tau^2)$ prior, the Bayes estimator of $\theta_i$ under squared error loss is $$\delta_i(X) = \frac{X_i}{1 + 1/\tau^2} = \left( 1 - \frac{1}{1 + \tau^2} \right) X_i. \tag{11.1}$$

Reading the formula

The Bayes estimator is $X_i$ scaled by a factor strictly between $0$ and $1$ — it shrinks $X_i$ toward zero, which is the prior mean. How aggressively?

  • If $\tau^2$ is large (prior is vague, $\theta$'s could be anywhere), the factor $1 - 1/(1+\tau^2) \approx 1$: barely any shrinkage. Trust the data.
  • If $\tau^2$ is small (prior tightly concentrated at $0$, $\theta$'s are believed small), the factor is close to $0$: shrink hard. Trust the prior.

The shrinkage factor is the answer to the question "how much should I trust this datum?". It compares the data's noise variance (here, $1$) to the prior variance ($\tau^2$). When noise dominates prior, shrink. When prior is vague, don't.

The empirical step

The Bayes estimator (11.1) requires knowing $\tau$. In real life, you don't. The empirical Bayes trick is: estimate $\tau$ from the data and plug it in.

Under the Bayesian model we just derived, $X_1, \dots, X_p$ are marginally i.i.d.\ $N(0, 1 + \tau^2)$. So $\sum_i X_i^2 = \|X\|^2$ has expectation $p(1 + \tau^2)$, and $\|X\|^2 / p$ is an unbiased estimator of $1 + \tau^2$.

What we actually need in (11.1) is the shrinkage factor $1/(1 + \tau^2)$. We could plug in $p / \|X\|^2$, but a slightly different multiple turns out to behave much better. James and Stein use $$\widehat{\frac{1}{1 + \tau^2}} = \frac{p - 2}{\|X\|^2}.$$ The reason for $p - 2$ rather than $p$ will become apparent in the next section, where it makes a key identity vanish exactly. For now, take it on faith.

The James–Stein estimator

Plugging the empirical estimate of $1/(1 + \tau^2)$ into the Bayes formula (11.1): $$\delta^{\text{JS}}(X) = \left( 1 - \frac{p - 2}{\|X\|^2} \right) X. \tag{11.2}$$ Each coordinate $X_i$ is shrunk toward zero by the same factor $1 - (p-2)/\|X\|^2$.

Pause — see what just happened

The estimator $\delta^{\text{JS}}_i(X)$ depends on every $X_j$, because $\|X\|^2 = X_1^2 + \cdots + X_p^2$. Each component pulls all the others' shrinkage levels toward itself. This is the cross-dependence we promised. The "borrowing of strength" happens through the shared shrinkage factor.

Worked example — baseball batting averages

The most famous real-world demonstration is Efron and Morris's 1975 analysis of major-league batting averages. Eighteen players had their first 45 at-bats from the 1970 season recorded; their batting averages $X_i$ are approximately normal estimates of each player's true long-run average $\theta_i$. The MLE is "use $X_i$": just believe each player's small sample.

After standardizing (variance-stabilizing transform), one gets exactly the $N(\theta_i, 1)$ setup. Compute $\|X\|^2$ across all 18 players, shrink each $X_i$ toward the grand mean by the factor $1 - (p - 3)/\|X - \bar X\|^2$ (the version that shrinks to the sample mean rather than zero), and predict each player's batting average for the rest of the season. The James–Stein predictions had total squared error roughly $3.5$ times smaller than the MLE predictions. The shrunken estimate for the best hitter (Clemente) was substantially worse than the MLE for him alone — but the gains on the mediocre hitters more than paid for it across the pool.

Common pitfall — shrinking the wrong direction

The formula $(1 - (p-2)/\|X\|^2) X$ shrinks toward zero. That works because we set up the prior with mean zero. If your $\theta_i$'s are believed to cluster around some other value (say a grand mean $\nu$), the correct estimator subtracts $\nu$, shrinks, and adds back: $\delta^{\text{JS}}_i = \nu + (1 - (p-3)/\|X-\nu\|^2)(X_i - \nu)$.

If you shrink toward the wrong target — say, toward zero when the truth is centered at $100$ — you can do catastrophically worse than the MLE. The shrinkage is only safe relative to a sensible center.

Why the word "empirical"

The derivation has a Bayesian flavour, but $\tau$ is not modelled as a random variable; we just estimate it from data. Statisticians call $\tau$ a hyperparameter. A fully Bayesian treatment would put a prior on $\tau$ as well — leading to a hierarchical Bayes model. Empirical Bayes is the pragmatic shortcut: estimate the hyperparameter from data and pretend you knew it. Keener returns to hierarchical models in Section 15.1.

11.2 Risk of the James–Stein Estimator

We claimed $\delta^{\text{JS}}$ dominates $X$ in compound risk when $p \geq 3$. Now we prove it. The proof has a beautiful structure: Stein's identity gives us an unbiased estimator of the risk, and for $\delta^{\text{JS}}$ that estimator is manifestly less than $p$.

Stein's identity in one dimension

Lemma 11.1 — Stein's identity

Let $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, let $h : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable (absolutely continuous is enough), and suppose $E|h'(X)| < \infty$. Then $$E[(X - \mu) h(X)] = \sigma^2 \, E[h'(X)].$$

Reading the lemma

Multiplying by $(X - \mu)$ inside an expectation is the same as differentiating $h$ and multiplying by $\sigma^2$. It's an "integration by parts" for the normal distribution, and it converts cross-terms involving $(X - \mu)$ — which we usually have to expand and grind through — into derivatives, which we can often compute symbolically.

The proof works for $N(0,1)$ first via Fubini: write $h(x) = \int_0^x h'(y)\,dy$, swap the order of integration in $\int x h(x) e^{-x^2/2} dx$, and use $\int_y^\infty x e^{-x^2/2} dx = e^{-y^2/2}$. The general $(\mu, \sigma^2)$ case follows by standardizing $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$.

The multivariate version

Now let $X_1, \dots, X_p$ be independent with $X_i \sim N(\theta_i, 1)$, and let $h : \mathbb{R}^p \to \mathbb{R}^p$. Write $Dh(x)$ for the $p \times p$ matrix of partial derivatives, $[Dh(x)]_{ij} = \partial h_i(x)/\partial x_j$, and $\operatorname{tr}\{Dh(x)\} = \sum_i \partial h_i / \partial x_i$ for its trace.

Lemma 11.2 — multivariate Stein

Under integrability, $E \, \|Dh(X)\| < \infty$, $$E\bigl[ (X - \theta)' h(X) \bigr] = E \operatorname{tr}\{Dh(X)\}.$$

The proof is one application of the scalar Stein identity per coordinate, then summing. The key insight is that conditioning on all coordinates except $X_i$ reduces the $i$-th term to a one-dimensional problem in $X_i$, which is exactly what Lemma 11.1 handles.

An unbiased estimator of the risk

Now the punchline. Given an estimator $\delta(X)$ of $\theta$, write $\delta(X) = X - h(X)$ — so $h(X)$ is the "shrinkage" that $\delta$ applies. Then $$\| \delta(X) - \theta \|^2 = \| X - \theta - h(X) \|^2 = \|X - \theta\|^2 + \|h(X)\|^2 - 2 (X - \theta)' h(X).$$ Take expectations. The first term is $p$ (sum of $p$ unit variances). The third term is, by Lemma 11.2, $2 E \operatorname{tr}\{Dh(X)\}$. So:

Theorem 11.3 — unbiased risk estimator

Suppose $X_i \sim N(\theta_i, 1)$ independently and $\delta(X) = X - h(X)$ with $h$ differentiable. Define $$\hat R = p + \|h(X)\|^2 - 2 \operatorname{tr}\{Dh(X)\}.$$ Then $R(\theta, \delta) = E_\theta \, \hat R$, provided $E_\theta \, \|Dh(X)\| < \infty$.

Pause — what makes this remarkable

$\hat R$ depends only on $X$, not on $\theta$. Given any candidate estimator, we can compute (in principle) a function of the data whose expectation equals the risk — without knowing the truth. That's already a useful tool: we can compare two estimators by comparing the realized values of their $\hat R$'s, on any data set.

Applying it to James–Stein

For the James–Stein estimator (11.2), $h(X) = \dfrac{p-2}{\|X\|^2} X$. Componentwise, $h_i(x) = (p-2) x_i / \|x\|^2$.

We need $\operatorname{tr}\{Dh(x)\}$. By the quotient rule, $$\frac{\partial h_i(x)}{\partial x_i} = \frac{p-2}{\|x\|^2} - \frac{2(p-2) x_i^2}{\|x\|^4}.$$ Summing over $i$: $$\operatorname{tr}\{Dh(x)\} = \frac{p(p-2)}{\|x\|^2} - \frac{2(p-2) \sum_i x_i^2}{\|x\|^4} = \frac{p(p-2) - 2(p-2)}{\|x\|^2} = \frac{(p-2)^2}{\|x\|^2}.$$

And $\|h(x)\|^2 = \sum_i (p-2)^2 x_i^2 / \|x\|^4 = (p-2)^2 / \|x\|^2$.

Plug into Theorem 11.3:

$$\hat R = p + \frac{(p-2)^2}{\|X\|^2} - 2 \cdot \frac{(p-2)^2}{\|X\|^2} = p - \frac{(p-2)^2}{\|X\|^2}. \tag{11.5}$$
Why $p - 2$ was the magic constant

The choice $c = p - 2$ in the shrinkage factor $c / \|X\|^2$ is the one that lets $\|h\|^2$ and $2 \operatorname{tr}\{Dh\}$ combine into a single negative term. Any other constant $c$ gives $\hat R = p + c^2/\|X\|^2 - 2c(p-2)/\|X\|^2$, whose minimum over $c$ is at $c = p - 2$. James and Stein chose the optimum.

The domination theorem

Since $(p-2)^2/\|X\|^2 \geq 0$ for $p \geq 2$, equation (11.5) gives $$R(\theta, \delta^{\text{JS}}) = E_\theta \hat R = E_\theta\!\left[ p - \frac{(p-2)^2}{\|X\|^2} \right] < p = R(\theta, X)$$ whenever $p \geq 3$ (so that the subtracted term has strictly positive expectation — note $1/\|X\|^2$ has infinite expectation at $\theta = 0$ when $p \leq 2$, which is part of why the result fails there).

The headline

When $p \geq 3$, the James–Stein estimator has strictly smaller compound risk than $X$ for every value of $\theta$. Not on average over some prior; not in some asymptotic limit; every $\theta$. In the language of decision theory (next section), $X$ is inadmissible: it is dominated.

How much improvement, and where?

When $\|\theta\|$ is large, $\|X\|$ is large with high probability, the shrinkage factor is near $1$, and $\delta^{\text{JS}} \approx X$ with similar risk. The gain is concentrated near the origin. At $\theta = 0$, $\|X\|^2 \sim \chi^2_p$, and from $E_0[1/\|X\|^2] = 1/(p-2)$ (a chi-square computation, as in (4.10)), $$R(0, \delta^{\text{JS}}) = p - \frac{(p-2)^2}{p-2} = p - (p-2) = 2.$$

The two — independent of $p$

At the origin, no matter how many dimensions you have, the James–Stein estimator has risk exactly $2$. The MLE has risk $p$ there. So for $p = 100$, the improvement is a factor of $50$. The James–Stein estimator essentially says "estimate the $\theta$'s by their average — wait, they're all zero — so estimate them all by zero". When that's roughly right, it's vastly better than the MLE; when it's wrong, the guarantee says you're still no worse.

Common pitfall — admissibility doesn't mean "best"

$\delta^{\text{JS}}$ dominates $X$, but $\delta^{\text{JS}}$ is itself dominated! The "positive part" version, $\delta^{\text{JS+}} = \max(0, 1 - (p-2)/\|X\|^2) X$, has uniformly smaller risk than $\delta^{\text{JS}}$ (it forbids the shrinkage factor from going negative, which can happen when $\|X\|^2 < p - 2$). So $\delta^{\text{JS}}$ is also inadmissible. Even $\delta^{\text{JS+}}$ is inadmissible. Finding admissible estimators in high dimensions is genuinely hard.

11.3 Decision Theory

The James–Stein result raised the natural question: is $X$ also inadmissible in $p = 1$ or $p = 2$? The answer turns out to be no — $X$ is admissible in those dimensions. To make sense of these claims we need a small dose of decision theory.

The vocabulary

Formal decision theory has five ingredients:

  • A parameter space $\Omega$ (the unknown $\theta$ lives here).
  • An action space $A$ (the things you can decide to do).
  • A data space $\mathcal{X}$ (here, $\mathbb{R}^n$).
  • A model $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\}$.
  • A loss function $L : \Omega \times A \to [-\infty, \infty]$, taken non-negative and measurable.

A nonrandomized decision rule is a measurable function $\delta : \mathcal{X} \to A$. A randomized rule is a stochastic kernel $\delta_x$: observing $X = x$, draw an action from the probability distribution $\delta_x$. The set of all rules is $\mathcal{D}$.

The risk function of $\delta$ is $$R(\theta, \delta) = E_\theta L(\theta, \delta(X)) = \int L(\theta, \delta(x)) \, dP_\theta(x).$$

Example 11.4 — estimation

To estimate $g(\theta)$, take $A = \mathbb{R}$ and call $\delta$ an estimator. Standard losses are squared error $L(\theta, a) = (a - g(\theta))^2$ and weighted squared error $L(\theta, a) = w(\theta)(a - g(\theta))^2$.

Example 11.5 — testing

For testing $H_0: \theta \in \Omega_0$ vs.\ $H_1: \theta \in \Omega_1$, take $A = \{0, 1\}$. A randomized rule is determined by the success probability $\phi(x) = \delta_x(\{1\})$ — the test function. Under zero-one loss, the risk is the power function on $\Omega_1$ and one minus the power on $\Omega_0$ (i.e., the type-I error rate on the null).

Admissibility

Definition — admissibility

A rule $\delta^*$ dominates $\delta$ if $R(\theta, \delta^*) \leq R(\theta, \delta)$ for all $\theta \in \Omega$ with strict inequality for some $\theta$. $\delta$ is inadmissible if some $\delta^*$ dominates it; otherwise $\delta$ is admissible.

Reading the definition

Admissibility is a minimal sanity check, not a recommendation. An admissible rule simply has the property that no competitor is uniformly at least as good and somewhere strictly better. That leaves a lot of room. For instance, "always estimate $\theta = 17$" is admissible — no other rule beats it at $\theta = 17$. Bad, but admissible. Conversely, inadmissibility is damning: someone offered you a strictly better procedure for free.

Bayes rules and integrated risk

Given a prior $\Lambda$ on $\Omega$, the integrated risk is $$R(\Lambda, \delta) = \int R(\theta, \delta) \, d\Lambda(\theta), \tag{11.6}$$ and the Bayes risk is the infimum, $R(\Lambda) = \inf_{\delta \in \mathcal{D}} R(\Lambda, \delta)$. $\delta$ is Bayes for $\Lambda$ if it achieves this infimum: $R(\Lambda, \delta) = R(\Lambda)$.

An important technical note: in (11.6) and the Bayes risk, $\Lambda$ doesn't actually need to be a probability measure — finiteness or even just $\sigma$-finiteness is enough. We call $\delta$ proper Bayes if it is Bayes for some probability prior; rules that are Bayes only for "improper" priors (with $\Lambda(\Omega) = \infty$) are excluded from this designation.

Why Bayes rules tend to be admissible

Theorem 11.6

If $\delta$ is the essentially unique Bayes rule for $\Lambda$, then $\delta$ is admissible.

The proof is one line: if $\delta^*$ dominated $\delta$, then $\delta^*$ would also be Bayes (because it integrates to at most the same value), and by uniqueness $\delta = \delta^*$ a.e., so their risks would coincide. Contradiction.

Theorem 11.7

If risk functions are continuous in $\theta$, $\delta$ is Bayes for $\Lambda$ with finite integrated risk, and $\Lambda$ has support equal to all of $\Omega$, then $\delta$ is admissible.

The support condition is doing real work: it says the prior $\Lambda$ doesn't ignore any open subset of $\Omega$. Combined with continuity of risk functions, this forces any dominating $\delta^*$ to agree with $\delta$ everywhere.

Complete classes

A collection of decision rules is a complete class if every rule outside the class is inadmissible. So a complete class contains all admissible rules. A general theorem (Theorem 11.8, due to Brown) says that under regularity (dominated family, strictly convex loss, loss diverging to $\infty$), the pointwise limits of Bayes rules with finitely supported priors form a complete class. Translation: in regular problems, every admissible rule is a limit of Bayes rules.

A reframing

This is one of the most philosophically loaded results in statistics. It says: if you accept squared error loss (or any nice convex loss) and you want to use an admissible procedure, you're committed to using something that looks Bayesian — for some prior, possibly improper. Frequentist and Bayesian methods are less separate than they appear.

A sufficient condition for admissibility

Theorem 11.9 (sketch)

Assume risk functions are continuous in $\theta$. If for every closed ball $\overline{B_r(x)}$ there exist finite measures $\Lambda_m$ with $R(\Lambda_m, \delta) < \infty$, $\liminf \Lambda_m\bigl(\overline{B_r(x)}\bigr) > 0$, and $R(\delta, \Lambda_m) - R(\Lambda_m) \to 0$, then $\delta$ is admissible.

The idea: $\delta$ is "almost Bayes" for a sequence of priors that don't degenerate locally. If a competing $\delta^*$ dominated $\delta$, the gap would integrate against $\Lambda_m$ to a positive lower bound (using the local non-degeneracy), contradicting the assumption that $\delta$ achieves the Bayes risk asymptotically.

Example 11.10 — $\bar X$ is admissible in $p = 1$

Consider $X_1, \dots, X_n$ i.i.d.\ $N(\theta, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ known, and the prior $\Theta \sim N(0, \tau^2)$. The Bayes estimator is the shrunken mean $\bar X / (1 + \sigma^2 / (n \tau^2))$ with integrated risk $\sigma^2 \tau^2 / (\sigma^2 + n \tau^2)$. As $\tau \to \infty$, this Bayes rule converges to $\bar X$.

Naive choice: $\Lambda_m = N(0, m)$. But this has densities tending to zero, so $\Lambda_m(\overline{B_r(x)}) \to 0$. Fix: rescale, $\Lambda_m = \sqrt{m} \cdot N(0, m)$. Its density is $\varphi(\theta/\sqrt{m})$, which tends to $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$, and by dominated convergence $\Lambda_m(\overline{B_r(x)}) \to 2r/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

Scaling the prior by $\sqrt{m}$ scales everything by $\sqrt{m}$, so $R(\bar X, \Lambda_m) = \sqrt{m} \sigma^2 / n$ and $R(\Lambda_m) = \sqrt{m} \sigma^2 m / (\sigma^2 + nm)$, and $$R(\bar X, \Lambda_m) - R(\Lambda_m) = \frac{\sqrt{m} \sigma^4}{n(\sigma^2 + nm)} \to 0.$$ Theorem 11.9 fires: $\bar X$ is admissible. A delicate version of this argument works in $p = 2$ as well — but conjugate normals will not work, and the priors must be chosen with great care. Past $p = 2$, no rescue is possible: this is exactly what Stein's $p \geq 3$ paradox is telling us.

The big arc

Putting the chapter together: empirical Bayes provides a recipe (shrink toward the prior mean by an amount estimated from the data). For normal means, the recipe yields the James–Stein estimator. Stein's identity provides an unbiased risk estimator. The risk estimator shows James–Stein beats the MLE for $p \geq 3$. Decision theory tells us $\bar X$ is admissible for $p \leq 2$, so this $p \geq 3$ vs.\ $p \leq 2$ split is a genuine dimensional phenomenon, not a quirk of the prior.

Summary

The whole chapter, in seven bullets:

  1. Compound loss is the sum of squared errors across many parameters. Asking about it (rather than about one parameter at a time) is what unlocks shrinkage.
  2. Under a $N(0, \tau^2)$ prior on each $\theta_i$, the Bayes estimator is $\delta_i(X) = X_i / (1 + 1/\tau^2)$ — shrink toward the prior mean, with strength controlled by $\tau$.
  3. Empirical Bayes: estimate the hyperparameter $\tau$ from the data and plug in. For normal means, this yields the James–Stein estimator $\delta^{\text{JS}} = (1 - (p-2)/\|X\|^2) X$.
  4. Stein's identity: $E[(X - \mu) h(X)] = \sigma^2 E[h'(X)]$. The multivariate version converts cross-terms in risk computations into traces of Jacobians.
  5. Using Stein's identity, the unbiased risk estimator $\hat R = p + \|h\|^2 - 2 \operatorname{tr}\{Dh\}$ proves $R(\theta, \delta^{\text{JS}}) < p$ for all $\theta$ when $p \geq 3$. The MLE $X$ is inadmissible.
  6. Decision theory formalizes the comparison via parameter / action / data spaces, loss, risk. Admissibility means no competitor dominates you. Bayes rules are usually admissible; conversely, every admissible rule is essentially a limit of Bayes rules.
  7. $\bar X$ is admissible in $p = 1, 2$ (use Theorem 11.9 with rescaled normal priors) but inadmissible in $p \geq 3$. The transition is sharp and the cause is genuinely the geometry of high-dimensional space.
