Risk, Sufficiency, Completeness, and Ancillarity

How statisticians compare estimators, and how they spot the parts of a dataset that actually carry information about the unknown.

Book pages: 39–59 ~50 min reading time Prereqs: Chapters 1–2

3.1 Models, estimators, and the risk function

What is a statistical model, really?

Inferential statistics is, at heart, the science of guessing something we cannot see — an unknown parameter $\theta$ — from something we can see — data $X$. The link between the two is a statistical model: a family of candidate distributions $$\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\},$$ one $P_\theta$ for each possible value of the unknown $\theta$. The set $\Omega$ is called the parameter space; it might be a finite set (does the coin favor heads or tails?), a single interval (a success probability in $[0,1]$), or a subset of $\mathbb{R}^p$ (a regression coefficient vector). We write $X \sim P_\theta$ to mean "the data $X$ was drawn from the distribution indexed by $\theta$".

The slogan is: nature picks one $\theta$ in $\Omega$, draws $X$ from $P_\theta$, hands us $X$, and hides $\theta$. Inference is the reverse trip — using $X$ to say something about $\theta$.

Definition — statistic

A statistic is any function $\delta(X)$ of the data alone. It may not depend on $\theta$ — only on what we actually observed. When $\delta$ is meant to approximate some quantity $g(\theta)$, we call $\delta(X)$ an estimator of $g(\theta)$.

The qualifier "of the data alone" is important. A formula like $\bar X - \theta$ is not a statistic, because we cannot compute it without knowing the very $\theta$ we are trying to estimate. A formula like $\bar X = (X_1 + \cdots + X_n)/n$ is a statistic — it uses only the numbers in front of us.

Loss functions: what does a bad estimate cost?

To compare estimators, we need to agree on what "bad" means. The decision-theoretic answer is a loss function $L(\theta, d)$: the price we pay if we report the value $d$ when the truth is $\theta$. Two natural ground rules:

  • $L(\theta, g(\theta)) = 0$ — getting the answer exactly right costs nothing.
  • $L(\theta, d) \geq 0$ — losses are never negative.

The single most common choice is squared-error loss, $$L(\theta, d) = \big(g(\theta) - d\big)^2,$$ which penalizes large errors much more than small ones. Absolute-error loss $|g(\theta) - d|$ and $0/1$-loss $1\{g(\theta) \neq d\}$ are also standard. The choice of loss reflects what you actually care about — not a universal truth.

From loss to risk

The trouble is that $L(\theta, \delta(X))$ is itself random: even a fantastic estimator will sometimes get unlucky data. To average over that randomness we define the risk function $$R(\theta, \delta) = E_\theta L(\theta, \delta(X)),$$ where $E_\theta$ means "expectation when $X \sim P_\theta$". For each candidate $\theta$, the risk is one number: the expected loss we pay using $\delta$ if nature's choice is $\theta$. As $\theta$ varies over $\Omega$, the risk traces out a function. To compare two estimators, we compare their risk functions.

Worked example — coin on edge (Example 3.1)

Spin a coin on its edge $n = 100$ times. Let $X$ count heads. Then $X \sim \text{Binomial}(100, \theta)$, and the model is $\mathcal{P} = \{\text{Binomial}(100, \theta) : \theta \in [0, 1]\}$. A natural estimator of $\theta$ is the sample proportion $\delta(X) = X/100$. Under squared-error loss, $$R(\theta, \delta) = E_\theta\!\left[\left(\theta - \frac{X}{100}\right)^2\right] = \operatorname{Var}_\theta\!\left(\frac{X}{100}\right) = \frac{\theta(1 - \theta)}{100}.$$ This is largest at $\theta = 1/2$ (where the binomial has the most variance) and smallest near the boundary values $0$ and $1$. The risk function is an inverted parabola.

Why a function and not a number?

You might wish for a single "the risk of $\delta$" number, but that would require averaging over $\theta$ — which means putting a prior distribution on $\theta$. That is the Bayesian road, and Keener takes it later. Frequentist inference keeps the risk as a function of $\theta$ and asks: can I find a $\delta$ whose risk curve sits below every competitor's, for every $\theta$? Often the answer is no, and that's the central difficulty of estimation.

Pause — when risk curves cross

Consider three competing estimators for the binomial $\theta$: $\delta_0(X) = X/100$, $\delta_1(X) = (X+3)/100$, and $\delta_2(X) = (X+3)/106$ (a shrunken version). Plotting their risks, $\delta_2$ wins when $\theta$ is near $1/2$, but $\delta_0$ wins near $0$ and $1$. Neither estimator dominates the other across all $\theta$. If we knew $\theta$ we could pick — but if we knew $\theta$ we would not need to estimate it. This is the whole problem of statistics in one paragraph.

Admissibility, in one breath

An estimator $\delta$ is inadmissible if there is a competing $\tilde\delta$ with $R(\theta, \tilde\delta) \leq R(\theta, \delta)$ for every $\theta$, and strict inequality for some $\theta$. Inadmissibility is a death sentence: you'd be silly to use a procedure that another procedure beats everywhere. If no competitor improves on $\delta$, then $\delta$ is admissible. Admissibility is a weak requirement — many obviously poor estimators are admissible — but inadmissibility is a strong condemnation.

3.2 Sufficient statistics

The motivating thought

Suppose your data is a long list of numbers $X = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)$, but you suspect a single function $T(X)$ already contains every drop of information about $\theta$ that the full sample carries. Can we make that precise? Keener opens this section with an arresting calculation. Two i.i.d. exponential variates $X, Y$ are reshuffled into $\tilde X = UT$ and $\tilde Y = (1-U)T$, where $T = X + Y$ and $U$ is an independent uniform on $(0,1)$. The punchline: $(\tilde X, \tilde Y)$ has exactly the same joint distribution as $(X, Y)$. So if you only know $T$, you can manufacture fake data $(\tilde X, \tilde Y)$ — using a random number generator that does not depend on $\theta$ — that is statistically indistinguishable from the real data.

The conclusion is striking: as far as learning about $\theta$ goes, the single number $T = X + Y$ is just as informative as the pair $(X, Y)$. The dimension dropped from two to one with no loss. We call $T$ a sufficient statistic.

Definition 3.2 — sufficient statistic

Let $X \sim P_\theta$ with $\theta \in \Omega$. A statistic $T = T(X)$ is sufficient for $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta\}$ if, for every $t$, the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T = t$ does not depend on $\theta$.

Why this is the right definition

Read it as: once you have told me $T$, the leftover detail in $X$ is pure noise — the $\theta$-information has already been wrung out. Concretely, if I know $T = t$, I can simulate a fresh dataset from the $\theta$-free conditional distribution $Q_t$, and that simulated dataset has the same marginal distribution as the real $X$. The data and the simulation are statistically interchangeable. Sufficiency says: everything $\theta$-relevant in $X$ is already encoded in $T$.

Notation gloss: $Q_t(B) = P_\theta(X \in B \mid T = t)$ is the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T = t$. The crucial property is that this object — despite appearing to depend on $\theta$ through the symbol $P_\theta$ — actually does not. It is the same kernel $Q_t$ regardless of which $\theta$ generated the data.

Worked example — Bernoulli trials

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. Bernoulli$(\theta)$ and let $T = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$. Given $T = t$, every sequence of $0$'s and $1$'s with exactly $t$ ones is equally likely — the conditional distribution is uniform over the $\binom{n}{t}$ such sequences, with no $\theta$ in sight. So $T$ is sufficient. Knowing the order of the heads and tails gives you nothing extra; only the total count carries information about $\theta$.

The randomization theorem

Sufficiency would be a mere curiosity if it had no operational payoff. Here is the payoff: given any estimator $\delta(X)$, there is a randomized estimator based only on $T$ with the same risk function. The construction: when $T = t$, draw a fake $\tilde X$ from $Q_t$ using a random number generator, and report $\delta(\tilde X)$. Because $\tilde X$ has the same distribution as $X$ under every $P_\theta$, the risk is unchanged. So whatever you can do with $X$, you can match using just $T$ (plus an independent dice roll).

Pause — randomized vs. non-randomized

The argument so far only promises a randomized estimator from $T$. In Section 3.7 we will see that when the loss is convex, we can do strictly better: a non-randomized estimator based on $T$ beats any randomized one. So sufficient statistics will turn out to be the only estimators worth considering — not merely as good as any other, but actually superior.

3.3 The Factorization Theorem

From definition to a usable check

Definition 3.2 is elegant but inconvenient: to verify sufficiency we'd have to compute conditional distributions, which is a chore. The Factorization Theorem (sometimes called Fisher–Neyman) turns sufficiency into a one-line check on densities.

Definition 3.5 — dominated family

A family $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta\}$ is dominated if there exists a single $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu$ such that $P_\theta \ll \mu$ for every $\theta$. Each $P_\theta$ then has a density $p_\theta = dP_\theta/d\mu$.

"Dominated" is mild. For continuous models we usually take $\mu$ to be Lebesgue measure; for discrete models, counting measure. Mixed and exotic families need slightly more care but the assumption holds in essentially every situation you'll meet.

Theorem 3.6 — Factorization Theorem

Let $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\}$ be dominated by $\mu$, with densities $p_\theta$. A statistic $T$ is sufficient if and only if there exist non-negative functions $g_\theta$ and $h$ such that $$p_\theta(x) = g_\theta\big( T(x) \big) \, h(x) \quad \text{for $\mu$-a.e. } x.$$

How to read the formula

Two ingredients. The factor $h(x)$ depends only on the data — not on $\theta$ — and so contributes nothing to any comparison between candidate $\theta$'s. The factor $g_\theta(T(x))$ depends on $\theta$ but reaches the data $x$ only through the summary $T(x)$. Cancelling shared $h(x)$'s in any likelihood ratio $p_\theta(x)/p_{\theta'}(x)$, you find the ratio depends on $x$ only through $T(x)$. So $T$ contains all the $\theta$-discriminating information.

Worked example — Bernoulli, factored

For i.i.d. Bernoulli$(\theta)$ data the joint mass function is $$p_\theta(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n \theta^{x_i}(1-\theta)^{1-x_i} = \theta^{\sum x_i} (1-\theta)^{n-\sum x_i}.$$ Set $T(x) = \sum x_i$, $g_\theta(t) = \theta^t (1-\theta)^{n-t}$, and $h(x) = 1$. The factorization is immediate, so $T$ is sufficient — recovering by formula what we argued by symmetry above.

Worked example — Normal one-sample

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\theta = (\mu, \sigma^2)$ both unknown. The joint density is $$p_\theta(x) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2} \exp\!\left[ -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 \right] = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-n/2} \exp\!\left[ \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}\sum x_i - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum x_i^2 - \frac{n\mu^2}{2\sigma^2}\right].$$ Setting $T(x) = \big(\sum x_i, \, \sum x_i^2\big)$, we can pull the entire $\theta$-dependence into a function of $T(x)$, with $h(x) \equiv 1$. So the pair $(\sum X_i, \sum X_i^2)$ is sufficient — equivalently, $(\bar X, S^2)$ is sufficient.

Worked example — Poisson

For i.i.d. Poisson$(\lambda)$ data, $$p_\lambda(x) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{\lambda^{x_i} e^{-\lambda}}{x_i!} = \lambda^{\sum x_i} e^{-n\lambda} \cdot \frac{1}{\prod x_i!}.$$ With $T(x) = \sum x_i$, $g_\lambda(t) = \lambda^t e^{-n\lambda}$, and $h(x) = 1/\prod x_i!$, factorization gives $T$ sufficient.

Worked example — uniform with shifting support (Example 3.8)

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. uniform on $(\theta, \theta + 1)$. The marginal density $f_\theta = 1_{(\theta, \theta+1)}$ forces us to keep track of where the data live. The joint density equals one exactly when every $x_i$ falls in the interval, i.e. when $\min_i x_i > \theta$ and $\max_i x_i < \theta + 1$. So $$p_\theta(x) = 1_{(\theta, \infty)}\!\big(\min_i x_i\big) \cdot 1_{(-\infty, \theta + 1)}\!\big(\max_i x_i\big).$$ Both factors depend on $\theta$ and on data only through $T = (\min_i X_i, \max_i X_i)$. So this two-dimensional statistic is sufficient — even though $\theta$ is only one-dimensional.

Common pitfall — indicator handling

When the support of $p_\theta$ depends on $\theta$, you must keep the indicator functions explicit and check the factorization works for every $x$, not just $x$ in the support. Sloppy bookkeeping of indicators is the most frequent mistake in homework-set sufficiency problems.

3.4 Minimal sufficiency

Why "minimal"?

Sufficient statistics are not unique. If $T$ is sufficient and we apply any one-to-one function $f$ to it, $\tilde T = f(T)$ is also sufficient. Worse, padding works: the entire data vector $X$ is trivially sufficient. So is $(X, U)$ for any independent random number $U$. We want the most compressed sufficient statistic — the one that throws away every drop of nuisance information while keeping all the $\theta$-information.

Definition 3.9 — minimal sufficient

A statistic $T$ is minimal sufficient if it is sufficient and, for every other sufficient statistic $\tilde T$, there is a function $f$ with $T = f(\tilde T)$ almost surely under every $P \in \mathcal{P}$.

Translation

$T$ is minimal sufficient if it is a function of every other sufficient statistic — meaning every other sufficient statistic must "know" at least as much as $T$. Geometrically, the family of sufficient statistics forms a hierarchy of coarsenings; minimal sufficient statistics sit at the bottom of the hierarchy. There is nothing finer you can squeeze out.

Why $(\sum_{i \le n} X_i, \sum_{i > n} X_i)$ is not minimal (Example 3.10)

For i.i.d. $N(\theta, 1)$ data with $\theta \in \mathbb R$, splitting the data into two halves and reporting the two partial sums gives a sufficient statistic — but it carries more information than needed. The full sum $T = \sum_{i=1}^{2n} X_i$ is also sufficient and is a function of the partial-sum pair: $T = f(\tilde T)$ with $f(t_1, t_2) = t_1 + t_2$. Section 3.5 will show $T$ is minimal sufficient. So the two-sum statistic is unnecessarily detailed.

Likelihood shape and minimal sufficiency

Here is the slogan to remember: the minimal sufficient statistic is whatever determines the shape of the likelihood function. Two data points $x$ and $y$ give the same likelihood shape if $p_\theta(x)$ and $p_\theta(y)$ are proportional as functions of $\theta$ — written $p_\theta(x) \propto_\theta p_\theta(y)$.

Theorem 3.11 — likelihood-shape criterion

Suppose $\mathcal{P}$ is a dominated family with densities $p_\theta(x) = g_\theta(T(x))\, h(x)$. If $$p_\theta(x) \propto_\theta p_\theta(y) \implies T(x) = T(y),$$ then $T$ is minimal sufficient.

Why the criterion works

Sufficiency already tells us the likelihood depends on data only through $T$. The reverse implication in the theorem says: distinct values of $T$ produce distinct likelihood shapes. Together: $T$ and the likelihood shape are in one-to-one correspondence. Any other sufficient statistic must also determine the likelihood shape, hence must determine $T$ — that's exactly the definition of minimal sufficiency.

Worked example — exponential family minimal sufficient statistic (Example 3.12)

For an $s$-parameter exponential family $$p_\theta(x) = e^{\eta(\theta) \cdot T(x) - B(\theta)} h(x),$$ suppose $p_\theta(x) \propto_\theta p_\theta(y)$. Cancelling $h$'s and constants, we get $\eta(\theta) \cdot T(x) = \eta(\theta) \cdot T(y) + c$ for some $c$ that does not depend on $\theta$. Taking differences across two parameter values $\theta_0, \theta_1$, the constant $c$ disappears and we are left with $[\eta(\theta_0) - \eta(\theta_1)] \cdot [T(x) - T(y)] = 0$. If the differences $\eta(\theta_0) - \eta(\theta_1)$ span all of $\mathbb R^s$ as $\theta_0, \theta_1$ range over $\Omega$, then $T(x) - T(y) = 0$, hence $T(x) = T(y)$. So $T$ is minimal sufficient — provided the parametrization is rich enough.

Worked example — Laplace location (Example 3.13)

For i.i.d. data from the double-exponential density $f_\theta(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} e^{-|x - \theta|}$, the joint log-likelihood is $-\sum |x_i - \theta| + \text{const}$. Sufficiency from the factorization theorem gives the order statistics $X_{(1)} \le \cdots \le X_{(n)}$ as a sufficient statistic. Are they minimal? Two datasets $x, y$ produce proportional likelihoods only when $\sum |x_i - \theta|$ and $\sum |y_i - \theta|$ differ by a constant in $\theta$. Each is a piecewise-linear function of $\theta$ whose slope jumps by $2$ at each order statistic. The two piecewise-linear functions can differ by a constant only when their breakpoints coincide — that is, when $x$ and $y$ have the same order statistics. So the order statistics are minimal sufficient.

Pause — is $\bar X$ sufficient for $N(\theta, 1)$?

For i.i.d. $N(\theta, 1)$ with variance known, $\bar X$ is minimal sufficient — try Example 3.12 with the one-parameter exponential form. Now change the question slightly: i.i.d. $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with both unknown. Then $\bar X$ alone is not sufficient — you also need $\sum X_i^2$ (or equivalently $S^2$) to recover the likelihood. The same statistic can be sufficient for one parametrization and inadequate for another. Sufficiency depends on the model, not just the data.

3.5 Completeness

The unfamiliar condition

Completeness is a technical strengthening of sufficiency. It says, roughly: there is no non-trivial function of the statistic whose expectation is zero for every $\theta$.

Definition 3.14 — complete statistic

A statistic $T$ is complete for $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\}$ if $$E_\theta f(T) = 0 \text{ for every } \theta \in \Omega \implies f(T) = 0 \ \text{a.s. under every } P_\theta.$$

What completeness is really saying

Take any function $f$ of $T$ that integrates to zero against every distribution in the family. If the family is "rich enough" — if $\theta$ can scan widely enough over $\Omega$ — the only way to keep the integral at zero for all $\theta$ is for $f$ itself to be zero almost surely. Completeness is a non-degeneracy condition on the family of distributions, not on $T$ in isolation. A tiny family (say, just one distribution) is hardly ever complete; a full-rank exponential family always is.

Worked example — uniform on $(0, \theta)$ (Example 3.16)

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. uniform on $(0, \theta)$ with $\theta > 0$, and let $T = \max_i X_i$. Factorization gives $T$ sufficient. Its density is $p_T(t) = n t^{n-1}/\theta^n$ on $(0, \theta)$. Suppose $E_\theta f(T) = 0$ for every $\theta$. Then $\int_0^\theta f(t) t^{n-1} dt = 0$ for every $\theta > 0$. Differentiating in $\theta$ gives $f(\theta) \theta^{n-1} = 0$, so $f(\theta) = 0$ for every $\theta > 0$. Hence $f \equiv 0$ and $T$ is complete.

Theorem 3.17 — complete + sufficient $\Rightarrow$ minimal sufficient

If $T$ is both complete and sufficient, then $T$ is minimal sufficient.

The proof idea: if $\tilde T$ is minimal sufficient, then $\tilde T = f(T)$ (since $T$ is sufficient and $\tilde T$ is minimal). Define $g(\tilde T) = E[T \mid \tilde T]$ (this is $\theta$-free because $\tilde T$ is sufficient). Then $E_\theta(T - g(\tilde T)) = 0$ for every $\theta$. By completeness, $T = g(\tilde T)$ almost surely. So $T$ and $\tilde T$ are one-to-one functions of each other, and $T$ is also minimal sufficient.

Full-rank exponential families: the cleanest case

Definition 3.18 — full rank

An exponential family $p_\theta(x) = \exp\{\eta(\theta) \cdot T(x) - B(\theta)\} h(x)$ is of full rank if the interior of $\eta(\Omega)$ is non-empty and the components $T_1, \ldots, T_s$ do not satisfy any linear constraint $v \cdot T = c$ almost surely.

Theorem 3.19

In a full-rank exponential family, $T$ is complete (and hence, with sufficiency from factorization, minimal sufficient).

Worked example — Normal known variance

For i.i.d. $N(\mu, 1)$ data with $\mu \in \mathbb R$, the joint density is a one-parameter exponential family with sufficient statistic $T = \sum X_i$ and natural parameter $\eta = \mu$ ranging over all of $\mathbb R$. Full rank holds, so $T$ — equivalently $\bar X$ — is complete and minimal sufficient.

Worked example — Poisson

For i.i.d. Poisson$(\lambda)$ data with $\lambda > 0$, the natural parameter is $\eta = \log \lambda \in \mathbb R$ and $T = \sum X_i$. The family is full-rank, so $T$ is complete and minimal sufficient. This will make Poisson examples a recurring testbed in Chapter 4 (unbiased estimation).

Common pitfall — completeness without full rank

If you restrict an exponential family's parameter space — say, fix $\sigma^2$ in a normal model to a known value rather than letting it vary — the rank may drop, and what was complete becomes incomplete. A standard counterexample: $X \sim N(\theta, \theta^2)$ (so the mean and variance are tied together). The natural parameter vector traces a curve in $\mathbb{R}^2$, not an open set, and completeness fails. Lesson: completeness is a property of the whole family, and rank-deficiency kills it.

3.6 Ancillarity and Basu's theorem

Statistics that say nothing about $\theta$

Definition 3.20 — ancillary statistic

A statistic $V = V(X)$ is ancillary for $\mathcal{P}$ if its distribution does not depend on $\theta$.

Why ancillaries matter

An ancillary statistic by itself contains zero information about $\theta$ — its distribution is the same whatever $\theta$ is. Examples: differences of order statistics in a location family, ratios in a scale family, the sample variance in a $N(\mu, 1)$ location model. Although they say nothing about $\theta$ on their own, ancillary statistics can interact with sufficient statistics in subtle ways — especially in conditional inference (Chapter 7).

Theorem 3.21 — Basu's theorem

If $T$ is complete and sufficient for $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\}$ and $V$ is ancillary, then $T$ and $V$ are independent under every $P_\theta$.

Reading Basu

The information about $\theta$ has been wrung out of the data into $T$, and there is none in $V$. Completeness guarantees that this clean split goes a step further: not just informationally separate, but probabilistically independent. Independence is much stronger than no-shared-information; it's a remarkable bonus.

Worked example — independence of $\bar X$ and $S^2$ (Example 3.22)

Fix $\sigma$ known and let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\mu \in \mathbb R$. The family $\mathcal{P}_\sigma$ is a full-rank one-parameter exponential family with complete sufficient statistic $\bar X$. Now consider $S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (X_i - \bar X)^2$. Setting $Y_i = X_i - \mu$, the $Y_i$ are i.i.d. $N(0, \sigma^2)$ — a distribution that does not depend on $\mu$. And $S^2$ written in terms of the $Y_i$ is identical: $S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum (Y_i - \bar Y)^2$. So $S^2$ has the same distribution for every $\mu$ — it is ancillary. By Basu, $\bar X \perp S^2$. This is the famous independence used in deriving the $t$-distribution (Chapter 4).

Pause — Basu in disguise

Basu's theorem yields many "miraculous" independence results almost for free. Examples to chew on: for i.i.d. uniform$(0, 1)$ data, $\max_i X_i$ is complete sufficient and $X_1/\max_i X_i$ is ancillary — so they're independent. For i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ data, $\bar X$ is complete sufficient and $X_1/\bar X$ is ancillary — independent. Each looks like a calculation; Basu makes it a one-liner.

Common pitfall — Basu needs completeness

Sufficiency alone is not enough. Without completeness, an ancillary statistic can absolutely be correlated with a sufficient one. The classical counterexample uses a curved exponential family. Don't drop the "complete" hypothesis from Basu.

3.7 Convex loss and the Rao–Blackwell theorem

Convex functions, briefly

A function $f$ on a convex set $C \subseteq \mathbb R^p$ is convex if for every $x \ne y$ in $C$ and every $\gamma \in (0, 1)$, $$f(\gamma x + (1 - \gamma) y) \le \gamma f(x) + (1 - \gamma) f(y).$$ Geometrically, the graph of $f$ lies below the chord joining any two of its points. If the inequality is strict whenever $x \ne y$, $f$ is strictly convex. Common examples: $x^2$, $e^x$, $-\log x$, $|x|$ (not strictly), $1/x$ on $(0, \infty)$.

Theorem 3.25 — Jensen's inequality

Let $f$ be convex on an open interval $C$, $X$ a random variable with $P(X \in C) = 1$ and $E|X| < \infty$. Then $f(EX) \le E f(X)$. If $f$ is strictly convex, the inequality is strict unless $X$ is almost surely constant.

Mental picture

Drop a support line through the point $(EX, f(EX))$ on the graph of $f$. The line sits below the graph everywhere. Take expectations on both sides of "line $\le f$" and the line's value at $EX$ is exactly $f(EX)$ — that's the inequality. Strictness comes from the fact that the gap between line and graph is positive everywhere except at the tangent point.

Rao–Blackwell: condition on a sufficient statistic, win for free

Theorem 3.28 — Rao–Blackwell

Let $T$ be sufficient for $\mathcal{P}$, let $\delta(X)$ be an estimator of $g(\theta)$, and define $$\eta(T) = E[\delta(X) \mid T].$$ If $L(\theta, \cdot)$ is convex and $R(\theta, \delta) < \infty$ for every $\theta \in \Omega$, then $$R(\theta, \eta) \le R(\theta, \delta) \quad \text{for every } \theta.$$ If $L(\theta, \cdot)$ is strictly convex, the inequality is strict unless $\delta(X) = \eta(T)$ almost surely under $P_\theta$.

The Rao–Blackwell move in one sentence

Given any estimator, replace it by its conditional expectation given a sufficient statistic. The result is a function of $T$ alone, and (for convex loss) it has at most the risk of the original — usually less. It's the cheapest improvement in statistics: just apply $E[\cdot \mid T]$.

Two things are subtly important. First, $\eta(T) = E[\delta(X) \mid T]$ is itself a statistic — it does not depend on $\theta$ — precisely because $T$ is sufficient. (Without sufficiency, conditional expectations would generally involve $\theta$ and couldn't be used as estimators.) Second, the improvement is strict for strictly convex loss unless $\delta$ was already a function of $T$. So once you've Rao–Blackwellized once, doing it again gains nothing.

Worked example — Bernoulli, estimating $\theta^2$

Let $X_1, \ldots, X_n$ be i.i.d. Bernoulli$(\theta)$. The crude estimator $\delta(X) = X_1 X_2$ has $E_\theta \delta = \theta^2$, so it is unbiased for $\theta^2$, but its variance is huge — most of the data is being ignored. Take $T = \sum X_i$ (the complete sufficient statistic) and Rao–Blackwellize: $$\eta(T) = E[X_1 X_2 \mid T] = P(X_1 = 1, X_2 = 1 \mid T) = \frac{T(T-1)}{n(n-1)}.$$ This new estimator is still unbiased for $\theta^2$ (the conditional expectation preserves $E_\theta$) and has uniformly smaller variance. We will see in Chapter 4 that, since $T$ is complete, $\eta(T)$ is in fact the unique minimum-variance unbiased estimator of $\theta^2$.

Pause — why convexity?

The proof of Rao–Blackwell is literally Jensen's inequality applied to the conditional distribution of $\delta(X)$ given $T$: convexity of $L(\theta, \cdot)$ pushes the expectation inside. Drop convexity (e.g. with $0/1$-loss in classification) and the theorem can fail — averaging an estimator can make it worse. Squared error, absolute error, log loss, and Kullback–Leibler are all convex, so Rao–Blackwell applies widely.

Common pitfall — conditioning on the wrong thing

Rao–Blackwell requires conditioning on a sufficient statistic. Conditioning on something arbitrary (e.g. a single $X_i$) does not give a $\theta$-free estimator and can introduce bias. Conditioning on the data $X$ itself returns $\delta$ unchanged and gains nothing. The sweet spot is a non-trivial sufficient $T$ — ideally the minimal sufficient one.

Randomized estimators are inadmissible

A randomized estimator $\delta(X, U)$ uses an auxiliary $\theta$-independent random number $U$. Treating both $X$ and $U$ as data, $X$ alone is sufficient (since $U$ contributes no $\theta$-information). Rao–Blackwell then says $E[\delta(X, U) \mid X]$ has lower risk under any strictly convex loss. So randomization can never help with strictly convex loss — a comforting fact when you start worrying whether your estimator should secretly involve a coin flip.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 3

The whole chapter, in eight bullets:

  1. A statistical model $\{P_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\}$ pairs each candidate parameter with a distribution for the data. An estimator $\delta(X)$ is any function of the data alone.
  2. A loss function $L(\theta, d)$ encodes the cost of guessing $d$ when the truth is $\theta$. Its expectation, the risk function $R(\theta, \delta)$, scores estimators across all $\theta$.
  3. $T$ is sufficient if the conditional distribution of $X$ given $T$ does not depend on $\theta$. Equivalently (Factorization Theorem), the joint density factors as $g_\theta(T(x)) h(x)$.
  4. $T$ is minimal sufficient if every other sufficient statistic determines $T$. Operationally: $T$ is in one-to-one correspondence with the shape of the likelihood function.
  5. $T$ is complete if no non-trivial function of $T$ has zero expectation under every $\theta$. Full-rank exponential families always have complete minimal sufficient statistics.
  6. A statistic $V$ is ancillary if its distribution does not depend on $\theta$. By Basu's theorem, complete sufficient and ancillary statistics are independent.
  7. Rao–Blackwell: under convex loss, conditioning any estimator on a sufficient statistic produces a $\theta$-free estimator with at most the same risk — strictly less for strictly convex loss unless the original was already a function of $T$.
  8. Sufficient statistics aren't merely "as good as" the data — under convex loss they are strictly better than any estimator that ignores them, and any randomized estimator is beaten by a non-randomized counterpart.
