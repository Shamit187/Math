Chapter 2

Exponential Families

A single algebraic template that captures the binomial, Poisson, normal, exponential, gamma, and most of the distributions you will ever meet — and hands you their moments for free.

Book pages: 25–38 ~35 min reading time Prereqs: Chapter 1 (measures, densities, integrals)

Why exponential families?

Chapter 1 built the vocabulary for talking about one distribution at a time. But statistics is rarely about a single distribution. We have data, we don't know the distribution that produced it, and we want to infer the distribution from the data. To make that tractable, we usually assume the distribution belongs to some parametric family: a collection of candidate distributions indexed by a parameter $\theta$.

Most of the parametric families you have ever heard of — Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, geometric, negative binomial, normal, exponential, gamma, beta, multinomial — turn out to share a single algebraic skeleton. That skeleton is the exponential family. The point of this chapter is to identify that skeleton, write it down once, and then reap the rewards: clean formulas for moments, sufficient statistics that fall out automatically, and differentiation tricks that replace pages of calculus.

The promise of this chapter

If a family fits the exponential template, you get a "machine" that turns one derivative of one function into the mean, two derivatives into the variance, three into the skewness — and so on. The hard work is hidden inside the template itself; once you write your distribution in canonical form, the calculations become almost mechanical.

2.1 Densities and Parameters

Building the template piece by piece

We are going to assemble a single formula that, with different ingredients, produces almost every classical density. Before stating it, here are the ingredients — each one is something you have met in Chapter 1.

  • $\mu$ — a base measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$. Usually Lebesgue measure (continuous data) or counting measure on the integers (discrete data).
  • $h : \mathbb{R}^n \to [0, \infty)$ — a fixed non-negative "carrier" function. It does not depend on any parameter; it just says where the distribution can live and shapes things slightly.
  • $T_1, \dots, T_s : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ — a finite list of measurable functions of the data, called sufficient statistics. (We will see why this name fits in Chapter 3.)
  • $\eta = (\eta_1, \dots, \eta_s) \in \mathbb{R}^s$ — a vector of canonical parameters, one for each $T_i$.

The recipe combines them by exponentiating a linear combination of the $T_i$'s, multiplying by the carrier $h$, and then renormalizing so the result integrates to $1$. The normalization constant is what makes the whole thing work, and it gets a name of its own.

Definition 2.1 — canonical exponential family

For each $\eta \in \mathbb{R}^s$, let

$$A(\eta) = \log \int \exp\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^s \eta_i T_i(x)\right] h(x) \, d\mu(x). \tag{2.1}$$

Whenever $A(\eta) < \infty$, the function

$$p_\eta(x) = \exp\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^s \eta_i T_i(x) - A(\eta)\right] h(x) \tag{2.2}$$

is a probability density with respect to $\mu$. The set

$$\Xi = \{\eta \in \mathbb{R}^s : A(\eta) < \infty\}$$

is the natural parameter space, and $\{p_\eta : \eta \in \Xi\}$ is an $s$-parameter exponential family in canonical form.

Decoding the formula

Look at the exponent: $\sum \eta_i T_i(x) - A(\eta)$. The first piece, $\sum \eta_i T_i(x)$, is the only place $x$ and $\eta$ meet — and they meet linearly. The second piece, $A(\eta)$, depends only on the parameter, not on $x$. Its job is bookkeeping: $A(\eta)$ is whatever it has to be to make $\int p_\eta \, d\mu = 1$. That is why we took the logarithm in (2.1) — exponentiating $-A(\eta)$ then divides out the total mass.

So the formula says: any density of the form "$h(x)$ times an exponential of a linear function of the $T_i(x)$'s, divided by a normalizing constant" is in the family. The structure is rigid; only the linear coefficients $\eta_i$ vary across the family.

A glossary of the Greek letters and subscripts, since they all matter:

  • $\eta_i$ ("eta-sub-$i$", Greek "eta") — the $i$-th canonical parameter. Real-valued.
  • $T_i(x)$ — the $i$-th sufficient statistic. A function of the data, not of $\eta$.
  • $A(\eta)$ — the log-partition function or cumulant generating function of the family. The single most important object in the chapter; almost every theorem we prove is about its derivatives.
  • $\Xi$ ("Xi", Greek capital X) — the set of $\eta$'s for which $A(\eta)$ is finite. Some parameter values blow the normalizer up to $\infty$; we must exclude them.
  • $h(x)$ — the carrier. Often $h(x) = 1$ on the support and $0$ off it, but not always.

A worked example with concrete numbers

Example 2.1 — the exponential distribution

Let $\mu$ be Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$, $h(x) = 1_{(0, \infty)}(x)$ (the indicator that $x > 0$), $s = 1$, and $T_1(x) = x$. Then

$$A(\eta) = \log \int_0^\infty e^{\eta x} \, dx.$$

This integral converges only when $\eta < 0$ (otherwise $e^{\eta x}$ does not decay). For $\eta < 0$, $\int_0^\infty e^{\eta x} \, dx = -1/\eta$, so

$$A(\eta) = \log(-1/\eta), \qquad \Xi = (-\infty, 0).$$

The resulting density is $p_\eta(x) = e^{\eta x - \log(-1/\eta)} \cdot 1_{(0,\infty)}(x) = (-\eta) e^{\eta x} \cdot 1_{(0,\infty)}(x)$. Writing $\lambda = -\eta > 0$, this is $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ — the familiar exponential density with rate $\lambda$.

Pause — the parameter you know vs. the parameter the theory likes

Notice that the natural parameter $\eta$ is not the rate $\lambda$ you learned in undergrad. It is $-\lambda$. The canonical parameter is whatever makes the density factor cleanly into $e^{\eta T(x)}$ times stuff — it does not have to match the parameter you are used to. This mismatch is normal and we deal with it next.

Reparameterization: writing the family in your favorite coordinates

Most textbook distributions are parameterized in a "natural-language" way — the exponential by its rate $\lambda$ or mean $1/\lambda$, the normal by $(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the binomial by success probability $p$. These parameters are usually not the canonical $\eta$. To accommodate this, we allow a change of variables.

Definition 2.1' — general exponential family

Let $\eta : \Omega \to \Xi$ be a function from some parameter space $\Omega$ into the natural parameter space. For $\theta \in \Omega$, define

$$p_\theta(x) = \exp\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^s \eta_i(\theta) T_i(x) - B(\theta)\right] h(x),$$

where $B(\theta) = A(\eta(\theta))$. The family $\{p_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\}$ is an $s$-parameter exponential family.

What just happened

We did not change the family of distributions — we changed the labels on the distributions. Each $\theta$ in $\Omega$ picks out an $\eta(\theta)$ in $\Xi$, and the density $p_\theta$ is identical to $p_{\eta(\theta)}$. So you can work in whatever coordinates you find natural, and switch to canonical coordinates only when you need the differential identities of Section 2.2.

Example 2.2 — the normal distribution as a 2-parameter family

The $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ density is

$$p_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}.$$

Expand the squared term and group by powers of $x$:

$$p_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left[\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} x - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} x^2 - \left(\frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log\sigma\right)\right].$$

Reading off the ingredients with $\theta = (\mu, \sigma^2)$:

  • $T_1(x) = x$ with $\eta_1(\theta) = \mu/\sigma^2$,
  • $T_2(x) = x^2$ with $\eta_2(\theta) = -1/(2\sigma^2)$,
  • $B(\theta) = \mu^2/(2\sigma^2) + \log\sigma$,
  • $h(x) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

So $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is a 2-parameter exponential family with sufficient statistics $x$ and $x^2$.

Common pitfall — keeping track of which is which

Two distinct objects share the letter "$A$": the function $A(\eta)$ defined in canonical coordinates, and the function $B(\theta) = A(\eta(\theta))$ in the original coordinates. They have the same values but different derivatives because they are functions of different variables. When the chapter writes "$\partial A / \partial \eta_j$" the differentiation is in canonical coordinates. Forgetting this leads to chain-rule errors.

i.i.d. samples — exponential structure survives

Statisticians almost never work with one observation. They work with a sample $X_1, \dots, X_n$, usually i.i.d. A beautiful property of exponential families is that i.i.d. samples form another exponential family, with the same canonical parameters but new sufficient statistics that are sums.

Suppose each $X_j$ has the marginal density $\exp[\sum_i \eta_i(\theta) T_i(x) - B(\theta)] h(x)$. By independence, the joint density of $(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ is the product of the marginals, which equals

$$\exp\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^s \eta_i(\theta) \!\left(\sum_{j=1}^n T_i(X_j)\right) - n B(\theta)\right] \prod_{j=1}^n h(X_j). \tag{2.3}$$

This is again exponential family form, with new ingredients

$$\tilde T_i(x) = \sum_{j=1}^n T_i(x_j), \quad \tilde B(\theta) = n B(\theta), \quad \tilde h(x) = \prod_{j=1}^n h(x_j).$$
Example 2.3 — i.i.d. normal sample

For $X_1, \dots, X_n \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, the joint density is exponential family with

$$\tilde T_1 = \sum_{j=1}^n X_j, \qquad \tilde T_2 = \sum_{j=1}^n X_j^2,$$

the same canonical parameters $\eta_1 = \mu/\sigma^2$ and $\eta_2 = -1/(2\sigma^2)$ as before, and $\tilde B(\theta) = n[\mu^2/(2\sigma^2) + \log\sigma]$. The whole information content of the sample is squeezed into the two numbers $\sum X_j$ and $\sum X_j^2$. (We will prove this rigorously in Chapter 3 as a statement about sufficiency.)

Why this matters in practice

Whatever conclusions you draw from a normal sample of size $n$, you can draw them by looking only at $\sum X_j$ and $\sum X_j^2$. The remaining $n - 2$ numbers in the data are statistical noise — they tell you nothing about $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ that the two sums do not already say. This dimensional collapse is the single most useful thing exponential families do for us.

2.2 Differential Identities

The payoff: moments by differentiation

Here is the promised reward. In a canonical exponential family, taking partial derivatives of $A(\eta)$ gives us expectations of the sufficient statistics. No integrals, no clever substitutions — just calculus.

The argument is short, almost suspiciously so. Start from the definition of $A$:

$$e^{A(\eta)} = \int \exp\!\left[\sum_i \eta_i T_i(x)\right] h(x) \, d\mu(x).$$

If we could differentiate both sides with respect to $\eta_j$, and if we could swap the order of differentiation and integration on the right, we would get

$$e^{A(\eta)} \, \frac{\partial A(\eta)}{\partial \eta_j} = \int T_j(x) \exp\!\left[\sum_i \eta_i T_i(x)\right] h(x) \, d\mu(x).$$

Dividing both sides by $e^{A(\eta)}$ rewrites the right-hand side as an expectation under $p_\eta$, because $p_\eta(x) = e^{\sum_i \eta_i T_i(x) - A(\eta)} h(x)$. We arrive at the master identity:

$$\boxed{\quad E_\eta T_j(X) = \frac{\partial A(\eta)}{\partial \eta_j} \quad} \tag{2.4}$$

The big asterisk on this derivation is the "if we could swap differentiation and integration" step. The next theorem says: inside the interior of the natural parameter space, we can.

Theorem 2.4 — differentiation under the integral

Let $\Xi_f$ be the set of $\eta \in \mathbb{R}^s$ for which

$$\int |f(x)| \exp\!\left[\sum_i \eta_i T_i(x)\right] h(x) \, d\mu(x) < \infty.$$

Then

$$g(\eta) = \int f(x) \exp\!\left[\sum_i \eta_i T_i(x)\right] h(x) \, d\mu(x)$$

is continuous and has continuous partial derivatives of all orders for $\eta$ in the interior $\Xi_f^\circ$. These derivatives can be computed by differentiating under the integral sign.

What this is buying us

Theorem 2.4 is a license. It says: anywhere inside the natural parameter space (not on the boundary, but inside), you may treat the integral as if it were a finite sum. You can differentiate term-by-term, swap limits, take mixed partials in any order — all of it. The proof relies on the dominated convergence theorem, which we will unpack in Section 2.3.

Higher moments — variances and covariances by second derivatives

Equation (2.4) was just the first derivative. Differentiating once more gives the second moment, and the structure turns out to be the variance/covariance, not the raw second moment. The clean way to see this is to differentiate (2.4) again:

$$\frac{\partial^2 A(\eta)}{\partial \eta_j \partial \eta_k} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \eta_k} E_\eta T_j(X).$$

Carrying out the derivative inside the expectation (Theorem 2.4 again) gives $\operatorname{Cov}_\eta(T_j(X), T_k(X))$. In particular,

$$\frac{\partial^2 A(\eta)}{\partial \eta_j^2} = \operatorname{Var}_\eta(T_j(X)).$$

We will get to a full statement of this in Section 2.4 in the language of cumulant generating functions. For now, the slogan: first derivatives of $A$ give means, second derivatives give variances and covariances.

Worked example — Poisson via differentiation

For $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$, the mass function is

$$P(X = x) = \frac{\lambda^x e^{-\lambda}}{x!} = \frac{1}{x!} \exp[x \log\lambda - \lambda], \quad x = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Setting $\eta = \log\lambda$ (so $\lambda = e^\eta$), $T(x) = x$, $h(x) = 1/x!$, and $\mu$ counting measure on $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, the mass function becomes

$$P(X = x) = \frac{1}{x!} \exp[\eta x - e^\eta].$$

So $A(\eta) = e^\eta$. The natural parameter space is all of $\mathbb{R}$ (every $\eta$ gives a finite $A$). Differentiating:

$$E_\eta X = A'(\eta) = e^\eta = \lambda, \quad \operatorname{Var}_\eta(X) = A''(\eta) = e^\eta = \lambda.$$

Mean and variance both equal $\lambda$ — a classical fact derived without ever summing $\sum_x x \lambda^x e^{-\lambda}/x!$ by hand. And every higher derivative gives $\lambda$ too, so all cumulants of the Poisson are equal to $\lambda$.

Worked example — Bernoulli via differentiation

For $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ on $\{0, 1\}$,

$$P(X = x) = p^x (1-p)^{1-x} = \exp\!\left[x \log\frac{p}{1-p} + \log(1-p)\right].$$

The canonical parameter is $\eta = \log\frac{p}{1-p}$ (the log-odds or logit), $T(x) = x$, $h(x) = 1$, and $\mu$ is counting measure on $\{0, 1\}$. Solving for $p$ gives $p = e^\eta/(1 + e^\eta)$, so $1 - p = 1/(1 + e^\eta)$ and

$$A(\eta) = -\log(1-p) = \log(1 + e^\eta).$$

Differentiating:

$$E_\eta X = A'(\eta) = \frac{e^\eta}{1 + e^\eta} = p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}_\eta(X) = A''(\eta) = \frac{e^\eta}{(1+e^\eta)^2} = p(1-p).$$

Both classical results in one line. Notice also that the logit appears here not because anyone chose it deliberately, but because it is forced on us by the requirement that the density factor into "$e^{\eta x}$ times stuff that doesn't depend on $\eta$".

Pause — boundary vs. interior

Theorem 2.4 applies in the interior $\Xi^\circ$ of the natural parameter space. The boundary points are excluded. For the exponential family in Example 2.1, $\Xi = (-\infty, 0)$ is open and equals its interior; no issue. For some families, the boundary represents degenerate cases (e.g.\ a binomial with $p = 0$ or $p = 1$), and the derivative identities can fail there. Always check that $\eta$ is interior before invoking (2.4).

2.3 Dominated Convergence

Why this section exists

We assumed in (2.4) that we could swap a derivative with an integral. Let's understand why this is not automatic and what makes it work. The tool is the dominated convergence theorem, one of the workhorse theorems of measure theory — and it will reappear in every later chapter.

A derivative is a limit. So differentiating under an integral amounts to asking: when does

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \int f_n \, d\mu \;=\; \int \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n \, d\mu \;?$$

That is, when can I push a limit inside an integral? The unsettling answer is: not always. Example 2.6 below shows a sequence where the limit of the integrals is $1$ but the integral of the limit is $0$. Something has to prevent this kind of bad behavior, and that something is a "dominating function" — an integrable umbrella that caps the entire sequence from above.

Theorem 2.5 — dominated convergence

Let $f_n$, $n \geq 1$, be measurable functions with $|f_n| \leq g$ (a.e.\ $\mu$) for all $n$. If $\int g \, d\mu < \infty$ and $\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = f(x)$ for $\mu$-almost every $x$, then

$$\int f_n \, d\mu \to \int f \, d\mu \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$
Plain English

"If the sequence of functions $f_n$ stays under a fixed integrable ceiling $g$, then pointwise convergence $f_n \to f$ implies convergence of the integrals." The ceiling $g$ keeps the mass from escaping to infinity. Without it, the functions can "run away" sideways or vertically, and you lose control.

Example 2.6 — what goes wrong without domination

Let $\mu$ be Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$ and define $f_n(x) = 1_{(n, n+1)}(x)$ — a bump of height $1$ and width $1$ sliding off to the right. For every fixed $x$, $f_n(x) = 0$ once $n$ is large enough, so $f_n \to 0$ pointwise. But $\int f_n \, d\mu = 1$ for every $n$, while $\int 0 \, d\mu = 0$. The integrals do not converge to the integral of the limit.

The trouble: no integrable function $g$ dominates the entire sequence. To bound every $f_n$ you would need $g \geq 1$ on $(1, \infty)$, but then $\int g \, d\mu = \infty$.

Applying dominated convergence to swap derivative and integral

Let's see how dominated convergence justifies (2.4). For simplicity take $s = 1$, so $A$ is a function of one variable. To differentiate at $\eta = 0$ inside an interval $[-2\epsilon, 2\epsilon] \subseteq \Xi$, write the derivative as a limit of difference quotients:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{e^{A(\epsilon/n)} - e^{A(0)}}{\epsilon/n} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \int f_n(x) \, d\mu(x), \quad f_n(x) = \frac{e^{\epsilon T(x)/n} - 1}{\epsilon/n} \, h(x). \tag{2.5}$$

As $n \to \infty$, $f_n(x) \to T(x) h(x)$ pointwise — this is just $\frac{d}{d\eta} e^{\eta T(x)}|_{\eta = 0}$. The question is whether $\int f_n \, d\mu \to \int T(x) h(x) \, d\mu$. By dominated convergence, it does — provided we can find a dominating $g$.

Using the elementary bounds $|e^t - 1| \leq |t| e^{|t|}$ and $|t| \leq e^{|t|}$ for any real $t$, one shows that $|f_n(x)| \leq \frac{1}{\epsilon}\big(e^{2\epsilon T(x)} + e^{-2\epsilon T(x)}\big) h(x) =: g(x)$. And $g$ is integrable because

$$\int e^{\pm 2\epsilon T(x)} h(x) \, d\mu(x) = e^{A(\pm 2\epsilon)} < \infty,$$

which is precisely why we asked for $\eta$ to be interior to $\Xi$ — we need a small interval $[-2\epsilon, 2\epsilon]$ on which $A$ is finite, so the bound $g$ is integrable.

The whole argument in one sentence

Being inside the natural parameter space gives us room to wiggle; the wiggle room provides an integrable dominator; the dominator licenses dominated convergence; dominated convergence licenses differentiation under the integral. That is why Theorem 2.4 is true.

Common pitfall — pointwise convergence is not enough

A frequent mistake is to write "$f_n \to f$ pointwise, so $\int f_n \to \int f$". Example 2.6 is the warning. Always check for a dominating function (or use a different theorem like monotone convergence). In exponential family arguments, the dominator is essentially always built from $e^{\pm 2\epsilon T(x)} h(x)$ for $\epsilon$ small enough to keep $\eta \pm 2\epsilon$ inside $\Xi$.

2.4 Moments, Cumulants, and Generating Functions

The moment generating function

Let $T = (T_1, \dots, T_s)'$ be a random vector in $\mathbb{R}^s$ (the prime denotes transpose; $T$ is a column). The moment generating function (MGF) of $T$ is

$$M_T(u) = E\, e^{u_1 T_1 + \cdots + u_s T_s} = E\, e^{u \cdot T}, \quad u \in \mathbb{R}^s,$$

and the cumulant generating function (CGF) is its logarithm,

$$K_T(u) = \log M_T(u).$$
Why "moment generating"

Formally expanding the exponential, $e^{u \cdot T} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty (u \cdot T)^k / k!$. Taking expectations, $M_T(u) = \sum_k E[(u \cdot T)^k] / k!$. So differentiating $M_T$ at the origin picks off the moments of $T$ — exactly the way a Taylor series picks off derivatives of its function. The cumulants are similarly extracted from $K_T$; we will see in a moment that they are the more useful objects.

Lemma 2.7 — MGF determines distribution

If the MGFs $M_X(u)$ and $M_Y(u)$ of two random vectors $X, Y$ are both finite and agree on some set with non-empty interior, then $P_X = P_Y$ — they have the same distribution.

Translation

The MGF is a fingerprint. If two random variables share a moment generating function over even a tiny open ball, they share a distribution. This is what makes MGFs useful for identifying distributions: compute the MGF, match it against a table, declare victory.

Moments and cumulants

Define the $(r_1, \dots, r_s)$-th moment and cumulant of $T$ by

$$\alpha_{r_1, \dots, r_s} = E[T_1^{r_1} \cdots T_s^{r_s}], \qquad \kappa_{r_1, \dots, r_s} = \frac{\partial^{r_1}}{\partial u_1^{r_1}} \cdots \frac{\partial^{r_s}}{\partial u_s^{r_s}} K_T(u) \Big|_{u=0}.$$

A parallel formula (Theorem 2.8) says the same partials of $M_T$ at $0$ give the moments $\alpha_{r_1, \dots, r_s}$. Both statements require $M_T$ to be finite in some neighborhood of the origin — the same "interior" condition we needed for differentiation under the integral.

What cumulants are, intuitively

For $s = 1$, the relation $K = \log M$ implies $K' = M'/M$. Differentiating once more (using the quotient rule) gives $K'' = (M M'' - (M')^2)/M^2$. Evaluating both at $u = 0$, where $M(0) = 1$, yields

$$\kappa_1 = E T, \qquad \kappa_2 = E T^2 - (E T)^2 = \operatorname{Var}(T).$$

So the first cumulant is the mean, the second is the variance. Higher cumulants describe higher-order shape: $\kappa_3$ measures skewness, $\kappa_4$ measures (excess) kurtosis, and so on.

Cumulants vs. moments — why bother with cumulants?

Cumulants have a magical additivity property: cumulants of sums of independent random variables add. If $T = Y_1 + \cdots + Y_n$ with the $Y_j$'s independent, then by independence of $e^{u \cdot Y_j}$'s,

$$M_T(u) = M_{Y_1}(u) \cdots M_{Y_n}(u), \qquad K_T(u) = K_{Y_1}(u) + \cdots + K_{Y_n}(u).$$

Taking partials at $0$, the cumulants of $T$ are sums of the cumulants of the $Y_j$. This is exactly why the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, the variance of an independent sum is the sum of the variances, and similar identities for higher orders. Moments themselves do not add this way (think $E[(X+Y)^2] \neq E X^2 + E Y^2$ in general), but their cumulants do.

Why exponential families love cumulants: $A$ is the CGF (almost)

Suppose $X$ has a canonical exponential family density $p_\eta$ and let $T = T(X)$ be the vector of sufficient statistics. Compute its MGF directly from the density:

$$E_\eta e^{u \cdot T(X)} = \int e^{u \cdot T(x)} e^{\eta \cdot T(x) - A(\eta)} h(x) \, d\mu(x) = e^{A(u + \eta) - A(\eta)},$$

where the last equality holds whenever $u + \eta \in \Xi$, because then the integrand is itself $p_{u+\eta}$ multiplied by $e^{A(u+\eta) - A(\eta)}$ and integrates to that constant. Taking the logarithm,

$$\boxed{\quad K_T(u) = A(u + \eta) - A(\eta). \quad}$$

This is the central identity. It says: the CGF of the sufficient statistic $T$ is just the log-partition function $A$ shifted by $\eta$. Differentiating with respect to $u_j$ at $u = 0$ gives

$$\kappa_j = \frac{\partial A(\eta)}{\partial \eta_j} \quad \text{(matches (2.4))},$$

and the matrix of second partials of $A$ at $\eta$ is the covariance matrix of $T$. More generally, all cumulants of $T$ are partial derivatives of $A$ at $\eta$. We have promoted $A$ from "normalizing nuisance" to "moment-generating machine".

Example 2.10 — all cumulants of the Poisson

We saw that for Poisson($\lambda$), the canonical parameter is $\eta = \log\lambda$ and $A(\eta) = e^\eta$. Every derivative of $A$ is again $e^\eta = \lambda$. So

$$\kappa_1 = \kappa_2 = \kappa_3 = \cdots = \lambda.$$

All cumulants of a Poisson are equal to its mean. This is a uniquely Poisson signature; no other distribution does this.

Example 2.11 — normal with $\sigma^2$ fixed

The family $\{N(\mu, \sigma^2) : \mu \in \mathbb{R}\}$ with $\sigma^2$ fixed is a 1-parameter exponential family with $T(x) = x$, $\eta = \mu/\sigma^2$, and $A(\eta) = \sigma^2 \eta^2 / 2$. Differentiating:

$$\kappa_1 = A'(\eta) = \sigma^2 \eta = \mu, \quad \kappa_2 = A''(\eta) = \sigma^2, \quad \kappa_3 = \kappa_4 = \cdots = 0.$$

All cumulants beyond the second are zero — another distinctive signature, this one of the normal. (In fact, a theorem of Marcinkiewicz says the normal is the only distribution with finitely many non-zero cumulants.)

From cumulants to moments and back

The identities $M = e^K$, differentiated repeatedly and evaluated at zero, give a sequence of equations relating moments to cumulants:

$$E T = \kappa_1, \quad E T^2 = \kappa_2 + \kappa_1^2, \quad E T^3 = \kappa_3 + 3 \kappa_1 \kappa_2 + \kappa_1^3,$$ $$E T^4 = \kappa_4 + 3 \kappa_2^2 + 4 \kappa_1 \kappa_3 + 6 \kappa_1^2 \kappa_2 + \kappa_1^4.$$

These can be inverted to express cumulants in terms of moments. Centering simplifies the algebra: shifting $T$ by a constant changes only $\kappa_1$, leaving all higher cumulants alone. So for centered variables ($\kappa_1 = 0$),

$$\kappa_3 = E(T - ET)^3, \qquad \kappa_4 = E(T - ET)^4 - 3 \operatorname{Var}^2(T).$$

The first formula says skewness is the third central moment; the second says (excess) kurtosis is the fourth central moment, minus the value it would have for a normal.

Worked example — moments of Poisson from cumulants

Using the moment-from-cumulant identities with $\kappa_1 = \kappa_2 = \kappa_3 = \kappa_4 = \lambda$,

$$E X = \lambda, \quad E X^2 = \lambda + \lambda^2, \quad E X^3 = \lambda + 3\lambda^2 + \lambda^3,$$ $$E X^4 = \lambda + 7\lambda^2 + 6\lambda^3 + \lambda^4.$$

The classical "Poisson moments" all fall out of "$A(\eta) = e^\eta$" plus the moment/cumulant translation.

Pause — what just happened

We did not integrate anything in the last example. We wrote down $A(\eta) = e^\eta$, observed that it is its own derivative, read off all cumulants, and translated to moments by a fixed algebraic recipe. This is what "exponential families turn statistics into calculus" really means.

Higher-dimensional cumulants

In dimension $s > 1$, the first-order cumulants $\kappa_{1,0,\dots,0}, \kappa_{0,1,0,\dots,0}, \dots$ are the means $E T_1, \dots, E T_s$. The second-order cumulants are the entries of the covariance matrix: $\kappa_{2,0,\dots,0} = \operatorname{Var}(T_1)$, $\kappa_{1,1,0,\dots,0} = \operatorname{Cov}(T_1, T_2)$, and so on. Higher mixed cumulants have less elementary interpretations and become combinatorially complex — but their additivity over independent sums remains the property that makes them indispensable for limit theorems.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 2

The chapter, distilled to seven bullets:

  1. An exponential family is the set of densities of the form $p_\eta(x) = h(x) \exp[\sum_i \eta_i T_i(x) - A(\eta)]$, where $A$ is whatever it has to be to normalize.
  2. The natural parameter space $\Xi$ is the set of $\eta$'s for which $A$ is finite. The canonical form uses $\eta$ as the parameter; reparameterizations swap in $\theta$ via a function $\eta(\theta)$.
  3. i.i.d. samples from an exponential family form another exponential family, with sufficient statistics $\tilde T_i = \sum_j T_i(X_j)$ — sums replace individual values. This is the first hint of sufficiency.
  4. Theorem 2.4: inside the interior of $\Xi$, you can differentiate under the integral sign as many times as you like. The key tool is dominated convergence.
  5. The first identity: $E_\eta T_j(X) = \partial A / \partial \eta_j$. The second: $\operatorname{Cov}_\eta(T_j, T_k) = \partial^2 A / (\partial \eta_j \partial \eta_k)$.
  6. Dominated convergence: if $|f_n| \leq g$ with $\int g \, d\mu < \infty$ and $f_n \to f$ a.e., then $\int f_n \, d\mu \to \int f \, d\mu$. The "umbrella" function $g$ is what makes the swap legal.
  7. For canonical exponential families the CGF of $T$ is $K_T(u) = A(u + \eta) - A(\eta)$, so all cumulants of $T$ are partial derivatives of $A$ at $\eta$. The log-partition function is the cumulant generating function, up to a shift.
