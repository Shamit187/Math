Unbiased Estimation

From "best unbiased" to "maybe bias isn't the enemy": how risk, sufficiency, and Fisher information conspire to set the speed limit on estimation.

Book pages: 61–83 ~45 min reading time Prereqs: Chapters 1–3

4.1 Minimum Variance Unbiased Estimators

Why this chapter exists at all

In Chapter 3 we learned that "best estimator" is a slippery phrase. Two estimators can each be better on a different part of the parameter space — their risk curves cross, and there is no clean winner. So we either change the loss function, change the criterion (minimax, Bayes), or change the class of estimators we consider. This chapter pursues the third option.

The idea is simple: if we restrict to estimators that are unbiased — meaning they hit the right answer on average — then comparing risks under squared-error loss reduces to comparing variances. And once we can compare variances directly, we can ask for a single best estimator: the one whose variance is smallest at every $\theta$ simultaneously. When such an estimator exists, it is called UMVU.

The promise is enticing. The catch is that unbiased estimators sometimes don't exist, sometimes don't beat biased competitors, and occasionally are even ridiculous. We'll get to all three. First, the setup.

Definition 4.1 — unbiased estimator

An estimator $\delta$ is unbiased for the parametric function $g(\theta)$ if $$E_\theta \,\delta(X) = g(\theta), \quad \forall \theta \in \Omega.$$ If at least one unbiased estimator exists, $g$ is called U-estimable.

Read the definition carefully. The estimator must equal $g(\theta)$ on average — and it must do so for every $\theta$, not just one. That uniform requirement is what makes unbiasedness restrictive. It also rules out trivial cheats like "always return $0$": that estimator is only unbiased if $g \equiv 0$.

Not every $g$ is U-estimable

Whether $g$ can be estimated unbiasedly depends on what the family of distributions actually lets the data say. Two cautionary tales.

Example 4.1 — uniform $(0, \theta)$

Suppose $X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, \theta)$. An estimator $\delta(X)$ is unbiased for $g(\theta)$ iff $$\int_0^\theta \delta(x) \, \theta^{-1} \, dx = g(\theta), \quad \forall \theta > 0,$$ which rearranges to $$\int_0^\theta \delta(x) \, dx = \theta\, g(\theta).$$ Letting $\theta \downarrow 0$ forces $\theta\, g(\theta) \to 0$. Differentiating both sides with respect to $\theta$ (fundamental theorem of calculus) gives $$\delta(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\bigl(x\, g(x)\bigr) = g(x) + x\, g'(x).$$ So once $g$ is reasonably smooth, the unbiased estimator is forced upon us. For $g(\theta) = \theta$, this gives $\delta(X) = 2X$.

Example 4.2 — binomial and the impossible $\sin\theta$

If $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)$ and we want $g(\theta) = \sin\theta$, then unbiasedness reads $$\sum_{k=0}^n \delta(k) \binom{n}{k} \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n - k} = \sin\theta, \quad \forall \theta \in (0, 1).$$ The left side is a polynomial in $\theta$ of degree at most $n$. The right side is $\sin\theta$, which is not a polynomial. No estimator can fix that — $\sin\theta$ is simply not U-estimable from a binomial sample.

Read this geometrically

Unbiasedness is a linear constraint on the function $\delta$. The set of "things estimable unbiasedly" is therefore the image of estimators under the linear map $\delta \mapsto E_\theta\, \delta$. If your target $g$ lies outside that image, you are out of luck — and no amount of cleverness will help.

From bias to variance to UMVU

With squared-error loss $L(\theta, d) = (d - g(\theta))^2$, the risk of an unbiased estimator collapses to its variance:

$$R(\theta, \delta) = E_\theta\bigl(\delta(X) - g(\theta)\bigr)^2 = \operatorname{Var}_\theta\bigl(\delta(X)\bigr).$$

So the natural goal is: minimize variance, simultaneously, over all $\theta$.

Definition 4.3 — UMVU

An unbiased estimator $\delta$ is uniformly minimum variance unbiased (UMVU) if $$\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta) \leq \operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta^*), \quad \forall \theta \in \Omega,$$ for every competing unbiased estimator $\delta^*$.

"Uniformly" is the load-bearing word. A UMVU estimator must dominate every alternative at every parameter value at once. There is no reason in general to expect such an animal to exist. Yet — and this is the magic — when the family has a complete sufficient statistic, a UMVU does exist whenever the target is U-estimable at all.

Theorem 4.4 — UMVU via complete sufficient statistic

If $g$ is U-estimable and $T$ is complete sufficient, then there is an essentially unique unbiased estimator based on $T$, and it is UMVU.

The proof is short and worth following because it ties together every notion from Chapter 3.

Sketch. Take any unbiased $\delta(X)$ and Rao–Blackwellize: $\eta(T) = E[\delta \mid T]$. By smoothing, $E_\theta\, \eta(T) = E_\theta\, \delta = g(\theta)$, so $\eta(T)$ is unbiased. If $\eta^*(T)$ is another unbiased estimator built from $T$, then $E_\theta[\eta(T) - \eta^*(T)] = 0$ for every $\theta$; completeness forces $\eta(T) = \eta^*(T)$ a.s. So $\eta(T)$ is essentially unique. And Rao–Blackwell guarantees its variance never exceeds that of any other unbiased $\delta^*$, by conditioning $\delta^*$ on $T$.

The trick in one sentence

"Find any unbiased estimator, then condition on the complete sufficient statistic." Sufficiency keeps the unbiasedness; completeness keeps the answer unique; Rao–Blackwell shrinks the variance. Three Chapter-3 ideas in one line.

How to actually find a UMVU

The theorem suggests two strategies:

  1. Solve for $\eta(T)$ directly. Write down $E_\theta \,\eta(T) = g(\theta)$ as an integral or sum equation and solve for $\eta$.
  2. Rao–Blackwellize. Take any unbiased estimator you can think of — even a silly one — and compute $E[\delta \mid T]$.
Example 4.5 — uniform sample, redux

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. $\text{Uniform}(0, \theta)$. The maximum $T = \max\{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$ is complete sufficient (Example 3.16). Its density is $n t^{n-1}/\theta^n$ on $(0, \theta)$. So $\eta(T)$ unbiased for $g(\theta)$ means $$\int_0^\theta \eta(t)\, \frac{n t^{n-1}}{\theta^n}\, dt = g(\theta),$$ i.e. $n \int_0^\theta t^{n-1} \eta(t)\, dt = \theta^n g(\theta)$. Differentiating, $$n \theta^{n-1} \eta(\theta) = \frac{d}{d\theta}\bigl(\theta^n g(\theta)\bigr), \qquad \eta(t) = g(t) + \frac{t\, g'(t)}{n}.$$ For $g(\theta) = \theta$, we get the UMVU estimator $\eta(T) = \tfrac{n+1}{n} T$.

The "naïve" unbiased estimator was $\delta = 2\bar X$. A direct computation gives $$\operatorname{Var}_\theta\bigl(\eta(T)\bigr) = \frac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta) = \frac{\theta^2}{3n},$$ so the ratio is $3/(n+2) \to 0$. The UMVU is dramatically better than the moment-matching estimator. This is what sufficiency buys you.

Example 4.6 — binomial $\theta^2$

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be i.i.d. Bernoulli$(\theta)$, so $T = \sum X_i \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \theta)$ is complete sufficient. To estimate $g(\theta) = \theta^2$, start with the silly-but-unbiased $\delta = X_1 X_2$: $E_\theta[X_1 X_2] = \theta^2$. Now condition on $T$: $$P_\theta(X_1 = X_2 = 1 \mid T = t) = \frac{\theta^2 \binom{n-2}{t-2} \theta^{t-2} (1-\theta)^{n-t}}{\binom{n}{t} \theta^t (1-\theta)^{n-t}} = \frac{t(t-1)}{n(n-1)}.$$ The UMVU estimator of $\theta^2$ is therefore $T(T-1)/[n(n-1)]$.

Notice how that second example illustrates the strategy beautifully: a useless-looking estimator (just multiply the first two observations) becomes the best possible estimator after one conditioning step. The "wasted" observations $X_3, \dots, X_n$ are exactly what $T$ uses to clean it up.

Common pitfall

UMVU does not always exist. If the family lacks a complete sufficient statistic, the unbiasedness constraint can be too weak to pick out a single winner — different unbiased estimators may beat each other on different parts of $\Omega$. Theorem 4.4 is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one, but in practice "find the complete sufficient statistic" is the recipe.

4.2 Second Thoughts About Bias

The development so far has been seductive: a single best estimator, a clean recipe, a satisfying theorem. But the whole construction rests on a premise that deserves scrutiny — that unbiasedness is the right property to demand. It often isn't.

Bias-variance trade-off: a biased estimator can win

Back to the uniform example. The UMVU estimator of $\theta$ was $\eta(T) = \tfrac{n+1}{n}T$. But we restricted to the form $\delta_a = aT$; what is the best multiplier $a$ under squared-error risk, not variance?

Recall $E_\theta T = n\theta/(n+1)$ and $E_\theta T^2 = n\theta^2/(n+2)$. The risk of $\delta_a = aT$ is

$$R(\theta, \delta_a) = a^2 E_\theta T^2 - 2 a \theta E_\theta T + \theta^2 = \theta^2 \left(\frac{n}{n+2} a^2 - \frac{2n}{n+1} a + 1\right).$$

This quadratic in $a$ is minimized at $a^\star = (n+2)/(n+1)$, giving $R(\theta, \delta_{a^\star}) = \theta^2/(n+1)^2$. Compare this with the UMVU risk, $\theta^2/[n(n+2)] = \theta^2/(n^2 + 2n)$. Since $(n+1)^2 = n^2 + 2n + 1 > n^2 + 2n$, the biased $\delta_{a^\star}$ has strictly smaller risk.

Pause — what just happened?

The UMVU estimator is, by definition, the best unbiased estimator. A biased estimator with smaller variance can beat it overall. The bias buys us variance reduction, and that's a good trade. The general identity is

$$R(\theta, \delta) = \operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta) + b^2(\theta, \delta), \qquad b(\theta, \delta) = E_\theta\, \delta - g(\theta).$$

Bias contributes quadratically. A little bias hurts only a little. Trading a chunk of variance for a sliver of bias is often a great deal.

This is not a curiosity. The bias-variance trade-off is at the heart of nonparametric curve estimation, ridge regression, the Stein shrinkage phenomenon, and essentially every modern smoothing or regularization technique. Insisting on $b = 0$ leaves a lot of statistical power on the table.

Sometimes the only unbiased estimator is absurd

Example 4.7 — truncated Poisson

Suppose $X$ has mass function $$P_\theta(X = x) = \frac{\theta^x e^{-\theta}}{x!\,(1 - e^{-\theta})}, \quad x = 1, 2, \dots,$$ a Poisson truncated to $\{1, 2, \dots\}$. We want to estimate $g(\theta) = e^{-\theta}$ (the missing mass). The unbiasedness equation forces $$\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{\delta(k)}{k!} \theta^k = 1 - e^{-\theta} = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k!} \theta^k.$$ Matching coefficients term by term gives $\delta(k) = (-1)^{k+1}$. The UMVU estimator of $e^{-\theta} \in (0, 1)$ is therefore $(-1)^{X+1}$: it equals $+1$ when $X$ is odd and $-1$ when $X$ is even.

Common pitfall — "best unbiased" can be ridiculous

The estimator $(-1)^{X+1}$ takes values $\pm 1$ while the parameter lives in $(0, 1)$. It is provably UMVU, and provably absurd. Unbiasedness is not the same as "good". When the unbiasedness constraint is too restrictive, the only estimator satisfying it can be useless. A reasonable biased estimator — for instance, $e^{-\bar X}$ in the un-truncated problem — would crush it.

Keep both observations in mind throughout the rest of the chapter. We will keep developing the unbiased theory, because it is mathematically clean and historically influential, but the takeaway is not "always be unbiased". It is: "unbiasedness gives one well-defined optimum; whether you want that optimum is a separate question".

4.3 Normal One-Sample Problem — Distribution Theory

The most important concrete example in introductory statistics is the i.i.d. sample $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Almost every $t$-test, $F$-test, and confidence interval you've seen depends on knowing the joint distribution of $\bar X$ and $S^2$. Here we derive those facts cleanly, using moment generating functions and the gamma family.

Four facts about a normal random variable

Let $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ and $Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma$. Then:

  1. Standardization: $Z \sim N(0, 1)$, and more generally $aX + b \sim N(a\mu + b, a^2 \sigma^2)$.
  2. MGF of $Z$: $M_Z(u) = e^{u^2/2}$. (Complete the square in $\int e^{uz} e^{-z^2/2}\, dz/\sqrt{2\pi}$.)
  3. MGF of $X$: $M_X(u) = e^{u\mu + u^2 \sigma^2/2}$, since $X = \mu + \sigma Z$.
  4. Sums of independent normals: if $X_i \sim N(\mu_i, \sigma_i^2)$ independent, then $X_1 + X_2 \sim N(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)$, by multiplying MGFs.
Why MGFs do the heavy lifting

Convolution of densities is painful; multiplication of MGFs is trivial. As long as the MGF exists, "the MGF determines the distribution" (Lemma 2.7) means we can identify the distribution of a sum just by recognizing the product. Every distributional result in this section follows that template.

Sample mean and sample variance

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be a random sample from $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$. By Example 2.3 this is a two-parameter full-rank exponential family with complete sufficient statistic $T = \bigl(\sum X_i, \, \sum X_i^2\bigr)$. Define

$$\bar X = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \qquad S^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2.$$

The mapping $T \leftrightarrow (\bar X, S^2)$ is one-to-one (using $\sum X_i^2 = (n-1) S^2 + n \bar X^2$), so $(\bar X, S^2)$ is also complete sufficient.

From the four facts above, summing and rescaling:

$$\bar X \sim N\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right).$$

From Example 3.22 (Basu), $\bar X$ and $S^2$ are independent. So we just need the marginal distribution of $S^2$.

The gamma and chi-square families

The gamma distribution $\Gamma(\alpha, \beta)$ with shape $\alpha > 0$ and scale $\beta > 0$ has density

$$f_{\alpha, \beta}(x) = \frac{x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-x/\beta}}{\beta^\alpha\, \Gamma(\alpha)}, \quad x > 0.$$

Its MGF, valid for $u < 1/\beta$, is $M(u) = (1 - u\beta)^{-\alpha}$. Multiplying MGFs gives the key convolution rule: $$X \sim \Gamma(\alpha_x, \beta), \, Y \sim \Gamma(\alpha_y, \beta) \text{ independent} \implies X + Y \sim \Gamma(\alpha_x + \alpha_y, \beta).$$

Definition 4.8 — chi-square distribution

The chi-square distribution on $p$ degrees of freedom, $\chi^2_p$, is the distribution of $Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_p^2$ where $Z_1, \dots, Z_p$ are i.i.d. $N(0, 1)$.

A short calculation gives the MGF of $Z^2$: $M_{Z^2}(u) = (1 - 2u)^{-1/2}$, which is the MGF of $\Gamma(1/2, 2)$. By the convolution rule, $\chi^2_p = \Gamma(p/2, 2)$ with MGF $(1 - 2u)^{-p/2}$ for $u < 1/2$.

The distribution of $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2$

Set $Z_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma$, all i.i.d. $N(0, 1)$. Let $$V \equiv \frac{(n-1) S^2}{\sigma^2} = \sum_{i=1}^n (Z_i - \bar Z)^2.$$ Expanding the square, $$\sum_{i=1}^n (Z_i - \bar Z)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2 - n \bar Z^2.$$ So $V + n \bar Z^2 = \sum Z_i^2 \sim \chi^2_n$. Now $\sqrt n\, \bar Z \sim N(0, 1)$, so $n \bar Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1$. Independence of $V$ (a function of $S^2$) and $n \bar Z^2$ (a function of $\bar X$) lets us factor the joint MGF: $$M_V(u)\, M_{n \bar Z^2}(u) = (1 - 2u)^{-n/2} \implies M_V(u) = (1 - 2u)^{-(n-1)/2}.$$ That's the MGF of $\chi^2_{n-1}$. So

$$\frac{(n-1) S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}.$$
Pause — what did the proof actually use?

Three ingredients: (i) the convolution rule for gammas, (ii) the independence of $\bar X$ and $S^2$ from Basu's theorem, and (iii) the uniqueness of MGFs. The "degrees of freedom decrement" — $n$ becomes $n - 1$ — comes from algebraically separating out $\bar Z^2$. That single subtraction is the entire intuition behind "we lose a degree of freedom for estimating the mean."

Combined: $\bar X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ and $(n-1) S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$, independent. This pair of facts is the engine that runs the entire normal one-sample theory: $t$-statistics, confidence intervals for $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, the $F$-test for ratios of variances, and the construction of UMVU estimators in the next section.

4.4 Normal One-Sample Problem — Estimation

Now we cash in. Since $(\bar X, S^2)$ is complete sufficient, any unbiased function of it is automatically UMVU by Theorem 4.4. The plan: compute moments of $S$ (using the gamma representation) and put together UMVU estimators of various functions of $(\mu, \sigma^2)$.

Moments of $S$

Since $V = (n-1) S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1} = \Gamma((n-1)/2, 2)$, we have $S^r = \sigma^r V^{r/2}/(n-1)^{r/2}$ and

$$E_\theta S^r = \frac{\sigma^r}{(n-1)^{r/2}} E V^{r/2} = \frac{\sigma^r\, 2^{r/2}\, \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{r + n - 1}{2}\right)}{(n - 1)^{r/2}\, \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right)},$$ valid for $n + r > 1$.

Rearranging, the quantity $$\widehat{\sigma^r} = \frac{(n - 1)^{r/2}\, \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n - 1}{2}\right)}{2^{r/2}\, \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{r + n - 1}{2}\right)}\, S^r$$ is unbiased for $\sigma^r$, and hence UMVU. Special cases worth noting:

  • $r = 2$: $S^2$ itself is UMVU for $\sigma^2$. (The constant collapses to $1$.)
  • $r = 1$: The UMVU estimator of $\sigma$ is not $S$. It is $S$ times a gamma-function correction factor. $S$ is biased downward as an estimator of $\sigma$ (Jensen's inequality applied to $\sqrt{\cdot}$).
Did you ever question this?

Almost every textbook treats $S$ as "the standard deviation". It is a fine estimator, but it is biased — and the bias is non-trivial for small $n$. By Stirling's formula the correction factor is $1 - r(r-2)/(4n) + O(1/n^2)$, so for $r = 1$ the bias is $O(1/n)$ and disappears for large samples. Practitioners ignore it for good reason. But notice the lesson: even in the simplest, most studied model, the "obvious" estimator is biased.

UMVU for $\mu$ and friends

Since $E \bar X = \mu$, the sample mean $\bar X$ is UMVU for $\mu$.

For $\mu^2$: $\bar X^2$ is not unbiased, because $E \bar X^2 = \mu^2 + \sigma^2/n$. Subtracting an unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2/n$ fixes it: $$\widehat{\mu^2} = \bar X^2 - \frac{S^2}{n}, \quad \text{UMVU for } \mu^2.$$

For the signal-to-noise ratio $\mu/\sigma$: use independence. $\bar X$ is UMVU for $\mu$ and depends only on $\bar X$. The UMVU for $1/\sigma$ (the $r = -1$ case above) depends only on $S$. Since $\bar X$ and $S$ are independent, their product is unbiased for $\mu/\sigma$: $$\widehat{\mu/\sigma} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\, \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{n-1}\, \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n-2}{2}\right)}\cdot \frac{\bar X}{S}.$$ This is a rescaled $1/t$-statistic.

UMVU for a normal quantile

The $p$th quantile of $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ is $x_p = \mu + \sigma\, \Phi^{-1}(p)$. Combining the UMVU estimators of $\mu$ and $\sigma$ (which are independent, so we just add):

$$\widehat{x_p} = \bar X + \frac{\sqrt{n - 1}\, \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{2}\, \Gamma(n/2)}\, S\, \Phi^{-1}(p).$$
Pattern recognition

Every formula in this section has the same shape: "the right gamma-function correction times the natural moment estimator". The corrections come from the chi-square distribution of $S^2$. Independence of $\bar X$ and $S$ lets us combine them additively or multiplicatively without worry. This is why the normal model is the gold standard example — every quantity has a closed-form UMVU.

4.5 Variance Bounds and Information

Theorem 4.4 tells us how to build a UMVU when a complete sufficient statistic is available. But what is the smallest variance any unbiased estimator could possibly have? That question — independent of any specific construction — leads to the Cramér–Rao bound and to Fisher information.

The covariance inequality, the engine of the bound

For any random variables $X, Y$ with finite variances, the Cauchy–Schwarz-style inequality

$$\operatorname{Cov}^2(X, Y) \leq \operatorname{Var}(X)\, \operatorname{Var}(Y) \tag{4.11}$$

follows from expanding $E[(X - EX)\sigma_Y \pm (Y - EY)\sigma_X]^2 \geq 0$. Rearranging, $$\operatorname{Var}(X) \geq \frac{\operatorname{Cov}^2(X, Y)}{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}.$$ This looks unhelpful — it bounds $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ in terms of $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ itself via the covariance. The trick is to choose $Y$ cleverly so that $\operatorname{Cov}(\delta, Y)$ is the same for every unbiased $\delta$. Then the right side is a constant, depending only on $g$ and the model.

The likelihood-ratio choice and Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins

Let $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta\}$ have densities $p_\theta$, and suppose $\theta + \Delta \in \Omega$. Define the likelihood ratio $L(x) = p_{\theta + \Delta}(x)/p_\theta(x)$ (taking $L = 1$ where $p_\theta = 0$, under the regularity that $p_{\theta + \Delta}$ also vanishes there). Then for $h$ integrable under $P_{\theta + \Delta}$, $$E_{\theta + \Delta}\, h(X) = \int h\, p_{\theta + \Delta}\, d\mu = \int h\, L\, p_\theta\, d\mu = E_\theta\, L(X)\, h(X).$$ Apply this with $h = 1$ (so $E_\theta L = 1$) and $h = \delta$ (so $E_{\theta + \Delta}\, \delta = E_\theta\, L \delta$). Let $\psi(X) = L(X) - 1$; then $E_\theta\, \psi = 0$ and $$E_\theta\, \psi\, \delta = E_\theta\, L \delta - E_\theta\, \delta = E_{\theta + \Delta}\, \delta - E_\theta\, \delta = g(\theta + \Delta) - g(\theta).$$ That is: $\operatorname{Cov}_\theta(\delta, \psi)$ is the same for every unbiased $\delta$. Substituting into the covariance inequality gives the Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins bound:

$$\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta) \geq \frac{\bigl[g(\theta + \Delta) - g(\theta)\bigr]^2}{E_\theta\bigl(p_{\theta + \Delta}(X)/p_\theta(X) - 1\bigr)^2}. \tag{4.13}$$
Why does this work?

The estimator $\delta$ must "move" the right amount when $\theta$ moves to $\theta + \Delta$ — its mean must shift by exactly $g(\theta + \Delta) - g(\theta)$. The likelihood ratio $L$ is the random object that, on the data side, encodes the change in $\theta$. If small changes in $\theta$ produce big shifts in $L$, then the data are informative and small variance is feasible. If they don't, no estimator can pick up the signal.

Fisher information by taking $\Delta \to 0$

Under regularity that lets us interchange limits and integrals, dividing top and bottom of (4.13) by $\Delta^2$ and letting $\Delta \to 0$ gives $$\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta) \geq \frac{[g'(\theta)]^2}{I(\theta)},$$ where the Fisher information is $$I(\theta) = E_\theta \!\left(\frac{\partial \log p_\theta(X)}{\partial \theta}\right)^{\!2}. \tag{4.14}$$

The function $\partial \log p_\theta/\partial \theta$ is called the score. Two facts about it, under regularity allowing differentiation under the integral:

  1. $E_\theta[\partial \log p_\theta(X)/\partial \theta] = 0$. (Differentiate $\int p_\theta\, d\mu = 1$ in $\theta$.)
  2. $I(\theta) = \operatorname{Var}_\theta(\partial \log p_\theta(X)/\partial \theta) = -E_\theta[\partial^2 \log p_\theta(X)/\partial \theta^2]$. (Differentiate again.) Eq. (4.15)–(4.16).
Three faces of Fisher information

$I(\theta)$ can be computed as:

  • the expected squared score — its definition,
  • the variance of the score — since the score has mean zero,
  • the negative expected curvature of the log-likelihood — usually the easiest to compute.

The third form is why people call $I(\theta)$ "curvature": a sharply peaked log-likelihood means small variance is possible. A flat log-likelihood means the data barely distinguish $\theta$ from neighbouring values.

The Cramér–Rao bound

Theorem 4.9 — Cramér–Rao / information bound

Let $\mathcal{P} = \{P_\theta : \theta \in \Omega\}$ be a dominated family with $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ open, densities $p_\theta$ differentiable in $\theta$. Suppose $\delta$ has mean $g(\theta)$, $E_\theta \delta^2 < \infty$, and the regularity $$g'(\theta) = E_\theta\bigl[\delta(X)\, \psi(X, \theta)\bigr], \qquad \psi(X, \theta) = \frac{\partial \log p_\theta(X)}{\partial \theta}, \tag{4.17}$$ holds. Then $$\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta) \geq \frac{[g'(\theta)]^2}{I(\theta)}, \quad \theta \in \Omega.$$

Common pitfall — regularity matters

Condition (4.17) involves the estimator $\delta$. So in principle some estimators might violate it and have variance below the bound. Under mild conditions (Woodroofe–Simons 1983) the bound still holds for almost every $\theta$, but the literature is full of subtler results. The practical advice: when the model has bounded support that depends on $\theta$ (uniform $(0, \theta)$, shifted exponential, etc.), Cramér–Rao often does not apply. Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins is the safer tool there.

Reparameterization

If $\theta = h(\xi)$ is a smooth one-to-one reparameterization, the chain rule gives $$\tilde I(\xi) = [h'(\xi)]^2\, I(\theta). \tag{4.18}$$ That is, information transforms quadratically under change of variable. A useful sanity check: in any parameterization, $1/I$ scales like the variance of an unbiased estimator should — confirming the bound is a genuine geometric quantity, not a coincidence of coordinates.

Two showcase computations

Example 4.10 — exponential families

For a one-parameter canonical exponential family $p_\eta(x) = \exp[\eta T(x) - A(\eta)] h(x)$, the score is $T - A'(\eta)$, and $$I(\eta) = \operatorname{Var}_\eta(T) = A''(\eta).$$ If we instead use the mean parameterization $\mu = A'(\eta) = E_\eta T$, then (4.18) gives $I(\mu) = 1/\operatorname{Var}_\mu(T)$. Since $T$ is itself UMVU for $\mu$, with variance $\operatorname{Var}_\mu(T)$, the Cramér–Rao bound $1/I(\mu) = \operatorname{Var}_\mu(T)$ is achieved exactly. Exponential families are the canonical examples where the bound is sharp.

Example 4.11 — location families

If $P_\theta$ has density $p_\theta(x) = f(x - \theta)$, a change of variables shows $$I(\theta) = \int \frac{[f'(x)]^2}{f(x)}\, dx,$$ which does not depend on $\theta$. Location families have constant Fisher information — translating the parameter just translates the problem, so the local geometry is the same everywhere.

Information adds under independence

If $X$ and $Y$ are independent with densities $p_\theta$ and $q_\theta$, then the score of the joint density is the sum of the individual scores, and independence makes the cross-term vanish: $$I_{X, Y}(\theta) = I_X(\theta) + I_Y(\theta).$$ Iterating: for a random sample of size $n$, the total information is $n\, I(\theta)$. This is the formal statement of "information accumulates linearly with sample size", and it is the origin of the $\sqrt n$ rate that pervades asymptotic statistics.

Why $1/\sqrt n$?

If unbiased estimators have variance at least $[g'(\theta)]^2/[n I(\theta)]$, then their standard deviation is at least $|g'(\theta)|/\sqrt{n I(\theta)}$ — i.e. $\Theta(1/\sqrt n)$. The whole edifice of $\sqrt n$-asymptotic theory rests on this single inequality.

4.6 Variance Bounds in Higher Dimensions

Everything in the previous section extends to a vector parameter $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^s$, replacing the score by the gradient and the variance by a covariance matrix.

The Fisher information matrix

In regular models, $$I(\theta)_{ij} = E_\theta \!\left[\frac{\partial \log p_\theta(X)}{\partial \theta_i}\, \frac{\partial \log p_\theta(X)}{\partial \theta_j}\right] = \operatorname{Cov}_\theta\!\left(\frac{\partial \log p_\theta}{\partial \theta_i},\, \frac{\partial \log p_\theta}{\partial \theta_j}\right) = -E_\theta\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \log p_\theta(X)}{\partial \theta_i\, \partial \theta_j}\right].$$ In matrix notation, with $\nabla_\theta$ the gradient and $\nabla^2_\theta$ the Hessian, $$I(\theta) = E_\theta\bigl[(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta)(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta)'\bigr] = -E_\theta\, \nabla^2_\theta \log p_\theta(X).$$ The equality between the first two forms uses $E_\theta\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta = 0$, the vector version of the zero-mean-score property.

The multivariate Cramér–Rao bound

For an unbiased estimator $\delta$ of a scalar function $g: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, $$\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\delta) \geq \nabla g(\theta)'\, I^{-1}(\theta)\, \nabla g(\theta).$$ The gradient $\nabla g$ tells us which direction in parameter space the target $g$ is sensitive to; $I^{-1}(\theta)$ is the inverse information matrix in that direction.

Direction matters

If the model has lots of information about $\theta_1$ but little about $\theta_2$, then estimating a function that depends mostly on $\theta_1$ is easy, and one that depends mostly on $\theta_2$ is hard. The quadratic form $\nabla g'\, I^{-1}\, \nabla g$ captures this directional asymmetry. It's the same flavour as $\operatorname{Var}(v' X) = v'\, \operatorname{Cov}(X)\, v$ from Chapter 1: information replaces covariance, and $I^{-1}$ replaces $\operatorname{Cov}$.

Example 4.12 — multivariate exponential families

For an $s$-parameter canonical exponential family $p_\eta(x) = \exp[\eta \cdot T(x) - A(\eta)] h(x)$, $$\frac{\partial^2 \log p_\eta(X)}{\partial \eta_i\, \partial \eta_j} = -\frac{\partial^2 A(\eta)}{\partial \eta_i\, \partial \eta_j},$$ so the Fisher information is just the Hessian of the cumulant function: $$I(\eta) = \nabla^2 A(\eta).$$ Compact, elegant, and one of the most-used identities in all of statistics.

Reparameterization in higher dimensions

Under $\theta = h(\xi)$ with Jacobian $D h(\xi)$ defined by $[Dh]_{ij} = \partial h_i/\partial \xi_j$, the chain rule gives $$\tilde I(\xi) = [Dh(\xi)]'\, I(\theta)\, [Dh(\xi)].$$ This is the matrix analogue of $\tilde I = [h']^2 I$. Once you've memorized "information transforms by sandwiching with the Jacobian", you can switch between any parameterizations the problem suggests without doing the integrals again.

Summary

The whole chapter, condensed:

  1. An estimator is unbiased if $E_\theta\, \delta = g(\theta)$ for every $\theta$. Not every $g$ admits an unbiased estimator — that question (U-estimability) is settled by the form of the family.
  2. If a complete sufficient statistic $T$ exists and $g$ is U-estimable, there is a unique unbiased function of $T$, and it is UMVU (Theorem 4.4). Find it either by solving $E_\theta\, \eta(T) = g(\theta)$ or by Rao–Blackwellizing any unbiased estimator.
  3. UMVU is not always the right goal. A small bias can buy a large variance reduction (bias-variance trade-off), and sometimes the only unbiased estimator is absurd (truncated Poisson).
  4. For an i.i.d. normal sample, $\bar X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ and $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}$, independent. From these come UMVU estimators of every smooth function of $(\mu, \sigma^2)$ — usually equal to the natural moment estimator times a gamma-function correction.
  5. Fisher information $I(\theta) = E[(\partial \log p_\theta/\partial \theta)^2] = -E[\partial^2 \log p_\theta/\partial \theta^2]$ measures how much the data say about $\theta$. Information adds under independence, scales linearly with sample size, and transforms quadratically under reparameterization.
  6. The Cramér–Rao bound: any unbiased estimator of $g(\theta)$ has variance at least $[g'(\theta)]^2/I(\theta)$ (scalar case), or $\nabla g'\, I^{-1}\, \nabla g$ (vector case). In exponential families parameterized by the mean, the bound is achieved exactly.
  7. For models with parameter-dependent support, Cramér–Rao often fails; the Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins inequality (4.13) is the robust alternative.
