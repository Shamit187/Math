Chapter 1

Probability and Measure

The vocabulary every statistics paper assumes you already know — taught from scratch, with no rushing.

Book pages: 1–24 ~30 min reading time Prereqs: calculus, basic probability

Why does statistics need measure theory at all?

Imagine three different statisticians. Alice flips coins and counts heads. Bob measures the time it takes for a light bulb to fail. Carol studies how many emails arrive each minute, except that her sensor only logs whether "at least one" arrived — not how many.

Each of them is doing probability, but the nature of the numbers they record is different:

  • Alice's count is a whole number: $0, 1, 2, \dots$ — call this discrete.
  • Bob's time is any positive real number — call this continuous.
  • Carol's data is sometimes discrete (a count) and sometimes truncated (a "yes/no"). It's mixed.

In an undergraduate course you learn two completely separate sets of formulas: sums for the discrete case, integrals for the continuous case, and the mixed case is just sort of waved away. That works for problem sets, but it stops working the moment you try to write a single theorem that covers all three cases at once.

The promise of measure theory

Measure theory gives us one language — one formula — that handles Alice's sums, Bob's integrals, and Carol's mixed data, all at the same time. It's not about being more rigorous for rigor's sake. It's about not having to write every theorem twice.

Keener says it more bluntly in the chapter opening: this book does not fully develop measure theory. It just teaches you enough to read the notation in modern statistics. Our pace will be slower than the book's, but the destination is the same.

1.1 Measures

The one-line idea

A measure is a rule that assigns a "size" to subsets of some universal set $\mathcal{X}$.

That's it. The whole chapter is built on this one idea. Now we slow down and unpack it.

The universe $\mathcal{X}$

$\mathcal{X}$ is whatever set we're working with. It could be:

  • $\{H, T\}$ — outcomes of a coin flip.
  • $\{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ — counts of emails.
  • $\mathbb{R}$ — the real line, for measurements like temperature or time.
  • $\mathbb{R}^n$ — $n$-dimensional space, for vectors of measurements.

We give it a fancy name ($\mathcal{X}$, "script-X") just to signal that we're treating it abstractly. Don't let the symbol intimidate you — it's just "a set".

The "size" function $\mu$

A measure is written as $\mu$ (Greek "mu"). For each subset $A \subseteq \mathcal{X}$, the measure tells us $\mu(A)$ — a non-negative number we call the size of $A$.

Example 1.1 — counting measure

Let $\mathcal{X} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$ and define $$\mu(A) = \#A = \text{the number of elements in } A.$$ So $\mu(\{1, 2, 5\}) = 3$. This is called counting measure. The "size" of a set is just how many points are in it.

Example 1.2 — Lebesgue measure

Let $\mathcal{X} = \mathbb{R}^n$ and define $$\mu(A) = \int \!\!\cdots\!\! \int_A dx_1 \cdots dx_n.$$ For $n=1$, this is just the length of $A$. For $n=2$, the area. For $n=3$, the volume. This is called Lebesgue measure.

Why two examples matter

These two measures behave very differently:

  • Counting measure assigns mass $1$ to every single point: $\mu(\{x\}) = 1$.
  • Lebesgue measure assigns mass $0$ to every single point: $\mu(\{x\}) = 0$.

So a point can have "no length" but still "count as one item". The same set $\{0\}$ has two different sizes depending on which measure you use. This is the whole reason measure theory exists — to be explicit about which notion of size we're using.

When $\mu(\{x\}) > 0$, we call $x$ an atom of the measure, with mass $\mu(\{x\})$. Counting measure is "all atoms". Lebesgue measure has "no atoms". Real-world distributions are often somewhere in between.

The catch: we can't measure every subset

Here is the first place a math book usually loses people. So we go extra slow.

You'd think we could just define $\mu(A)$ for every subset $A$ of $\mathcal{X}$. For counting measure we can. But for Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$, mathematicians proved (Vitali, 1905) that there exist subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ so weirdly shaped that no consistent "length" can be assigned to them.

These pathological sets can't even be written down explicitly — their existence relies on the Axiom of Choice. But to keep our theory clean, we agree up front: we'll only ask for the measure of subsets that belong to a specific, well-behaved collection. That collection is called a $\sigma$-field.

Definition 1.3 — $\sigma$-field

A collection $\mathcal{A}$ of subsets of $\mathcal{X}$ is a $\sigma$-field (or $\sigma$-algebra) if:

  1. $\mathcal{X} \in \mathcal{A}$ and $\emptyset \in \mathcal{A}$.
  2. If $A \in \mathcal{A}$, then its complement $A^c = \mathcal{X} - A \in \mathcal{A}$.
  3. If $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$, then $\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \in \mathcal{A}$.
What is this saying?

It says: "a $\sigma$-field is any collection of sets that is closed under the things you'd want to do to sets — complementing, and taking countable unions". Everything else (intersections, set differences, etc.) follows from these three rules using De Morgan's laws.

Read it like a contract: if you start with sets I can measure, and you build new sets out of them using complements and countable unions, I promise I can still measure the result.

Pause

The "$\sigma$" in $\sigma$-field comes from the German word for "sum" — it specifically signals that we allow countably infinite unions (not just finite ones). This is the secret weapon: it lets us take limits.

What makes $\mu$ actually a "measure"

Definition 1.4 — measure

A function $\mu$ on a $\sigma$-field $\mathcal{A}$ is a measure if:

  1. $\mu(A) \in [0, \infty]$ for every $A \in \mathcal{A}$. (Sizes are non-negative; infinity allowed.)
  2. For disjoint $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal{A}$, $$\mu\!\left( \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \mu(A_i).$$
In words

Property 2 is called countable additivity. It says: if you break a set into a (countable) bunch of non-overlapping pieces, their sizes add up to the size of the whole. This is the entire content of what "size" should mean.

A consequence: continuity of measures

Suppose $B_1 \subset B_2 \subset \cdots$ is an increasing sequence of measurable sets and let $B = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n$ be their union. Then:

$$\mu(B) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mu(B_n). \tag{1.1}$$
What this is saying

If you nibble up to a set in steps that keep getting bigger, the sizes converge to the size of the whole. It's "continuity": you can take limits inside or outside the measure and get the same answer.

You'll see this used everywhere in proofs to justify "letting $n \to \infty$". Now you know why it's true.

Three pieces of jargon to memorize

  • $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$ — a measurable space. A set plus a $\sigma$-field on it, but no measure chosen yet.
  • $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ — a measure space. Now we've picked a measure.
  • If $\mu(\mathcal{X}) = 1$, then $\mu$ is a probability measure, often written $P$ instead of $\mu$. The triple is a probability space.
The big reveal

A probability is just a measure with $\mu(\mathcal{X}) = 1$. Everything you learned in undergrad probability is special vocabulary for this one special case. We're going to develop the general theory and then collapse it back to probability when needed.

$\sigma$-finite — a technical box we can tick once and forget

A measure is finite if $\mu(\mathcal{X}) < \infty$, and $\sigma$-finite if $\mathcal{X}$ can be broken into countably many pieces, each of finite measure. Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$ has $\mu(\mathbb{R}) = \infty$, but $\mathbb{R} = \bigcup_n [-n, n]$ and each $[-n, n]$ has length $2n < \infty$. So Lebesgue measure is $\sigma$-finite.

Keener says all measures in this book are $\sigma$-finite. Translation: don't worry about this. It's a footnote to ensure later theorems apply.

Borel sets — the $\sigma$-field for $\mathbb{R}^n$

What $\sigma$-field do we use on $\mathbb{R}^n$? Answer: the Borel sets — the smallest $\sigma$-field containing all open rectangles $(a_1, b_1) \times \cdots \times (a_n, b_n)$.

Translation

Start with all the "nice" rectangular boxes. Then add in everything you can build from them by taking complements and countable unions. Everything you'd naturally want to measure — intervals, balls, polygons, curved regions — is a Borel set. The non-Borel sets are the pathological ones we can't write down.

1.2 Integration

The point of this section

We need to integrate functions $f$ against a measure $\mu$. The result is written $$\int f \, d\mu \quad \text{or} \quad \int f(x) \, d\mu(x).$$

This single notation is supposed to cover both sums (when $\mu$ is counting measure) and ordinary integrals (when $\mu$ is Lebesgue measure). Concretely:

Example 1.5 — counting measure

If $\mu$ is counting measure on $\mathcal{X}$, then $$\int f \, d\mu = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} f(x).$$

Example 1.6 — Lebesgue measure

If $\mu$ is Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$, then $$\int f \, d\mu = \int \!\cdots\! \int f(x_1, \dots, x_n) \, dx_1 \dots dx_n.$$

The payoff

Once we've defined $\int f \, d\mu$ abstractly, we never again need separate theorems for "the discrete case" and "the continuous case". One symbol, one proof.

How is the integral actually defined?

Going slow here, because the construction is the cleverest part of measure theory. It happens in three steps.

Step 1 — indicator functions. The indicator $1_A$ of a set $A$ is the function

$$1_A(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & x \in A \\ 0 & x \notin A \end{cases}.$$

We define $$\int 1_A \, d\mu = \mu(A).$$ The integral of an indicator is the measure of the set it indicates. This is the bridge between integration and measure — and it's the only thing we have to define by hand. Everything else follows.

Step 2 — simple functions. A simple function is a finite linear combination of indicators:

$$f = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i \, 1_{A_i}, \qquad a_i \geq 0, \ A_i \in \mathcal{A}.$$

By linearity, we're forced to define

$$\int \left( \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i \, 1_{A_i} \right) d\mu = \sum_{i=1}^{m} a_i \, \mu(A_i).$$

That is: a simple function takes finitely many values; its integral is a weighted sum of those values, with weights equal to the measures of the level sets.

Step 3 — general non-negative functions. A theorem (Theorem 1.8 in the book) says: every non-negative measurable function $f$ is the increasing limit of simple functions $f_1 \leq f_2 \leq \cdots$. We define

$$\int f \, d\mu = \lim_{n \to \infty} \int f_n \, d\mu.$$

The limit is the same regardless of which increasing sequence we picked.

Why Theorem 1.8 isn't just a magic claim

Here is the explicit construction. For each resolution level $n$, partition the y-axis into strips of height $1/2^n$ and set $f_n(x)$ equal to the bottom of whichever strip $f(x)$ falls into (capped at $n$ to handle the infinite case). The result is a step function — finitely many constant levels — which is exactly a simple function. As $n$ grows, the strips get narrower ($1/2^n \to 0$), so $f_n$ creeps up toward $f$ from below. The sequence is increasing and converges to $f$ everywhere. The theorem is just saying this obvious rounding construction always works.

Step 4 — general functions. Split $f$ into its positive and negative parts:

$$f^+(x) = \max\{f(x), 0\}, \qquad f^-(x) = -\min\{f(x), 0\}.$$

Both $f^+$ and $f^-$ are non-negative, and $f = f^+ - f^-$. So we define

$$\int f \, d\mu = \int f^+ \, d\mu - \int f^- \, d\mu.$$

This is well-defined as long as we don't end up with $\infty - \infty$. When $\int |f| \, d\mu < \infty$, we say $f$ is integrable.

Mental picture

Riemann integration (calculus class) chops up the $x$-axis. Lebesgue integration chops up the $y$-axis — slicing the function horizontally by its value, then asking "how much $x$ takes this value?". That "how much" is exactly the measure. This re-slicing is what lets the theory absorb counting, integration, and weird in-between cases all at once.

What is "measurable"?

Definition 1.7

A real-valued function $f$ on $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$ is measurable if for every Borel set $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, $$f^{-1}(B) = \{x \in \mathcal{X} : f(x) \in B\} \in \mathcal{A}.$$

Translation

"Measurable" means the pre-image of every measurable set is measurable. That sounds circular but it isn't — it means $f$ doesn't tangle the structure of $\mathcal{X}$ so badly that we lose the ability to ask measurement questions about it.

Why pre-image and not image? You might expect the condition to say "the image of every measurable set is measurable". But that would be wrong — and here is a clean counterexample. Take $f$ that maps the entire real line to a single point: $f(x) = 0$ for all $x$. This is constant and completely "nice". Now take any crazy non-measurable set $A$. Its image under $f$ is just $\{0\}$, which is perfectly measurable. So requiring the image to be measurable would place no real constraint on $f$ at all.

The pre-image condition is what we actually need because we care about questions we can ask about $f$. A set $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ represents a question: "did $X$ land in $B$?" For that question to have a probability, the set of inputs that map into $B$ — i.e. $f^{-1}(B)$ — must be measurable in $\mathcal{X}$. That is exactly the definition. There is no constraint on what $f$ does to non-measurable sets because we never need to ask about them.

Every continuous function is measurable. Every piecewise-continuous function is measurable. Almost every function you can write down is measurable. Non-measurable functions exist but you can't construct one explicitly. In practice, just treat "measurable" as "fine".

1.3 Events, probabilities, and random variables

Now we restrict to the case $\mu(\mathcal{X}) = 1$ and rename things.

Let $P$ be a probability measure on $(E, \mathcal{B})$. The renaming:

  • Elements $e \in E$ → outcomes.
  • Sets $B \in \mathcal{B}$ → events.
  • $P(B)$ → the probability of event $B$.

A random variable $X$ is a measurable function $X : E \to \mathbb{R}$.

Re-read this slowly

A random variable is not a variable. It is not a number. It is a function — from the space of outcomes to the real numbers. The "randomness" is hidden in the outcome $e$; $X$ just reads off a number from each outcome.

Example — rolling a die

Let $E = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ with $P$ assigning probability $\tfrac{1}{6}$ to each outcome.

  • Define $X(e) = e$ — "the number shown". This is a random variable. $P(X = 3) = \tfrac{1}{6}$.
  • Define $Y(e) = \mathbf{1}\{e \text{ is even}\}$ — "did an even number come up?". Also a random variable. $P(Y = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

Both $X$ and $Y$ are functions defined on the same probability space. The word "variable" in "random variable" is historical accident — think function instead.

The distribution of $X$ is the measure $P_X$ on $\mathbb{R}$ defined by

$$P_X(A) = P\big( \{e \in E : X(e) \in A\} \big) = P(X \in A).$$
What's happening — concrete example

Think of a coin flip. The abstract outcome space is $E = \{\text{heads}, \text{tails}\}$, with $P(\text{heads}) = P(\text{tails}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Define $X(\text{heads}) = 1$ and $X(\text{tails}) = 0$. Then $P_X$ is the measure on $\mathbb{R}$ that assigns probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to the point $1$ and $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to the point $0$. The original $P$ lives on the coin flip space; $P_X$ carries the same information but lives on the number line, where we can do arithmetic with it.

More generally: $P$ lives on the abstract outcome space $E$ — which we usually can't see directly. $P_X$ pushes that probability forward onto $\mathbb{R}$ — where we can compute expectations, CDFs, and densities. We almost always work with $P_X$ and forget $E$ ever existed.

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is

$$F_X(x) = P(X \leq x) = P_X\big( (-\infty, x] \big).$$

We use the notation $X \sim Q$ to mean "$X$ has distribution $Q$", i.e. $P_X = Q$.

1.4 Null sets

A set $N$ is null with respect to $\mu$ if $\mu(N) = 0$.

If a statement holds for all $x$ except possibly in some null set, we say the statement holds almost everywhere (abbreviated a.e.). When $\mu$ is a probability measure, we instead say with probability one.

Unpacking "with probability one"

"With probability one" does not mean "always, without any exception." It means the set of exceptions has probability zero — but exceptions can still exist, they're just unmeasurably rare.

Example: pick a number $X$ uniformly at random from $[0,1]$. The probability that $X$ is irrational equals $1$ — because the rationals have Lebesgue measure zero. Every rational in $[0,1]$ is still a possible outcome, so there are exceptions. The statement "$X$ is irrational" holds with probability one, but not with certainty.

In statistics you will often see: "estimator $T_n$ converges to $\theta$ with probability one." This means there is a null set of sample sequences on which convergence fails — but since that set has probability zero, we ignore it for all practical purposes.

Example

Under Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$, the set of rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ has measure zero. So the function $f(x) = 1$ if $x$ is rational, $0$ otherwise, satisfies $f = 0$ almost everywhere. Therefore $\int f \, d\mu = 0$.

Four facts to internalize:

  1. If $f = 0$ a.e., then $\int f \, d\mu = 0$.
  2. If $f \geq 0$ and $\int f \, d\mu = 0$, then $f = 0$ a.e.
  3. If $f = g$ a.e., then $\int f \, d\mu = \int g \, d\mu$ (when either side exists).
  4. Changing $f$ on a null set does not change its integral.
Why we care

"Almost everywhere" is the integrator's way of saying "for our purposes, identical". Two functions that differ on a null set are interchangeable inside any integral. This is why densities aren't unique — only unique up to a null set.

1.5 Densities

The driving question

Given a distribution $P$, can we represent it as "integration of a function against a simpler measure"? The answer is yes — sometimes — and the function we get is called a density.

Why we want this

Working directly with an abstract measure $P$ is hard. But if we can write $P(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu$ for a familiar reference measure $\mu$ (Lebesgue or counting), then everything reduces to ordinary calculus or summation using the function $f$. Computing probabilities, expectations, and likelihood ratios all become concrete calculations on $f$.

In statistics specifically: a likelihood function is a density. When you write $p(x \mid \theta)$, you are writing a Radon–Nikodym derivative — the density of the data distribution with respect to Lebesgue or counting measure. The entire machinery of maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference is built on being able to work with these densities.

Definition 1.9 — absolute continuity

Let $P$ and $\mu$ be measures on $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$. We say $P$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mu$, written $P \ll \mu$, if $$\mu(A) = 0 \implies P(A) = 0.$$

Translation

$P \ll \mu$ means "$P$ doesn't put mass anywhere that $\mu$ doesn't see". If $\mu$ thinks a set is negligible, $P$ agrees.

Why does it matter? Because of the next theorem — when $P \ll \mu$, you can write $P$ as integration of a function against $\mu$. That function is the density.

Theorem 1.10 — Radon–Nikodym

If $P$ is a finite measure and $P \ll \mu$ (with $\mu$ $\sigma$-finite), then there exists a non-negative measurable function $f$ such that $$P(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu \quad \text{for every } A \in \mathcal{A}.$$

This $f$ is called the density of $P$ with respect to $\mu$, or the Radon–Nikodym derivative, written $$f = \frac{dP}{d\mu}.$$

Why the derivative notation?

Compare it to the fundamental theorem of calculus: $\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)$, where $f = F'$. Here, $f = dP/d\mu$ plays the same role: integrating $f$ against $\mu$ reconstructs $P$. So $f$ is the "derivative of $P$ with respect to $\mu$".

The analogy side by side:

  • FTC: You have a function $F$ (e.g. the CDF). Differentiate to get $f = F'$ (the PDF). Then integrating $f$ recovers $F(b) - F(a)$ — i.e. the probability in an interval.
  • Radon–Nikodym: You have a measure $P$. "Differentiate" with respect to $\mu$ to get $f = dP/d\mu$. Then integrating $f$ against $\mu$ over any set $A$ recovers $P(A)$.

When $\mu$ is Lebesgue measure, $f = dP/d\mu$ is literally the PDF you computed derivatives of in a calculus course: $f(x) = F_X'(x)$. The Radon–Nikodym theorem is the generalization that works even when $P$ has no classical derivative — for instance when $\mu$ is counting measure, in which case $f = dP/d\mu$ is just the mass function.

The two special cases that recover everything you know

Example 1.11 — absolutely continuous random variable

If $X$ has density $p$ with respect to Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$, then $$F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x p(u) \, du.$$ This is the "density" you learned in undergrad. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, $p(x) = F_X'(x)$.

Example 1.12 — discrete random variable

If $X$ takes values in a countable set $\mathcal{X}_0$ and $\mu$ is counting measure on $\mathcal{X}_0$, then $X$ has density $p$ with respect to $\mu$, where $$p(x) = P(X = x).$$ The "density" in the discrete case is just the mass function you've always used. The integral $\int A \, p \, d\mu$ becomes the sum $\sum_{x \in A} p(x)$.

Big takeaway

"PDF" and "PMF" are not two different objects. They are the same object — a Radon–Nikodym derivative — measured against two different background measures (Lebesgue vs. counting). This is the unification we were promised at the start of the chapter.

1.6 Expectation

The expectation of a random variable $X$ is

$$E[X] = \int X \, dP. \tag{1.4}$$

That formula is rarely used directly. The usable forms are:

$$E[X] = \int x \, dP_X(x), \qquad E[f(X)] = \int f \, dP_X.$$

And if $P_X$ has density $p$ with respect to $\mu$, then

$$\int f \, dP_X = \int f \, p \, d\mu.$$
Why this works

Substituting $p \, d\mu$ for $dP_X$ is exactly the change-of-variable trick the derivative notation $p = dP_X/d\mu$ suggests. Specializing:

  • $X$ continuous with density $p$: $\quad E[f(X)] = \int f(x) \, p(x) \, dx.$
  • $X$ discrete with mass function $p$: $\quad E[f(X)] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}_0} f(x) \, p(x).$

Both are special cases of the single formula above.

Linearity and ordering

For random variables $X, Y$ and constants $a, b$:

$$E[aX + bY] = a E[X] + b E[Y].$$

If $X \leq Y$ almost surely and both have finite expectations, then $E[X] \leq E[Y]$.

Variance, covariance, correlation

Define $\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(X - E[X])^2]$. A useful computational form: $$\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2.$$

Covariance between $X$ and $Y$: $$\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])] = E[XY] - E[X] E[Y].$$

Note $\operatorname{Cov}(X, X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$.

Correlation (the scale-invariant version): $$\operatorname{Cor}(X, Y) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X) \operatorname{Var}(Y)}} \in [-1, 1].$$ The endpoints $\pm 1$ are achieved exactly when $Y$ is a perfect linear function of $X$.

Common pitfall

Zero correlation does not imply independence. It only means there's no linear association. $Y = X^2$ with $X \sim N(0,1)$ has $\operatorname{Cor}(X, Y) = 0$ but they are obviously dependent.

1.7 Random vectors

A random vector is a function $X : E \to \mathbb{R}^n$, where each component $X_i$ is a random variable. Everything from the scalar case lifts naturally:

  • Distribution: $P_X(B) = P(X \in B)$ for Borel $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.
  • Density: $P_X$ may have density $p$ with respect to Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$ (continuous case) or counting measure on a countable set (discrete case).
  • Expectation: $E[X]$ is the vector of expectations of the components.
  • For a measurable $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, $E[T(X)] = \int T \, dP_X$.

1.8 Covariance matrices

A random matrix $W$ is a matrix whose entries are random variables. Its expectation $E[W]$ is the matrix of entry-wise expectations.

Useful identities (with $v$ constant vector, $A, B, C$ constant matrices):

$$E[v + AX] = v + A E[X],$$ $$E[A + BWC] = A + B \, E[W] \, C.$$

The covariance matrix of a random vector $X$ with mean $\mu = E[X]$ is $$\operatorname{Cov}(X) = E[(X - \mu)(X - \mu)']$$ where $'$ denotes transpose. Entry-wise: $[\operatorname{Cov}(X)]_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$.

Crucial transformation rule: for constant $v$ and constant matrix $A$, $$\operatorname{Cov}(v + AX) = A \operatorname{Cov}(X) A'. \tag{1.15}$$

Why this matters

The covariance matrix is the analogue of variance for vectors. Equation (1.15) is one of the most-used formulas in all of statistics — it tells you how covariance transforms under linear changes of variable. We'll see it again when we study the general linear model in Chapter 14.

1.9 Product measures and independence

Product measures

Given two measure spaces $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A}, \mu)$ and $(\mathcal{Y}, \mathcal{B}, \nu)$, there is a unique measure $\mu \times \nu$ on the product space such that $$(\mu \times \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A) \, \nu(B).$$

In words

Product measure assigns "area" to rectangles as length × height, and extends uniquely to everything else. Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the product of two Lebesgue measures on $\mathbb{R}$.

Theorem 1.17 — Fubini

If $f \geq 0$ (or if $\int |f| \, d(\mu \times \nu) < \infty$), then $$\int f \, d(\mu \times \nu) = \int \!\left[ \int f(x,y) \, d\nu(y) \right] d\mu(x) = \int \!\left[ \int f(x,y) \, d\mu(x) \right] d\nu(y).$$

Translation: double integrals can be done as iterated single integrals, in either order. Same theorem as in calculus, now stated for any product measure.

Independence

Definition 1.18 — independence

Random vectors $X \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $Y \in \mathbb{R}^m$ are independent if $$P(X \in A, Y \in B) = P(X \in A) \, P(Y \in B) \quad \text{for all Borel } A, B.$$

Equivalent form

Independence is equivalent to: the joint distribution of $(X, Y)$ is the product of the marginals, $$P_{(X,Y)} = P_X \times P_Y.$$ And if $X, Y$ have densities $p_X, p_Y$, the joint density is the product: $$p_{(X,Y)}(x, y) = p_X(x) \, p_Y(y).$$

The "factor the joint density" rule that you used in undergrad is just this fact dressed in symbols. It works in mixed cases too: $X$ continuous, $Y$ discrete — no problem, just take $\mu \times \nu$ to be Lebesgue × counting.

Functions of independent variables are independent: if $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent and $f_1, \dots, f_n$ are measurable, then $f_1(X_1), \dots, f_n(X_n)$ are independent. When $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent and all share the same distribution $Q$, we call them i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed) and refer to the collection as a random sample from $Q$.

1.10 Conditional distributions

Suppose we observe $X = x$. The distribution of $Y$ should change to reflect this new information. The conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x$ is the new probability measure for $Y$.

When $X$ is discrete with mass function $p_X$, the formula is exactly the one from undergrad:

$$Q_x(B) = P(Y \in B \mid X = x) = \frac{P(Y \in B, X = x)}{P(X = x)}. \tag{1.17}$$

For each value $x$ with $p_X(x) > 0$, $Q_x$ is a probability measure on Borel sets.

What if $X$ is continuous?

Then $P(X = x) = 0$ for every $x$, and the formula above is $0/0$. The general definition uses something called a stochastic transition kernel and is technical — Keener defers it to Chapter 6. The good news: the identities we're about to write down hold whether $X$ is discrete or not.

Conditional expectation and the tower property

The conditional expectation of $f(X, Y)$ given $X = x$ is

$$E[f(X, Y) \mid X = x] = \int f(x, y) \, dQ_x(y).$$

Define $H(x) = E[f(X, Y) \mid X = x]$ and write $H(X) = E[f(X, Y) \mid X]$.

The tower property (a.k.a. smoothing, a.k.a. law of total expectation)

$$E[f(X, Y)] = E\big[ E[f(X, Y) \mid X] \big].$$

Special cases worth memorizing:

  • $f(X, Y) = Y$: $\quad E[Y] = E[E[Y \mid X]]$.
  • $f(X, Y) = 1_B$: $\quad P(B) = E[P(B \mid X)]$ — the law of total probability.
Why "tower"?

The conditional expectation $E[Y \mid X]$ is itself a random variable. Taking another expectation over $X$ "averages it out" to recover $E[Y]$. Visualize a tower: compute the average within each $X$-level first, then average across levels. Either order — within-then-across, or all-at-once — yields the same answer.

The tower property also nests:

$$E[Y \mid X] = E\big[\, E[Y \mid X, W] \mid X \,\big]. \tag{1.20}$$

That is: conditioning on less information equals first conditioning on more and then averaging out the extra. This identity gets used constantly in Bayesian computations and martingale theory.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 1

The whole chapter, in eight bullets:

  1. A measure $\mu$ assigns sizes to "nice" subsets of a space $\mathcal{X}$.
  2. The "nice" subsets form a $\sigma$-field — closed under complements and countable unions.
  3. Probability is just a measure with total mass $1$.
  4. A random variable is a measurable function from outcomes to numbers; its distribution is the pushed-forward measure on $\mathbb{R}$.
  5. An integral $\int f \, d\mu$ unifies sums (counting measure) and ordinary integrals (Lebesgue measure).
  6. A density is a Radon–Nikodym derivative: $f = dP/d\mu$. PDFs and PMFs are both densities, just against different background measures.
  7. Expectation is an integral against $P$ or $P_X$; variance, covariance, and correlation are second moments.
  8. Independence means the joint distribution factors as a product. Conditioning is what you do when independence fails — and the tower property $E[Y] = E[E[Y \mid X]]$ is its single most useful consequence.
