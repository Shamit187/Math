Chapter 13

Optimal Tests in Higher Dimensions

When the parameter is multidimensional, the dream of a uniformly most powerful test breaks down. We rescue what we can by restricting to unbiased tests and conditioning away the nuisance.

Book pages: 255–267 ~45 min reading time Prereqs: Chapter 12, exponential families

Why this chapter exists

In Chapter 12 we lived in a clean one-parameter world. The Neyman–Pearson lemma gave us the most powerful test for a simple null against a simple alternative, and the monotone likelihood ratio property extended that to a uniformly most powerful (UMP) test for one-sided hypotheses about a single scalar parameter. Life was good.

Then reality intrudes. Most real testing problems have more than one parameter. The classic example: you want to test whether a normal population has mean zero — but you don't know the variance. The variance $\sigma^2$ is a nuisance parameter. It's not the thing you care about, but it sits in the likelihood and you can't pretend it isn't there.

The core difficulty

As soon as a nuisance parameter enters the picture, no single test is uniformly most powerful. The "best" test changes depending on the unknown value of $\sigma^2$. So the wish for one optimal procedure is too greedy — we need to ask for less.

This chapter does two things at once. First, it backs off from "UMP" to UMP unbiased — we only compete against tests that have the basic decency property of rejecting more often under the alternative than under the null. Second, it shows that for multiparameter exponential families, the UMP unbiased test can be constructed by conditioning on a sufficient statistic for the nuisance parameter. The conditioning kills the nuisance dependence; what's left is a one-parameter problem we already know how to solve.

Two payoffs at the end: the Student t-test and Fisher's exact test fall out of this machinery as special cases. Both are familiar from undergraduate courses, but here we see why they are the right tests.

13.1 Marginal and conditional distributions

The technical groundwork

The chapter opens with a lemma that looks dry but is the engine for everything that follows. The setup: we have two joint distributions $P_0$ and $P_1$ for a pair of random vectors $(Y, T)$. The Radon–Nikodym derivative of $P_1$ with respect to $P_0$ factors:

$$\frac{dP_1}{dP_0}(t, y) = a(y) \, b(t).$$

Notice the structure: $a$ depends only on $y$, $b$ depends only on $t$. The question is: what does this factorization say about the marginal distribution of $T$ and the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $T = t$?

Lemma 13.1

Assume $P_1 \ll P_0$ with $dP_1/dP_0(t, y) = a(y) b(t)$ and $a(y) > 0$. Let $Q_i$ be the marginal of $T$ under $P_i$ and $R_{it}$ the conditional of $Y$ given $T = t$ under $P_i$. Then $Q_1 \ll Q_0$ and $R_{1t} \ll R_{0t}$ (for $Q_1$-a.e. $t$), with

$$\frac{dQ_1}{dQ_0}(t) = b(t) \, E_0\!\left[a(Y) \mid T = t\right]$$

and

$$\frac{dR_{1t}}{dR_{0t}}(y) = \frac{a(y)}{\int a \, dR_{0t}}.$$
What the lemma is actually telling us

Read the two formulas side by side. The marginal density of $T$ picks up the factor $b(t)$ — the part of the likelihood ratio that depends only on $t$ — together with a normalizing integral over $y$. The conditional density of $Y$ given $T = t$ picks up the factor $a(y)$ — the part that depends only on $y$ — divided by the same normalizing integral.

In other words: a multiplicative factorization of the likelihood ratio produces a multiplicative split between the marginal and the conditional. The pieces don't mix.

Why do we need a lemma for this? In an introductory course you'd just write $$p_1(t, y) = p_1(y \mid t) \, p_1(t)$$ and split the joint by hand. The lemma is doing the same job, but for general measures — not necessarily a product of Lebesgue and counting, not necessarily having a clean joint density at all. It works whenever the likelihood ratio factors.

Application to exponential families

Now we use the lemma to dissect a multiparameter exponential family. Suppose $X$ has the canonical density

$$p_{\theta, \eta}(x) = h(x) \exp\!\left[\theta \cdot U(x) + \eta \cdot T(x) - A(\theta, \eta)\right] \tag{13.1}$$

where $\theta$ is $r$-dimensional and $\eta$ is $s$-dimensional. Both $U$ and $T$ are vector-valued sufficient statistics. We can think of $\theta$ as the parameters we care about and $\eta$ as the nuisance.

Theorem 13.2

For the family (13.1), there exist measures $\lambda_\theta$ and $\nu_t$ such that:

  1. With $\theta$ fixed, the marginal distribution of $T$ is an $s$-parameter exponential family with density $$\exp\!\left[\eta \cdot t - A(\theta, \eta)\right]$$ with respect to $\lambda_\theta$.
  2. The conditional distribution of $U$ given $T = t$ is an exponential family with density $$\exp\!\left[\theta \cdot u - A_t(\theta)\right]$$ with respect to $\nu_t$. This density does not depend on $\eta$.

Stop and stare at part 2. The conditional distribution of $U$ given $T = t$ depends on $\theta$ alone. The nuisance parameter $\eta$ has disappeared from the conditional law. This is the structural fact that powers the entire chapter.

Pause — why does conditioning kill the nuisance?

Look at the joint density (13.1). The nuisance parameter $\eta$ couples to $T$, not to $U$. So once we condition on $T = t$, the term $\eta \cdot T(x)$ becomes the constant $\eta \cdot t$ inside the exponent — and a constant in the exponent factors out and gets absorbed by the normalizing constant. What's left depends only on $\theta$.

Said another way: $T$ is sufficient for $\eta$ when $\theta$ is held fixed at any value. Conditioning on a sufficient statistic strips out everything the statistic was sufficient for.

The proof of Theorem 13.2 is a careful application of Lemma 13.1. Pick a reference point $(\theta_0, \eta_0)$ and write the likelihood ratio $p_{\theta, \eta}/p_{\theta_0, \eta_0}$, which factors into a function of $u$ and a function of $t$. Apply the lemma, and the marginal/conditional split falls out in the stated form.

Why this matters operationally

The plan for the rest of the chapter is:

  1. Take a multiparameter testing problem where $\theta$ is the parameter of interest and $\eta$ is nuisance.
  2. Condition on the sufficient statistic $T$ for the nuisance.
  3. The conditional problem only involves $\theta$ — it is a one-parameter testing problem.
  4. Apply Chapter 12's UMP machinery to the conditional problem.
  5. Show that the resulting conditional test is, in fact, UMP unbiased for the original multiparameter problem.

Step 5 is the subtle one. Why is the conditionally optimal test also optimal unconditionally? That's what Section 13.2 establishes.

13.2 UMP unbiased tests in higher dimensions

Unbiased tests and similar tests

Recall the level-$\alpha$ test $\varphi$ is unbiased if its power function $\beta_\varphi(\theta) = E_\theta \varphi$ satisfies $\beta_\varphi \leq \alpha$ on the null $\Omega_0$ and $\beta_\varphi \geq \alpha$ on the alternative $\Omega_1$. Loosely: an unbiased test rejects under the alternative at least as often as it rejects under the null.

When the power function is continuous (and for exponential families it always is), unbiasedness forces $\beta_\varphi = \alpha$ exactly on the boundary $$\omega = \overline{\Omega_0} \cap \overline{\Omega_1}.$$ A test satisfying $\beta_\varphi(\theta) = \alpha$ for all $\theta \in \omega$ is called $\alpha$-similar.

Why similar?

On the boundary $\omega$, the null and the alternative meet. The constraint $\beta_\varphi = \alpha$ on $\omega$ means the test "looks the same" — has the same rejection rate $\alpha$ — at every parameter point on the boundary. Hence "similar".

Operationally, similar tests are easier to characterize than unbiased tests, because the constraint is an equality (not an inequality) and it only applies on the lower-dimensional boundary $\omega$.

Lemma 13.3 — similar implies unbiased

Suppose $\varphi^*$ is $\alpha$-similar with level $\alpha$, and all power functions are continuous. If $\varphi^*$ is uniformly most powerful among $\alpha$-similar tests, then $\varphi^*$ is unbiased and uniformly most powerful among unbiased tests.

The proof is short: the constant test $\varphi \equiv \alpha$ is similar, so $\varphi^*$ has power at least $\alpha$ on $\Omega_1$ (unbiased). Any unbiased competitor has continuous power and so is automatically similar. The UMP-similar property of $\varphi^*$ then beats it.

The strategy in one sentence

"Best unbiased" is hard to attack directly. So we attack the easier problem "best among $\alpha$-similar tests", and Lemma 13.3 tells us we got the right answer anyway.

Neyman structure

Similar tests are still a big class. The next reduction narrows them down dramatically. Suppose $T$ is sufficient for the subfamily $\{P_\theta : \theta \in \omega\}$ on the boundary. Then a similar test $\varphi$ has Neyman structure if $$E_\theta[\varphi \mid T = t] = \alpha, \quad \text{for a.e. } t, \ \forall \theta \in \omega.$$

Theorem 13.5

If $T$ is complete and sufficient for $\{P_\theta : \theta \in \omega\}$, then every similar test has Neyman structure.

The proof rides on the definition of completeness. Let $h(T) = E_\theta[\varphi \mid T]$; sufficiency makes $h$ independent of $\theta \in \omega$. By smoothing, $E_\theta h(T) = \alpha$ for all $\theta \in \omega$, i.e. $E_\theta[h(T) - \alpha] = 0$. Completeness then forces $h(T) = \alpha$ almost surely. That's exactly Neyman structure.

Pause — why is this so useful?

Neyman structure converts a global constraint ("the test has level $\alpha$ on the whole boundary") into a pointwise constraint ("the conditional rejection rate given $T = t$ equals $\alpha$ for almost every $t$").

A pointwise constraint is exactly the kind of constraint we can attack with Neyman–Pearson conditionally. For each fixed value of $T = t$, the conditional test maximizing power subject to $E_\theta[\varphi \mid T = t] = \alpha$ is a one-parameter optimization. And we already know how to do that.

The main theorem: UMP unbiased tests via conditioning

Now we specialize. Suppose $X$ has density

$$p_{\theta, \eta}(x) = h(x) \exp\!\left[\theta U(x) + \eta \cdot T(x) - A(\theta, \eta)\right] \tag{13.2}$$

with $\theta$ univariate (the parameter of interest) and $\eta$ possibly $s$-dimensional (the nuisance). By Theorem 13.2, the conditional distribution of $U$ given $T = t$ is a one-parameter exponential family with canonical parameter $\theta$, free of $\eta$.

This conditional family has the monotone likelihood ratio property, so Chapter 12 (Theorem 12.9) hands us the UMP conditional test for the one-sided problem $H_0: \theta \leq \theta_0$ versus $H_1: \theta > \theta_0$:

$$\varphi_1 = \begin{cases} 1, & U > c(T) \\ \gamma(T), & U = c(T) \\ 0, & U < c(T) \end{cases}$$

with $c(\cdot)$ and $\gamma(\cdot)$ chosen so that $$P_{\theta_0, \eta}\!\left(U > c(t) \mid T = t\right) + \gamma(t) P_{\theta_0, \eta}\!\left(U = c(t) \mid T = t\right) = \alpha.$$

For the two-sided problem $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ versus $H_1: \theta \neq \theta_0$, Chapter 12's Theorem 12.26 gives the UMP unbiased conditional test:

$$\varphi_2 = \begin{cases} 1, & U < c_-(T) \text{ or } U > c_+(T) \\ \gamma_-(T), & U = c_-(T) \\ \gamma_+(T), & U = c_+(T) \\ 0, & c_-(T) < U < c_+(T) \end{cases}$$

with constants chosen so that

$$E_{\theta_0, \eta}[\varphi_2 \mid T = t] = \alpha, \qquad E_{\theta_0, \eta}[\varphi_2 U \mid T = t] = \alpha \, E_{\theta_0, \eta}[U \mid T = t].$$
Theorem 13.6 — the main result

If (13.2) is a full-rank exponential family with $\Omega$ open, then $\varphi_1$ is UMP unbiased for $H_0: \theta \leq \theta_0$ versus $H_1: \theta > \theta_0$, and $\varphi_2$ is UMP unbiased for $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ versus $H_1: \theta \neq \theta_0$.

The proof is the climax of the chapter. The one-sided case goes like this:

  1. The boundary is $\omega = \{\theta = \theta_0\}$, and $T$ is complete and sufficient for $\{P_{\theta_0, \eta} : \eta \in \mathbb{R}^s\}$ — a standard exponential family fact under full-rank conditions.
  2. By construction, $E_{\theta_0, \eta}[\varphi_1 \mid T] = \alpha$. So $\varphi_1$ has Neyman structure, and by smoothing, $\varphi_1$ is $\alpha$-similar.
  3. Any competing similar test $\varphi$ has Neyman structure (Theorem 13.5), so it satisfies $E_{\theta_0, \eta}[\varphi \mid T = t] = \alpha$ — the very constraint that $\varphi_1$ maximizes against.
  4. For $\theta > \theta_0$, the conditional optimality of $\varphi_1$ gives $E_{\theta, \eta}[\varphi_1 \mid T = t] \geq E_{\theta, \eta}[\varphi \mid T = t]$. Smoothing produces the same inequality for the unconditional power.
  5. Verifying level: for $\theta < \theta_0$, the MLR property in the conditional family makes the conditional power non-increasing in $\theta$, so $E_{\theta, \eta} \varphi_1 \leq \alpha$.
  6. So $\varphi_1$ is UMP $\alpha$-similar. By Lemma 13.3, UMP unbiased.

The two-sided case is similar in spirit but uses an extra ingredient: differentiating the power function at $\theta_0$ and setting the derivative to zero (a necessary condition for any unbiased test). That condition becomes $$E_{\theta_0, \eta}[\varphi U \mid T] = \alpha \, E_{\theta_0, \eta}[U \mid T],$$ which is exactly the second constraint defining $\varphi_2$.

Pause — what just happened?

We took a hard multiparameter testing problem and reduced it to a one-parameter problem by conditioning on the sufficient statistic for the nuisance. Each piece of machinery played a role:

  • Exponential family structure (Theorem 13.2) makes the conditional distribution depend on $\theta$ alone.
  • Completeness forces $\alpha$-similar tests to satisfy a pointwise (Neyman-structure) constraint.
  • Pointwise constraint + Chapter 12 machinery picks out the conditional UMP test.
  • Lemma 13.3 upgrades "UMP similar" to "UMP unbiased".

If you remember nothing else from this chapter, remember this chain.

Common pitfall

The conditional level $\alpha$ requirement is the engine, not a side note. Many students try to skip ahead and use unconditional probability to set the critical value. That gives an $\alpha$-level test in the unconditional sense, but it will not be the UMP unbiased test, because it won't have Neyman structure.

The critical value $c(T)$ in $\varphi_1$ is a function of the data $T$. It varies from sample to sample. That's exactly why the test is "conditional".

13.3 Examples: t-test and Fisher's exact test

Example 13.7 — the t-test as a conditional test

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be a random sample from $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$. We want to test $H_0: \mu \leq 0$ versus $H_1: \mu > 0$. The variance $\sigma^2$ is unknown — the classical nuisance parameter.

Writing the joint density in exponential-family form:

$$\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}} \exp\!\left[\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2} U(x) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} T(x) - \frac{n \mu^2}{2 \sigma^2} - n \log \sigma\right]$$

with $U(x) = x_1 + \cdots + x_n$ and $T(x) = x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2$. The canonical parameters are $$\theta = \frac{\mu}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \eta = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}.$$ Notice that the hypothesis $\mu \leq 0$ translates, since $\sigma^2 > 0$, into the single canonical condition $\theta \leq 0$. So we're in the framework of Theorem 13.6 with $\theta$ univariate and $\eta$ scalar nuisance.

The plan

Reject $H_0$ when $U > c(T)$, with $c(\cdot)$ chosen so that $$P_{\mu = 0}\!\left[U > c(t) \mid T = t\right] = \alpha.$$ All we need is the conditional distribution of $U$ given $T = t$ when $\mu = 0$.

This conditional distribution is derived elegantly using Basu's theorem. When $\mu = 0$, write $Z_i = X_i / \sigma$, so $Z = (Z_1, \dots, Z_n) \sim N(0, I)$. Then $\|X\|^2 = T$ and $$W = \frac{X}{\|X\|} = \frac{Z}{\|Z\|}.$$ The unit vector $W$ has a distribution that doesn't depend on $\sigma$ — it is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere $S^{n-1}$ (by orthogonal invariance of the standard normal). So $W$ is ancillary. Meanwhile $T = \|X\|^2$ is a complete sufficient statistic for the family $\{N(0, \sigma^2)^n : \sigma^2 > 0\}$. Basu's theorem then says $T \perp W$.

Because $X = W \sqrt{T}$ and $T \perp W$, $$E\!\left[h(X) \mid T = t\right] = E\!\left[h(W \sqrt{t})\right].$$ So conditionally on $T = t$, the vector $X$ is just $W \sqrt{t}$ with $W$ uniform on the sphere. Since $U = \mathbf{1}^\prime X = \sqrt{t} \cdot \mathbf{1}^\prime W$, $$P_{\mu = 0}\!\left[U > c(t) \mid T = t\right] = P\!\left(\mathbf{1}^\prime W > c(t)/\sqrt{t}\right).$$ This conditional probability equals $\alpha$ when $c(t)/\sqrt{t} = q$, the upper $\alpha$-quantile of $\mathbf{1}^\prime W$. The UMP unbiased test rejects when $$\frac{U}{\sqrt{T}} \geq q.$$

Recognize the test

The statistic $U/\sqrt{T}$ doesn't look familiar yet. But a tiny algebraic manipulation turns it into the t-statistic.

Recall $\bar{X} = U/n$ and the sample variance satisfies $$S^2 = \frac{T}{n-1} - \frac{U^2}{n(n-1)}.$$ Then $$t = \frac{\bar{X}}{S/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{U/\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{(T - U^2/n)/(n-1)}} = \frac{\sqrt{n-1} \cdot \operatorname{Sign}(U)}{\sqrt{nT/U^2 - 1}} = g\!\left(\frac{U}{\sqrt{T}}\right)$$ for a strictly increasing function $g$. So $U/\sqrt{T} > q$ if and only if $t > g(q)$. Under $\mu = 0$, the statistic $t$ has the Student t-distribution on $n - 1$ degrees of freedom, so we take $g(q) = t_{\alpha, n-1}$.

The conclusion

The UMP unbiased level-$\alpha$ test of $H_0: \mu \leq 0$ versus $H_1: \mu > 0$ rejects when $$t = \frac{\bar{X}}{S/\sqrt{n}} > t_{\alpha, n-1}.$$ This is exactly the textbook one-sample t-test. The two-sided version, rejecting when $|t| > t_{\alpha/2, n-1}$, is the UMP unbiased test of $H_0: \mu = 0$ versus $H_1: \mu \neq 0$.

Pause — why the t-test was the right answer all along

In a first course, the t-test is introduced as "the natural thing to do when $\sigma$ is unknown — just plug in $S$ for $\sigma$ in the $z$-statistic". This rationale leaves you with a nagging question: is this really the best test, or just a reasonable test?

The answer, given by Theorem 13.6 plus Basu's theorem: yes, it is the best. Among all unbiased level-$\alpha$ tests, the t-test has the highest power at every alternative. The "plug in $S$" rule isn't a heuristic — it's a consequence of conditioning on the sufficient statistic for the nuisance.

Example 13.8 — Fisher's exact test

The second showcase is a discrete example: testing independence in a $2 \times 2$ contingency table. Two yes/no questions $A$ and $B$ are asked of $n$ respondents. With $p_{ij} = P(X = i, Y = j)$ for $i, j \in \{1, 2\}$, the counts $N_{ij} = \#\{k : X_k = i, Y_k = j\}$ are multinomial. We want to test independence: $p_{ij} = p_{i+} p_{+j}$ for all $i, j$.

After noting that "positive dependence" (whether defined via covariance or via $P(X = 2 \mid Y = 2) > P(X = 2)$) is equivalent to the odds-ratio condition $p_{11} p_{22} > p_{12} p_{21}$, we re-parametrize. Take

$$U = N_{11}, \qquad T_1 = N_{11} + N_{12}, \qquad T_2 = N_{11} + N_{21}.$$

The values $T_1$ and $T_2$ are the row and column marginal sums. A bit of algebra rewrites the multinomial joint as a two-parameter canonical exponential family in $(U, T_1, T_2)$:

$$P(T = t, U = u) = h(u, t) \exp\!\left\{\theta u + \eta \cdot t - A(\theta, \eta)\right\}$$

with $$\theta = \log \frac{p_{11} p_{22}}{p_{12} p_{21}}, \qquad \eta = \begin{pmatrix} \log(p_{12}/p_{22}) \\ \log(p_{21}/p_{22}) \end{pmatrix}.$$ The parameter $\theta$ is the log odds-ratio — exactly the thing we want to test. The two-component $\eta$ is nuisance, capturing the marginal frequencies.

Independence is the statement $\theta = 0$. We're in the setup of Theorem 13.6 with $T = (T_1, T_2)$ as the nuisance sufficient statistic.

The conditional distribution

Under $\theta = 0$, we may take $p_{ij} = 1/4$ for all $i, j$ (because the conditional distribution doesn't depend on the value of $\eta$, only on $\theta$). Then $X_k$ and $Y_k$ are independent, the marginals $T_1$ and $T_2$ are independent binomials with success probability $1/2$, and a calculation gives $$P_0(U = u \mid T = t) = \frac{\binom{t_1}{u} \binom{n - t_1}{t_2 - u}}{\binom{n}{t_2}}.$$

This is the hypergeometric distribution: the number of red balls in a sample of size $t_2$ drawn without replacement from an urn with $t_1$ red and $n - t_1$ white balls.

The UMP unbiased test of $\theta \leq 0$ versus $\theta > 0$ rejects when $U > c(T)$, with $c(\cdot)$ and a randomization $\gamma(\cdot)$ chosen so that the conditional rejection probability equals $\alpha$ under the hypergeometric distribution. This is Fisher's exact test.

Conditioning on the margins

Notice what conditioning on $T$ means in plain terms: we condition on the row sums $T_1$ and column sums $T_2$. Given those marginal totals, the only remaining degree of freedom in the $2 \times 2$ table is $U = N_{11}$ — once you know $N_{11}$ and the margins, you can fill in the other three cells.

So the optimal test for independence treats the margins as ancillary and looks at how surprising the observed $N_{11}$ is under the hypergeometric reference distribution. That is precisely Fisher's recipe, and we've now derived it from optimality principles rather than from intuition alone.

Common pitfall

Fisher's exact test conditions on both sets of marginal totals being fixed. People sometimes wonder whether this is statistically justified — after all, in many real studies only one margin is genuinely fixed by design. The chapter's answer is firm: conditioning on $T$ is forced by the requirement that the test be UMP unbiased, because $T = (T_1, T_2)$ is the complete sufficient statistic for the nuisance parameter $\eta$.

In other words: the conditioning isn't a modeling choice or an approximation. It is what makes the test optimal.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 13

The chapter, in seven bullets:

  1. With more than one parameter, UMP tests usually do not exist. The remedy is to restrict competition to unbiased tests.
  2. For a multiparameter exponential family, the marginal of the nuisance statistic and the conditional distribution of the parameter-of-interest statistic factor cleanly. The conditional law depends only on the parameter of interest — the nuisance $\eta$ drops out.
  3. Lemma 13.3 says "UMP among $\alpha$-similar tests" implies "UMP among unbiased tests". This lets us substitute an easier optimization problem.
  4. When the nuisance sufficient statistic $T$ is complete, similar tests have Neyman structure: a pointwise conditional constraint $E[\varphi \mid T = t] = \alpha$.
  5. The pointwise constraint reduces to a one-parameter conditional problem we already know how to solve via Chapter 12 Neyman–Pearson and MLR machinery.
  6. The one-sample t-test is the UMP unbiased test of $H_0: \mu = 0$ in a normal sample with unknown variance. It falls out by conditioning on $\sum X_i^2$ and using Basu's theorem.
  7. Fisher's exact test is the UMP unbiased test of independence in a $2 \times 2$ contingency table. Conditioning on the marginal totals turns the test into a hypergeometric calculation.
