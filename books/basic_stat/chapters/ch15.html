Chapter 15

Bayesian Inference: Modeling and Computation

Chapter 7 told us what a posterior is. This chapter is about how to actually build one, and — when the integrals refuse to close — how to simulate it.

Book pages: 301–318 ~55 min reading time Prereqs: Ch 7 (Bayes basics), Ch 11 (empirical Bayes)

Why a second Bayesian chapter?

Chapter 7 introduced the Bayesian setup: a prior $\lambda(\theta)$, a model $p_\theta(x)$, and a posterior $\lambda(\theta \mid x) \propto \lambda(\theta) p_\theta(x)$. In that chapter we always managed to evaluate the posterior in closed form — by choosing conjugate priors that made the algebra clean.

Real research is not so cooperative. Two new problems force themselves on us as soon as we leave the textbook examples:

  • The prior is hard to pin down. A single $\lambda$ rarely captures everything we know. Often it is more honest to say "the prior depends on a tuning parameter $\tau$, and $\tau$ itself is uncertain." That leads us to hierarchical models.
  • The posterior is hard to evaluate. Once the prior gets richer or the parameter space gets bigger, the normalizing integral $\int \lambda(\theta) p_\theta(x)\, d\theta$ is generally analytically intractable and numerically expensive. That forces us to simulate the posterior using Markov chains.
The big shift from Chapter 7

Chapter 7 was about algebra: the posterior was a formula you could write down. Chapter 15 is about computation: the posterior is a distribution you can only sample from. Everything new in this chapter — Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs, mixing, convergence diagnostics — exists because we can no longer compute. We can only approximate.

Keener is sober about what this means: we trade one set of worries (does my prior really reflect my belief?) for another (has my sampler actually converged?). Both are real. The whole chapter is the trade-off in slow motion.

15.1 Hierarchical models

The one-line idea

A hierarchical model writes the prior in stages: instead of one prior $\lambda$, we use a family $\{\Lambda_\tau : \tau \in \mathcal{T}\}$ indexed by a parameter $\tau$, and put a prior on $\tau$ itself.

Definition — hierarchical Bayesian model

Let $\{\Lambda_\tau : \tau \in \mathcal{T}\}$ be a family of prior distributions for an unknown parameter $\Theta$. Treat the index $\tau$ as a realization of a random variable $T$ with its own distribution $G$. The model is then $$T \sim G, \qquad \Theta \mid T = \tau \sim \Lambda_\tau, \qquad X \mid T = \tau, \Theta = \theta \sim P_\theta.$$ $T$ is called a hyperparameter, and $G$ is the hyperprior.

Why bother with two layers? Because the marginal prior for $\Theta$ — found by integrating $T$ out — $$P(\Theta \in B) = \int \Lambda_\tau(B) \, dG(\tau)$$ is a mixture of the $\Lambda_\tau$. Mixtures are far more flexible than any single family, and they let us express uncertainty about the prior itself.

Prior elicitation: three flavors

Before we go further, a vocabulary check. Priors come in three rough flavors:

  • Informative priors encode substantive knowledge — e.g., "we know from previous experiments that the rate $\theta$ is around $0.3$." Hierarchical priors are usually informative-but-cautious: the hyperprior on $\tau$ admits we don't know exactly how informative to be.
  • Non-informative priors try to express ignorance. The flat prior $\lambda(\theta) \propto 1$ is the naive choice, but flat priors are not invariant under reparameterization, which is awkward.
  • Jeffreys priors fix the invariance problem. Jeffreys's rule is $\lambda(\theta) \propto \sqrt{\det I(\theta)}$, where $I$ is the Fisher information. By construction this is invariant under smooth reparameterizations, so it is the same prior whether you parameterize a binomial by $p$ or by $\log[p/(1-p)]$.
Why Jeffreys's rule is "non-informative"

Fisher information measures how sharply the likelihood discriminates between nearby values of $\theta$. Jeffreys's prior weights $\theta$-values in proportion to how distinguishable they are — which is the closest thing we have to "uniform on the natural geometry of the model." For $N(\theta, 1)$ it gives a flat prior on $\theta$. For $\mathrm{Binomial}(n, p)$ it gives the $\mathrm{Beta}(1/2, 1/2)$ prior on $p$, not flat.

Common pitfall — improper priors

Many non-informative priors (including most Jeffreys priors on unbounded parameters) are improper: $\int \lambda(\theta) \, d\theta = \infty$. That is fine if the posterior is still proper — $\int \lambda(\theta) p_\theta(x)\, d\theta < \infty$. But it is easy to construct hierarchical models where the prior is improper and the posterior is also improper, and MCMC will happily sample from the nonsense and look perfectly well-behaved. Always check that the posterior integrates.

Example 15.1 — compound estimation, hierarchically

Worked example — Normal means with unknown shrinkage

Recall the compound-estimation problem from Chapter 11: we have $n$ parameters $\Theta_1, \dots, \Theta_n$ and observe $X_i = \Theta_i + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 1)$ independent. The empirical Bayes story put $\Theta_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0, \tau^2)$ and estimated $\tau$ from the data.

The hierarchical Bayesian version is honest about $\tau$ being unknown. We write $$T \sim G, \qquad \Theta \mid T = \tau \sim N(0, \tau^2 I_n), \qquad X \mid \Theta = \theta \sim N(\theta, I_n).$$ Conditional on $T = \tau$, the $X_i$ are i.i.d. $N(0, 1 + \tau^2)$. So the marginal likelihood for $\tau$ is one-dimensional and easy. The Bayes estimator for $\Theta$ collapses, via the tower property, to $$\delta(X) = E[\Theta \mid X] = X \cdot E\!\left[\frac{T^2}{1+T^2} \,\Big|\, X\right].$$ The shrinkage factor $T^2/(1+T^2)$ is itself averaged over the posterior of $T$ — automatically accounting for our uncertainty about how much to shrink.

Compare this to the plug-in empirical Bayes estimator, which uses a point estimate $\hat\tau$. The hierarchical Bayes estimator integrates over the whole posterior of $T$ instead. When $n$ is small, this difference matters: empirical Bayes understates uncertainty.

Example 15.2 — ridge regression as a hierarchical model

Worked example — Bayesian ridge

In the linear model $Y \mid B = \beta \sim N(X\beta, \sigma^2 I)$ with $\sigma^2$ known, put a prior $B \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)$. The posterior is $$B \mid Y = y \sim N\!\left((X'X + \kappa I)^{-1} X'Y, \; \sigma^2 (X'X + \kappa I)^{-1}\right),$$ where $\kappa = \sigma^2 / \tau^2$. The posterior mean $(X'X + \kappa I)^{-1} X'Y$ is the ridge regression estimator! Ridge — originally introduced for numerical stability — is exactly Bayesian inference under a Gaussian prior. A hierarchical extension promotes $\tau$ to a random variable with its own hyperprior $G$.

The 8-schools model — the prototypical multilevel example

No introduction to hierarchical Bayes is complete without the 8-schools example (Rubin, 1981). Eight schools ran a coaching program for the SAT. School $i$ reports an estimated effect $y_i$ with known standard error $\sigma_i$. We model $$y_i \mid \theta_i \sim N(\theta_i, \sigma_i^2), \qquad \theta_i \mid \mu, \tau \sim N(\mu, \tau^2), \qquad \mu \sim \text{flat}, \quad \tau \sim \text{half-Cauchy}(0, 5).$$ The hyperparameter $\tau$ controls how much the schools' true effects can vary. If $\tau = 0$, all schools share the same effect $\mu$ (complete pooling). If $\tau \to \infty$, each school is on its own (no pooling). The posterior on $\tau$ tells us how much partial pooling is supported by the data.

Why this example is famous

The 8-schools dataset cleanly demonstrates shrinkage: estimates with large standard errors get pulled toward the global mean $\mu$, while precise estimates are left alone. The strength of pooling is learned from the data via $\tau$. The example is so canonical because the posterior of $\tau$ is notoriously bimodal-prone and tricky — it forces every MCMC method to prove itself.

The hierarchical beta-binomial

Worked example — rats and tumors

$K$ groups, group $k$ has $n_k$ trials and $y_k$ successes. We model $$y_k \mid p_k \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n_k, p_k), \qquad p_k \mid \alpha, \beta \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \beta),$$ and put a vague hyperprior on $(\alpha, \beta)$, e.g. $(\alpha + \beta)^{-5/2}$ on $\log(\alpha/\beta), \log(\alpha + \beta)$. Conditional on $\alpha, \beta$, each $p_k$ has a $\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha + y_k, \beta + n_k - y_k)$ posterior. The hyperposterior on $(\alpha, \beta)$ adjusts how strongly we pool $p_1, \dots, p_K$ toward a common mean $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$.

15.2 Bayesian robustness

Conjugate priors give clean formulas. But what if the conjugate prior is wrong — only a convenient approximation? Keener walks through a sobering example.

Take $X \mid \Theta = \theta \sim N(\theta, 1)$. Suppose the true prior $\Lambda$ is a $t$ with 3 degrees of freedom: $$\lambda(\theta) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}\pi \left(1 + \theta^2/3\right)^2}.$$ The conjugate alternative $\Lambda_N = N(0, 5/4)$ has densities visually almost identical to $\lambda$; the sup-norm distance is just $\|\lambda - \lambda_N\|_\infty = 0.0333$. By total-variation distance, $|\Lambda(B) - \Lambda_N(B)| \leq 7.1\%$ for every Borel $B$.

Yet the Bayes estimators behave very differently in the tails. Under $\Lambda_N$, the Bayes rule is $\delta_N(x) = 5x/9$ — a linear shrinkage. Its risk grows quadratically in $\theta$: $$R(\theta, \delta_N) = \frac{25 + 16\theta^2}{81}.$$ Under the true $t$-prior, the Bayes rule $\delta$ satisfies $\delta(x) = x - 4/x + o(1/x)$ as $|x| \to \infty$. So $\delta$ stops shrinking in the tails (its risk approaches $1$, the UMVU risk), while $\delta_N$ keeps shrinking and pays a quadratic price.

The integrated risk under the true $\Lambda$ is $0.482$ for $\delta$ and $0.901$ for $\delta_N$ — nearly twice as bad. A prior that looked correct to within $3\%$ in density produced an estimator that was nearly $90\%$ worse.

Common pitfall — light-tailed conjugate priors

Gaussian priors have light tails. If the truth has heavier tails, the Gaussian prior is overconfident about how small $\theta$ is. The estimator will shrink large signals too aggressively. A robust workaround is to use a heavy-tailed prior like a $t$ on a few degrees of freedom, accepting that closed-form posteriors are lost and that you will need MCMC.

Pause

The lesson is not "Bayesian inference is fragile." The lesson is that tail behavior of the prior matters far more than tail behavior of the data. Two priors can agree to $3\%$ everywhere and yet produce estimators that diverge as $|x| \to \infty$. Re-read Figure 15.3 in the book if this still feels surprising.

15.3 Markov chains

Both Metropolis–Hastings and Gibbs sampling work by constructing a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior. We need just enough Markov chain theory to know what "stationary" and "converges" mean.

Definition 15.4 — Markov chain

A sequence $X_0, X_1, X_2, \dots$ on a state space $(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{A})$ is a (time homogeneous) Markov chain with transition kernel $Q$ if for every $n \geq 1$, $B \in \mathcal{A}$, and almost every history $x_0, \dots, x_n$, $$P(X_{n+1} \in B \mid X_0 = x_0, \dots, X_n = x_n) = P(X_{n+1} \in B \mid X_n = x_n) = Q_{x_n}(B).$$

The chain has no memory beyond its current state. Given the present, the past tells you nothing about the future. The whole dynamics are encoded in $Q$ — for each state $x$, $Q_x$ is a probability distribution over "where you go next."

Pushing distributions forward

If $\pi_n$ is the distribution of $X_n$, then the distribution of $X_{n+1}$ is $$\pi_{n+1}(B) = \int Q_x(B) \, d\pi_n(x) \;\overset{\text{def}}{=}\; (\pi_n Q)(B).$$ That is, $\pi_{n+1} = \pi_n Q$. Iterating, $\pi_n = \pi_0 Q^n$.

Definition — stationary distribution

A distribution $\pi$ is stationary for $Q$ if $\pi = \pi Q$. If the chain starts at $\pi$, it stays at $\pi$ forever.

The whole strategy of MCMC is: design $Q$ so that the posterior is stationary, then run the chain. Under mild conditions (irreducibility and aperiodicity, plus some recurrence) the chain forgets its initial distribution and the marginal $\pi_n$ converges to the stationary $\pi$. Concretely, for any nice $f$, $$\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n f(X_i) \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \int f \, d\pi.$$ This is the ergodic theorem — the Markov-chain version of the law of large numbers. It is what makes simulated chains usable for estimating posterior expectations.

The finite case as intuition

When $\mathcal{X} = \{1, \dots, m\}$ is finite, the kernel is a transition matrix $Q$ with row sums one. Distributions are row vectors and $\pi_{n+1} = \pi_n Q$ becomes ordinary matrix multiplication. Perron–Frobenius theory then gives: if $Q$ is irreducible and aperiodic, there is a unique left eigenvector $\pi$ with eigenvalue $1$, all other eigenvalues are strictly inside the unit disk, and $Q^n \to \mathbf{1} \pi$ exponentially fast. The rate is controlled by the second-largest eigenvalue modulus — the closer it is to $1$, the slower the convergence. This is the finite-state version of "mixing."

Pause — MCMC converges to the posterior, but how long do we wait?

The theory promises convergence in the limit. In practice we run a chain for some finite $N$ iterations and hope it has reached stationarity. Two questions immediately appear:

  • Burn-in. The first samples reflect the initial state, not the posterior. We discard a prefix. How long? No general answer — usually 10–50% of the chain.
  • Mixing. Even after burn-in, consecutive samples are correlated. We need enough samples that the effective sample size $N_{\text{eff}} = N / (1 + 2 \sum_k \rho_k)$ is large, where $\rho_k$ is the lag-$k$ autocorrelation.

A chain with autocorrelation $0.99$ at lag 1 needs roughly $200{,}000$ iterations to give the same information as $1{,}000$ independent draws. We are always running on borrowed time.

15.4 Metropolis–Hastings algorithm

The setup

We have a target density $f$ (the posterior) on a state space $\mathcal{X}$, known up to a normalizing constant. We want a Markov chain whose stationary distribution has density $f$.

Pick a jump kernel $J$ — for each $x_0$, $J_{x_0}$ is a proposal distribution with density $j_{x_0}$. You are free to choose $J$ however you like, as long as it is easy to sample from and connects every relevant region of the space.

The algorithm

Metropolis–Hastings step

Given current state $X_n = x_0$:

  1. Propose $X^* \sim J_{x_0}$.
  2. Compute the acceptance ratio $$r(x_0, x^*) = \frac{f(x^*) / j_{x_0}(x^*)}{f(x_0) / j_{x^*}(x_0)}.$$
  3. Accept the proposal with probability $\min\{r(x_0, x^*), 1\}$. If accepted, $X_{n+1} = x^*$; otherwise $X_{n+1} = x_0$ (stay in place).
Why the formula has that shape

The ratio $r$ is constructed so the chain satisfies detailed balance with $f$: $$f(x_0) \, q(x_0, x^*) = f(x^*) \, q(x^*, x_0),$$ where $q(x, y)$ is the actual transition density (proposal times acceptance). Detailed balance implies stationarity: integrate both sides against any test set, and you get $\pi Q = \pi$.

Crucially, $r$ depends on $f$ only through ratios $f(x^*)/f(x_0)$. The normalizing constant cancels. This is what makes the algorithm useful: we never need to know $\int \lambda(\theta) p_\theta(x)\, d\theta$.

Why $\pi$ is stationary (the slow proof)

Let $\pi$ be the target with density $f$. We want to show that if $X_0 \sim \pi$, then $X_1 \sim \pi$. By the tower property, $$P(X_1 \in A) = \int Q_{x_0}(A) \, d\pi(x_0),$$ where, expanding the algorithm step, $$Q_{x_0}(A) = \mathbf{1}_A(x_0) + \int_A \min\{r(x_0, x^*), 1\}\, j_{x_0}(x^*)\, d\mu(x^*) - \mathbf{1}_A(x_0) \int \min\{r(x_0, x^*), 1\}\, j_{x_0}(x^*)\, d\mu(x^*).$$ Plugging in and simplifying, the key step is showing $$\int\!\!\int \min\{r(x_0, x^*), 1\} \, \mathbf{1}_A(x^*) f(x_0) j_{x_0}(x^*)\, d\mu(x_0) d\mu(x^*) = \int\!\!\int \min\{r(x_0, x^*), 1\} \, \mathbf{1}_A(x_0) f(x_0) j_{x_0}(x^*)\, d\mu(x_0) d\mu(x^*).$$ Using the explicit formula for $r$, the integrand on both sides becomes $\min\{f(x^*) j_{x^*}(x_0), \, f(x_0) j_{x_0}(x^*)\}$ — symmetric in $(x_0, x^*)$. By Fubini, the two sides are equal, so $\pi$ is stationary.

Tuning the proposal

A "random walk" Metropolis uses $J_x = N(x, \tau^2 I)$ for a step size $\tau$. The art is choosing $\tau$:

  • $\tau$ too small: the chain accepts almost every move, but each move is tiny. It explores slowly.
  • $\tau$ too large: most proposals land in low-density regions and are rejected. The chain sits still.
  • Just right: the acceptance rate sits near $0.234$ for high-dimensional Gaussian targets (a famous result of Roberts, Gelman, and Gilks). For one-dimensional targets, around $0.44$ is the sweet spot.
Common pitfall — poor mixing

If you see a trace plot where the chain wanders in one region for thousands of iterations before suddenly jumping to a different region, your proposal is too narrow for the target's geometry. The chain will look "stationary" within each region but fail to explore the rest of the posterior. This is the classic failure mode: an MCMC run that converges to the wrong distribution because it has not yet discovered the rest.

15.5 Gibbs sampler

The idea

The Gibbs sampler is a special case of Metropolis–Hastings where the proposal is always accepted. It works when the target distribution's conditional distributions are easier to sample from than the joint.

Gibbs sampler (two-variable case)

Let $(X, Y) \sim \pi$. Let $R_y$ be the conditional distribution of $X$ given $Y = y$, and $\tilde R_x$ the conditional of $Y$ given $X = x$. Starting from $(X_0, Y_0) = (x_0, y_0)$:

  1. Draw $X_1 \sim R_{y_0}$ (sample $X$ given the current $Y$).
  2. Draw $Y_1 \sim \tilde R_{x_1}$ (sample $Y$ given the new $X$).
  3. Repeat.

The chain $(X_i, Y_i)$ has $\pi$ as its stationary distribution.

For more than two blocks, just cycle through them: update each variable in turn, conditioning on the current values of all the others. The order can be fixed or random.

Why Gibbs is so useful

The big payoff is dimension reduction. To find the joint density from an unnormalized $g$, you would have to compute $$\int\!\!\int g(x, y) \, dx \, dy$$ — a high-dimensional integral. For the Gibbs sampler you only need $$\int g(x, y) \, dx \quad \text{and} \quad \int g(x, y) \, dy$$ to normalize the one-dimensional conditionals. Univariate integrals are dramatically easier.

Stationarity of Gibbs

Let $\pi_X, \pi_Y$ denote the marginals. By the tower property, $$P((X, Y) \in A) = \int\!\!\int \mathbf{1}_A(x, y)\, dR_y(x)\, d\pi_Y(y). \tag{15.5}$$ Reversing the roles, $$P((X, Y) \in A) = \int\!\!\int \mathbf{1}_A(x, y) \, d\tilde R_x(y)\, d\pi_X(x). \tag{15.6}$$ Now start the chain at $\pi$. Since $Y_0 \sim \pi_Y$ and $X_1 \mid Y_0 \sim R_{Y_0}$, $$P((X_1, Y_0) \in A) = \int\!\!\int \mathbf{1}_A(x, y)\, dR_y(x)\, d\pi_Y(y),$$ which matches (15.5), so $(X_1, Y_0) \sim \pi$ and therefore $X_1 \sim \pi_X$. Then $Y_1 \mid X_1 \sim \tilde R_{X_1}$ and the analogous computation against (15.6) gives $(X_1, Y_1) \sim \pi$. Stationary.

Worked example — Gibbs for a hierarchical normal model

Worked example — Gibbs for the 8-schools model

Recall the model: $$y_i \mid \theta_i \sim N(\theta_i, \sigma_i^2), \quad \theta_i \mid \mu, \tau \sim N(\mu, \tau^2), \quad \mu \sim N(0, 10^4), \quad \tau^2 \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}(a, b).$$ We want the joint posterior of $(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_8, \mu, \tau^2)$. Each full conditional is a known distribution:

  1. $\theta_i \mid \text{rest} \sim N\!\left( \dfrac{y_i / \sigma_i^2 + \mu / \tau^2}{1/\sigma_i^2 + 1/\tau^2},\; \dfrac{1}{1/\sigma_i^2 + 1/\tau^2}\right)$ — a precision-weighted average of the data and the group mean.
  2. $\mu \mid \text{rest} \sim N\!\left(\dfrac{\sum \theta_i / \tau^2}{8/\tau^2 + 1/10^4}, \; \dfrac{1}{8/\tau^2 + 1/10^4}\right)$.
  3. $\tau^2 \mid \text{rest} \sim \text{Inv-Gamma}\!\left(a + 4, \; b + \tfrac{1}{2}\sum (\theta_i - \mu)^2\right)$.

Each step is a closed-form draw. We cycle through (1), (2), (3) repeatedly. After burn-in, the samples approximate the joint posterior. From them we can estimate $E[\theta_i \mid y]$, posterior intervals, and the distribution of $\tau$ — which tells us how much pooling the data supports.

Gibbs as "MH that always accepts"

If you compute the Metropolis–Hastings acceptance ratio when the proposal is the full conditional, $r = 1$ identically. So Gibbs is Metropolis–Hastings with a free lunch — no rejected moves. The price is that you must be able to sample from each full conditional. When conditionals are not standard, you fall back to within-Gibbs Metropolis or specialized samplers (HMC, slice sampling).

15.6 Image restoration — Gibbs on a Markov random field

Gibbs sampling was introduced to statistics by Geman and Geman (1984) for Bayesian image restoration. The setup is striking: every pixel is a parameter, every parameter has a prior, the prior connects neighboring pixels, and Gibbs cycles through all of them.

The model

Pixels live on a rectangular grid $\mathcal{T} = \{1, \dots, m\} \times \{1, \dots, n\}$. The unknown greyscale image is $\Theta = (\Theta_z)_{z \in \mathcal{T}}$. The observed data is $$X_z \mid \Theta = \theta \;\stackrel{\text{ind}}{\sim}\; N(\theta_z, \sigma^2), \quad z \in \mathcal{T}.$$ The prior should encode that nearby pixels are correlated. Define an edge as a pair of pixels $\{z_1, z_2\}$ with $\|z_1 - z_2\| = 1$, and let $\mathcal{E}$ be the edge set. The prior is $$\lambda(\theta) \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{a}{2} \sum_z \theta_z^2 + b \sum_{\{z_1, z_2\} \in \mathcal{E}} \theta_{z_1} \theta_{z_2}\right], \tag{15.8}$$ with $|b| < a/4$ for integrability. This is a Gaussian Markov random field.

The Markov random field property

Fix the values of $\theta$ at the four neighbors of pixel $z$. The prior density, viewed as a function of $\theta_z$ alone, factors away from everything outside the neighborhood. So $\Theta_z$ is conditionally independent of all non-neighbors given its neighbors. This is the spatial analog of the Markov property in time. Image-processing folks call the prior a "Gibbs distribution," which is where the sampler got its name.

The trick — checkerboard updates

Partition the grid into "odd" and "even" pixels: $$\mathcal{T}_1 = \{(i,j) : i+j \text{ odd}\}, \qquad \mathcal{T}_2 = \{(i,j) : i+j \text{ even}\}.$$ Every neighbor of a pixel in $\mathcal{T}_1$ lives in $\mathcal{T}_2$, and vice versa. Conditional on $\mathcal{T}_1$, the values on $\mathcal{T}_2$ are mutually independent. So you can update all of $\mathcal{T}_2$ in parallel, then all of $\mathcal{T}_1$, alternating.

With $\tau = 1/\sigma^2$, the posterior conditional for $\theta_z$ given the rest is normal: $$\Theta_z \mid \text{neighbors}, X = x \sim N\!\left(\frac{\tau x_z + b S_z}{a + \tau}, \; \frac{1}{a + \tau}\right), \tag{15.12}$$ where $S_z = \sum_{\tilde z \in N_z} \theta_{\tilde z}$ is the sum of neighboring pixel values. Each pixel gets a precision-weighted average of its data $x_z$ and the average of its neighbors. Smoother priors (larger $b$, $\rho$ closer to $1$) shift more weight onto the neighbors.

Pause — over- and under-smoothing

In Keener's numerical example (Figure 15.4), three priors are compared on a noisy image of the letter A: $\rho = 0.70$ (under-smoothed), $\rho = 0.8565$ (matched to the empirical correlation), and $\rho = 0.95$ (over-smoothed). The under-smoothed prior actually gives the lowest MSE — a useful lesson that "match the prior to the truth" is not always optimal under squared-error loss. The matched prior produces visually nice results; the over-smoothed prior blurs the letter.

Posterior diagnostics & model comparison

Keener's text concentrates on the algorithms. In practice, no MCMC run is complete without diagnostics. A short field guide:

Trace plots

Plot each parameter against iteration. A healthy trace plot looks like a hairy caterpillar: rapidly oscillating around a stable mean with no trend. Slow drift, sudden jumps between regions, or sticky flat patches all indicate trouble.

Running multiple chains

Start several chains from over-dispersed initial values. Run them all to (apparent) stationarity. If they end up exploring different distributions, you have not converged. The Gelman–Rubin $\hat R$ statistic compares between-chain to within-chain variance: $$\hat R = \sqrt{\frac{\hat V}{W}}, \qquad \hat V = \frac{n-1}{n} W + \frac{1}{n} B,$$ where $W$ is mean within-chain variance and $B$ is between-chain variance. We want $\hat R < 1.01$.

Effective sample size

$N_{\text{eff}} = N / (1 + 2 \sum_{k=1}^\infty \rho_k)$, with $\rho_k$ the lag-$k$ autocorrelation. Always report $N_{\text{eff}}$ alongside the raw $N$. A chain with $N = 10^5$ and $N_{\text{eff}} = 80$ is barely better than 80 independent draws.

Model comparison

Bayes factors compare two models $M_1, M_2$ by the ratio of their marginal likelihoods $\int p(y \mid \theta_i, M_i) \lambda(\theta_i \mid M_i)\, d\theta_i$. Computing marginal likelihoods is much harder than computing posteriors — MCMC samples from $p(\theta \mid y)$ do not directly give $p(y)$. Workarounds include bridge sampling, nested sampling, and posterior predictive checks. For out-of-sample prediction, modern practice often prefers WAIC or LOO-CV, which estimate expected log predictive density from a single MCMC run.

Common pitfall — Bayes factors hate improper priors

Bayes factors involve the normalizing constants of the priors. If your prior is improper, those constants are undefined and the Bayes factor is meaningless — even when the individual posteriors are perfectly well-defined. This is a serious sticking point for "objective Bayesian" model comparison and one reason WAIC/LOO have become more popular.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 15

The chapter in nine bullets:

  1. A hierarchical model writes the prior in stages: $T \sim G$, $\Theta \mid T \sim \Lambda_T$. The marginal prior is then the mixture $\int \Lambda_\tau \, dG(\tau)$.
  2. Informative, non-informative, and Jeffreys priors encode different stances toward prior knowledge. Jeffreys's prior $\propto \sqrt{\det I(\theta)}$ is reparameterization-invariant.
  3. Bayesian robustness: tail behavior of the prior matters far more than its bulk. Two priors agreeing to $3\%$ in density can disagree by a factor of $2$ in integrated risk.
  4. A Markov chain $X_0, X_1, \dots$ has a transition kernel $Q$; distributions evolve by $\pi_{n+1} = \pi_n Q$. A distribution $\pi$ is stationary if $\pi = \pi Q$.
  5. For irreducible aperiodic chains, $\pi_n \to \pi$ and the ergodic theorem replaces the law of large numbers: time averages converge to space averages.
  6. Metropolis–Hastings turns any proposal $J$ into a chain with target $f$ by accepting moves with probability $\min\{r(x_0, x^*), 1\}$, where $r$ uses only ratios of $f$ — so normalizing constants cancel.
  7. The Gibbs sampler cycles through full conditionals, accepting every proposal. It exchanges multi-dimensional integration for repeated one-dimensional sampling.
  8. Posterior diagnostics — trace plots, $\hat R$, effective sample size — are not optional. The chain promises only asymptotic convergence; we must check the finite-sample reality.
  9. Failure modes to watch for: poor mixing, multimodality the chain cannot cross, improper posteriors from improper priors, and Bayes factors that depend on prior normalizing constants you forgot to specify.
