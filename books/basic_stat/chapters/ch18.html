Chapter 18

Nonparametric Regression

Estimating an unknown curve from noisy data — without ever assuming a formula for it.

Book pages: 367–390 ~45 min reading time Prereqs: linear regression, basic functional analysis

Why does the chapter exist?

Up to this point in the book, almost every statistical model came with a formula attached. Linear regression assumed $f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$. Quadratic regression added a $\beta_2 x^2$ term. Logistic regression hard-coded a logit link. In each case, you had to commit to a functional form before you ever looked at the data.

That commitment is a useful starting point with small samples — when there are only twenty data points, there is not enough information to support anything fancier than a low-degree polynomial. But suppose you have thousands of points. Then the constraint "must be quadratic" or "must be linear in $\log x$" starts to feel arbitrary. The data has plenty to say about the shape of the curve, and we should let it speak.

The shift in attitude

Parametric regression asks: given that $f$ has this form, what are the parameter values? Nonparametric regression asks: given only that $f$ is smooth, what curve fits the data? The "parameter" is no longer a finite vector — it is the entire function $f$, an object living in an infinite-dimensional space.

Keener treats two ways of carrying this out. Kernel methods (Section 18.1) build $\hat f$ by averaging nearby $Y$-values, with a tunable window controlling how much averaging happens. Smoothing splines (Section 18.3) instead pose an optimization problem: among all reasonably smooth functions, find the one that fits the data well but is not too wiggly. To set up splines properly we need a quick excursion into Hilbert spaces, which lives in Section 18.2. And in Section 18.4 the same ideas get bent into a tool for density estimation.

Throughout, one theme will dominate: the bias–variance tradeoff. Whatever knob we provide — a bandwidth, a smoothing parameter, a number of basis functions — turning it one way produces a smoother estimator with lower variance but higher bias; turning it the other way produces a wigglier estimator with lower bias but higher variance. Choosing the knob well is the whole art.

18.1 Kernel methods

The setup, slowly

We observe pairs $(x_i, Y_i)$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$, related by $$Y_i = f(x_i) + \epsilon_i,$$ with errors $\epsilon_i$ that are mean zero, uncorrelated, and share a common variance $\sigma^2$. The design points $x_1 < x_2 < \cdots < x_n$ are treated as known constants; only $Y_i$ is random. We assume $f$ is twice continuously differentiable, but nothing more — in particular, no functional form.

The goal is to estimate the curve $f$ at any point $x$ we care about.

A naive first try: linear interpolation

Here is the simplest possible idea. Just connect the data points with line segments. Define $$\hat h(x) = Y_i \, \frac{x - x_{i+1}}{x_i - x_{i+1}} + Y_{i+1} \, \frac{x - x_i}{x_{i+1} - x_i}, \qquad x \in [x_i, x_{i+1}].$$

This is the piecewise-linear interpolant. It hits every data point exactly. If the noise level were zero, it would do a respectable job. But because each $Y_i$ carries an error $\epsilon_i$, $\hat h$ inherits all that noise — it jumps up and down with every observation, never averaging out anything. It has essentially no bias and enormous variance. Useless as an estimate of a smooth $f$.

Why we need smoothing

The whole point of replication and smoothing in regression is to use neighboring observations to cancel out noise. $\hat h$ never averages anything, so it never gets to use that cancellation. To get a better estimator we need to smear $\hat h$ out — to replace its value at $x$ with a weighted average of its values in a neighborhood of $x$.

Convolution: the smoothing operation

The mathematical way to "smear out" a function is convolution against a kernel. Pick a probability density $W$ that is symmetric around zero and supported on $[-1, 1]$ — a small bump centered at the origin. Pick a bandwidth $b > 0$. Define $$\hat f(x) = \frac{1}{b} \int \hat h(t) \, W\!\left( \frac{x - t}{b} \right) dt.$$

Read this slowly. The factor $W((x-t)/b)/b$ is a bump centered at $x$ with width $b$. It assigns weight to values $t$ near $x$ and zero weight to values far from $x$. So $\hat f(x)$ is a weighted average of $\hat h(t)$ for $t$ within distance $b$ of $x$.

A cleaner way to see this: if $Z$ is a random variable with density $W$, then $$\hat f(x) = \mathbb{E}_Z \, \hat h(x - bZ).$$ We are averaging $\hat h$ over a small random shift of its argument. The size of the shift is controlled by $b$.

Two popular kernels

Both are symmetric, continuous, and supported on $[-1, 1]$:

  • Triangular: $W_2(x) = (1 - |x|)_+$. The "+" subscript means "set to zero outside the support".
  • Epanechnikov: $W_4(x) = \tfrac{3}{4}(1 - x^2)_+$. Slightly more efficient theoretically.

In practice the choice of kernel barely matters. The choice of $b$ matters enormously.

The Nadaraya–Watson view

It will help to rewrite the estimator as an explicit linear combination of the responses. Since $\hat h(x) = \sum_i u_i(x) Y_i$ with tent-function weights $u_i$, plugging into the convolution gives $$\hat f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n v_i(x) \, Y_i, \qquad v_i(x) = \frac{1}{b} \int u_i(t) \, W\!\left(\frac{x-t}{b}\right) dt.$$

This is the form to keep in mind: $\hat f(x)$ is a linear function of $Y$. Computing its mean and variance is now an exercise in handling these weights. The closely related Nadaraya–Watson estimator $\hat f_{NW}(x) = \frac{\sum_i K_b(x - x_i) Y_i}{\sum_i K_b(x - x_i)}$ uses the same averaging idea more directly; both fall under the umbrella of "kernel regression".

The bias of $\hat f(x)$

Take expectations. Because the operation is linear and $E Y_i = f(x_i)$, $$E \hat f(x) = \frac{1}{b} \int E\hat h(t) \, W\!\left(\frac{x-t}{b}\right) dt.$$ Now $E \hat h(t)$ is itself just the linear interpolant of the deterministic function $f$, which differs from $f(t)$ by at most $M_2 / (8 n^2)$ where $M_2 = \sup |f''|$ — a Taylor-expansion bound (the book's Eq. 18.5). For large $n$, that piece is tiny.

The interesting piece is the convolution of $f$ with the kernel. Writing $Z \sim W$, expanding by Taylor: $$E_Z f(x - bZ) = f(x) - b f'(x) E Z + \tfrac{1}{2} b^2 f''(x) E Z^2 + \cdots$$ Because $W$ is symmetric, $E Z = 0$, and the leading correction is the $b^2$ term. After collecting: $$\text{Bias}(\hat f(x)) = E\hat f(x) - f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} b^2 f''(x) \, E Z^2 + o(b^2) + O(1/n^2).$$

The shape of the bias

Bias scales with $b^2$ — quadratically in the bandwidth. And it is proportional to $f''(x)$, the local curvature of the true function. Where $f$ is straight, the bias is zero (a weighted average of points on a line is still on the line). Where $f$ bends sharply, smoothing produces a systematic distortion: averaging across a peak pulls the estimate down; averaging across a valley pulls it up. The flatter you are, the more you can smooth for free.

The variance of $\hat f(x)$

Since $\hat f(x) = \sum_i v_i(x) Y_i$ and the $Y_i$ are uncorrelated with variance $\sigma^2$, $$\mathrm{Var}(\hat f(x)) = \sigma^2 \sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2(x).$$

How many of the $v_i$ are nonzero? Only those with $|x_i - x| \lesssim b$, which on a uniform grid is about $nb$ points. Each such $v_i$ is roughly $\frac{1}{nb} W\!\left(\frac{x - x_i}{b}\right)$. So the sum is a Riemann approximation to an integral, and as $b \to 0$ with $nb \to \infty$, $$\mathrm{Var}(\hat f(x)) \;\approx\; \frac{\sigma^2}{nb} \int W^2(t) \, dt.$$

The shape of the variance

Variance scales like $1/(nb)$. Doubling the bandwidth roughly halves the variance: you are averaging twice as many points, so noise cancels twice as well. Increasing $n$ at fixed $b$ also reduces variance for the same reason.

The bias–variance tradeoff, made arithmetic

Combining, the mean squared error at a single point is $$\mathrm{MSE}(x) \approx \frac{\sigma^2 \int W^2}{nb} + \tfrac{1}{4} b^4 \big(f''(x)\big)^2 \big(EZ^2\big)^2.$$ The first term is variance; it decreases in $b$. The second is squared bias; it increases in $b$. Their sum has a minimum somewhere in between.

Averaging over $x \in [0, 1]$ to get the integrated mean squared error: $$\mathrm{IMSE} \approx \frac{c_1}{nb} + b^4 c_2,$$ where $c_1 = \sigma^2 \int W^2$ depends on the noise and the kernel, and $c_2 = \tfrac{1}{4}(EZ^2)^2 \int (f'')^2$ depends on the kernel and the local curvature of the true function. Setting the derivative to zero gives $$b_{\mathrm{opt}} = \left( \frac{c_1}{4 n c_2} \right)^{1/5}.$$

And with this choice, $$\mathrm{IMSE} \approx K_f \left( \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \right)^{4/5} \cdot \big(\text{kernel-dependent constant}\big).$$ The rate is $n^{-4/5}$, slower than the parametric $n^{-1}$ rate. This is the price of refusing to commit to a functional form.

Worked example — a simulated noisy curve

Take $f(x) = 16 x^3 - 19.2 x^2 + 4.68 x + 2.3$ on $[0, 1]$ with i.i.d. $N(0, 1)$ errors at $n = 25$ equally spaced design points (this is Keener's example). Try three bandwidths with the triangular kernel:

  • $b = 0.05$: very narrow. $\hat f$ tracks every noise spike — high variance, low bias.
  • $b = 0.24$: middling. Roughly matches $b_{\mathrm{opt}}$ for this setup. The estimate looks like a smooth version of the truth.
  • $b = 0.6$: very wide. $\hat f$ becomes nearly constant — almost all variance gone, but the wiggles of $f$ are ironed flat. Pure bias.

Plot all three on the same axes against the truth. The middle one wins. The narrow one is too jumpy; the wide one is too flat. The visible "smoothness" of $\hat f$ is precisely the bandwidth knob, and the bias–variance picture above is the analytic shadow of what your eye sees.

Bandwidth selection in practice

The formula for $b_{\mathrm{opt}}$ is gorgeous but useless directly — it depends on $\sigma^2$ and on $\int (f'')^2$, neither of which we know. Two strategies:

Plug-in. Estimate $\sigma^2$ and $\int (f'')^2$ from a pilot fit, then substitute into the formula. Workable but fragile, because $f''$ is harder to estimate than $f$ itself.

Cross-validation. Estimate the prediction error directly by holding out data. The expected squared prediction error at a fresh point is $$E[Y - \hat f(x)]^2 = E[f(x) + \epsilon - \hat f(x)]^2 = \sigma^2 + \mathrm{MSE}(x).$$ Up to the irreducible $\sigma^2$ that depends on no bandwidth, this is what we want to minimize.

A simple procedure: randomly split the data into an estimation half and a validation half, fit $\hat f^*_b$ on the estimation half, score it on the validation half, repeat several times, average, and choose the $b$ that minimizes the averaged score. Or use leave-one-out: fit $\hat f^{(-i)}_b$ omitting the $i$th point, compute $\sum_i (Y_i - \hat f^{(-i)}_b(x_i))^2$, minimize over $b$.

Pause — why cross-validation works

Why is leave-one-out unbiased for prediction error? Because $Y_i$ was not used to build $\hat f^{(-i)}_b$, so $Y_i - \hat f^{(-i)}_b(x_i)$ has the same distribution as the error at a genuinely new point. Squaring and averaging estimates the expected squared prediction error. The bandwidth that minimizes this estimate is automatically close to the bandwidth that minimizes the true risk.

Note: cross-validation does not estimate $\sigma^2 + \mathrm{IMSE}$ for one fixed dataset — it estimates the average over datasets. But for choosing among bandwidths that distinction does not matter: the optimum is the same.

Common pitfall — boundary effects

Near the endpoints of the design region, the kernel "wants" to average over points on both sides of $x$, but on one side there are no points. The estimator becomes effectively one-sided, and its bias is suddenly $O(b)$ rather than $O(b^2)$ — a much worse rate. The bias formula derived above breaks down within distance $b$ of an endpoint.

Fixes include: using boundary kernels (asymmetric kernels designed for the edge), reflecting the data across the boundary, or — most cleanly — using local polynomial regression, which adapts automatically because the local linear fit handles edges correctly. In Keener's Problem 18.2 you derive local quadratic regression: instead of a weighted average, fit a weighted least-squares quadratic $\beta_1 + \beta_2(x_i - x) + \beta_3(x_i - x)^2$ around the target point $x$, and take $\hat f(x) = \hat\beta_1$.

Common pitfall — curse of dimensionality

Everything in this section was one-dimensional. What if $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ for $d \geq 2$? A kernel regression in $d$ dimensions has bandwidth $b$ in each direction, so the effective neighborhood contains roughly $n b^d$ points. To keep that number large you need $b$ large; to keep the bias small you need $b$ small. The two demands fight harder as $d$ grows.

The optimal rate becomes $n^{-4/(4 + d)}$. At $d = 1$, this is the $n^{-4/5}$ rate from above. At $d = 5$, it is $n^{-4/9}$ — barely better than the variance going to zero at all. At $d = 10$ you are essentially not learning. This is the curse of dimensionality: nonparametric methods that work beautifully in 1D break down rapidly as $d$ grows. High-dimensional problems require structural assumptions (additivity, sparsity, low-rank, etc.) to make progress.

18.2 Hilbert spaces

To set up the spline approach we need to treat $f$ itself as a "vector" in an infinite-dimensional space. That requires a little bit of functional analysis. We are going slow, because every concept here pays back directly in Section 18.3.

Norms, inner products, completeness

Definition 18.1 — norm

A norm on a real vector space $V$ is a function $\| \cdot \| : V \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying:

  1. $\|x\| \geq 0$, with $\|x\| = 0$ only when $x = \mathbf{0}$.
  2. Triangle inequality: $\|x + y\| \leq \|x\| + \|y\|$.
  3. Homogeneity: $\|cx\| = |c| \cdot \|x\|$ for any scalar $c$.

A norm gives a notion of distance ($\|x - y\|$) and therefore a notion of convergence: $x_n \to x$ means $\|x_n - x\| \to 0$.

Definition 18.2 — inner product

An inner product on $V$ is a function $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ that is symmetric, bilinear, and positive definite ($\langle x, x \rangle \geq 0$ with equality only at zero). Every inner product induces a norm via $\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x \rangle}$, and that norm automatically satisfies the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\langle x, y \rangle| \leq \|x\| \cdot \|y\|$.

Translation

An inner product is the abstract version of the dot product. It gives you angles, orthogonality, and projections in addition to lengths. Many natural function spaces — like the space of square-integrable functions on $[0, 1]$, with inner product $\int_0^1 f(x) g(x) dx$ — are inner product spaces.

The completeness issue

A sequence $x_n$ is called Cauchy if $\|x_n - x_m\| \to 0$ as $n, m \to \infty$ — the terms bunch up. Every convergent sequence is Cauchy. The converse can fail in "incomplete" spaces.

A standard cautionary example

The rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ with the usual distance are incomplete: the sequence $3, 3.1, 3.14, 3.141, \dots$ is Cauchy but its limit $\pi$ is not in $\mathbb{Q}$. The fix is to expand $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ by adjoining all limits of Cauchy sequences. The same expansion exists for any normed space and produces its completion.

Definition 18.4 — Hilbert space

A Hilbert space is a complete inner product space — meaning every Cauchy sequence has a limit inside the space.

Completeness is what lets us take infinite expansions $x = \sum_k c_k e_k$ and trust that the limit exists. Without it, the spline argument in Section 18.3 would collapse.

Projections

The most important theorem we will use:

Theorem 18.5 — projection onto a closed subspace

Let $V$ be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space $H$. For any $x \in H$ there is a unique $y \in V$ minimizing $\|x - z\|$ over $z \in V$. This $y$ is characterized by $x - y \in V^\perp$ — the residual is orthogonal to the subspace.

Why this generalizes least squares

In finite-dimensional least squares, you minimize $\|Y - X\beta\|^2$ and the answer is determined by the normal equations: residual perpendicular to the column space. Theorem 18.5 is the infinite-dimensional version of that same statement. We will use it to decompose any candidate function $f$ into a "part that matters for the spline problem" plus an orthogonal "part that adds roughness without improving fit" — and conclude the optimal $f$ has the orthogonal part equal to zero.

Orthonormal bases

An orthonormal collection $\{e_t\}$ has $\|e_t\| = 1$ and $\langle e_s, e_t \rangle = 0$ for $s \neq t$. It is a basis if no nonzero element of $H$ is orthogonal to all of them. Every Hilbert space has an orthonormal basis (by Zorn's lemma in general; by Gram–Schmidt when separable). Once you have one, every element expands: $$x = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \langle x, e_k \rangle e_k.$$ The proof tracks partial sums, uses the Pythagorean relation to show they form a Cauchy sequence (this is Bessel's inequality $\sum_k \langle x, e_k \rangle^2 \leq \|x\|^2$), and invokes completeness to obtain the limit.

18.3 Splines

The penalized least squares idea

Back to nonparametric regression. The kernel approach smooths a rough interpolant. The spline approach takes a different angle: it asks for the function that fits the data well while remaining not too wiggly, with both criteria expressed in a single objective.

Concretely, for $x_i \in [0, 1]$ and an integer $m \geq 1$, choose $\hat f$ to minimize $$J(f) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big( Y_i - f(x_i) \big)^2 + \lambda \, \big\| f^{(m)} \big\|_2^2,$$ where $\|g\|_2^2 = \int_0^1 g^2$ is the squared $L^2$ norm. The first sum is residual sum of squares — the fit term. The second is a roughness penalty: the integrated squared $m$th derivative. The constant $\lambda \geq 0$ is the smoothing parameter.

The two extremes of $\lambda$

  • $\lambda = 0$: no penalty. $\hat f$ can interpolate the data exactly — and will, oscillating wildly to do so.
  • $\lambda \to \infty$: the penalty dominates. $\|f^{(m)}\|_2 = 0$ forces $f^{(m)} \equiv 0$, meaning $\hat f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $m - 1$. Pure parametric.

In between, $\lambda$ trades off fit against smoothness exactly as bandwidth $b$ did for kernels. Large $\lambda$ = more smoothing = lower variance, higher bias. Small $\lambda$ = less smoothing = higher variance, lower bias. Cross-validation is the standard way to pick $\lambda$.

The Sobolev space

For the penalty to make sense, $f^{(m)}$ has to exist in some integral sense. The right home for $f$ is the Sobolev space $W_m[0, 1]$: functions with $m - 1$ continuous derivatives and an absolutely continuous $(m-1)$st derivative whose derivative-a.e. is square-integrable. Equipped with the inner product $$\langle f, g \rangle = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} f^{(k)}(0) \, g^{(k)}(0) + \int_0^1 f^{(m)}(x) g^{(m)}(x) \, dx,$$ $W_m[0, 1]$ is a Hilbert space.

Why this inner product?

The boundary terms $f^{(k)}(0) g^{(k)}(0)$ might look strange. They are there to make the inner product positive definite: if you only used the integral $\int f^{(m)} g^{(m)}$, then any polynomial of degree less than $m$ would have zero "norm" — that's a seminorm, not a norm. Adding the boundary terms repairs the deficiency by anchoring the low-order Taylor coefficients.

The reproducing kernel

$W_m[0, 1]$ has a remarkable property: there exists a function $K : [0, 1]^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ such that for every $f \in W_m[0, 1]$ and every $y \in [0, 1]$, $$f(y) = \langle f, K(\cdot, y) \rangle.$$ Point evaluation is realized as an inner product. This means evaluation is a bounded linear functional on $W_m$ — convergence in $W_m$-norm forces pointwise convergence. Hilbert spaces with this property are called reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS), and $K$ is the reproducing kernel. Keener gives the explicit formula $$K(x, y) = \sum_{k=0}^{m-1} \frac{x^k y^k}{(k!)^2} + \int_0^{x \wedge y} \frac{(x - u)^{m-1} (y - u)^{m-1}}{((m-1)!)^2} \, du.$$

The clinching argument: the minimizer is a spline

Let $\Pi_m$ be the space of polynomials of degree $\leq m - 1$, and let $\eta_i = K(\cdot, x_i)$. Define $$V = \Pi_m \oplus \mathrm{span}\{\eta_1, \dots, \eta_n\}.$$ This is a finite-dimensional subspace of $W_m[0, 1]$ (dimension $n + m$).

Any $f \in W_m$ decomposes as $f = g + h$ with $g \in V$ and $h \in V^\perp$. Two crucial facts about $h$:

  • $h(x_i) = \langle h, \eta_i \rangle = 0$ for every design point — by orthogonality to each $\eta_i$.
  • $h^{(k)}(0) = 0$ for $k = 0, \dots, m - 1$ — by orthogonality to the monomials in $\Pi_m$.

Therefore $\|h\|^2 = \|h^{(m)}\|_2^2$ (only the integral term survives) and $\langle g, h \rangle = 0$ gives $\|g^{(m)} + h^{(m)}\|_2^2 = \|g^{(m)}\|_2^2 + \|h^{(m)}\|_2^2$. Plugging into $J$: $$J(g + h) = \sum_i \big(Y_i - g(x_i)\big)^2 + \lambda \|g^{(m)}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|h\|^2 = J(g) + \lambda \|h\|^2.$$ So any nonzero $h$-component strictly increases the objective. The minimizer must have $h = 0$, i.e. must live in the finite-dimensional $V$.

What just happened

We started with an infinite-dimensional optimization problem — minimize over all of $W_m[0, 1]$ — and proved that we never need to look outside an $(n + m)$-dimensional subspace. The infinite-dimensional problem collapses to ordinary linear algebra. This is one of the most beautiful applications of Hilbert space technology in statistics.

What is a spline?

Definition 18.14 — spline

A spline of order $q$ with knots $0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n < 1$ is a function that is a polynomial of degree $\leq q - 1$ on each interval $[x_i, x_{i+1}]$ and has $q - 2$ continuous derivatives on $[0, 1]$.

Working through the derivatives of $\eta_i$ shows that elements of $V$ are splines of order $2m$. Moreover, since $\eta_i$ is a polynomial of degree $\leq m - 1$ on the last interval $[x_n, 1]$, and by a time-reversal argument also on the first interval $[0, x_1]$, the minimizer is in fact a natural spline: a spline whose first and last segments are polynomials of low degree $q - 1 = m - 1$ rather than the higher $2q - 1 = 2m - 1$ allowed in the interior.

Cubic smoothing spline ($m = 2$)

The most-used case: take $m = 2$, so the penalty is $\int (f'')^2$ — the canonical "roughness" measure from physics (bending energy of a thin metal strip). The minimizer is a natural cubic spline: cubic between consecutive knots, with continuous second derivatives, and linear on $[0, x_1]$ and $[x_n, 1]$. This is the function MATLAB calls csaps, R calls smooth.spline, and most textbooks just call "the smoothing spline".

Computing $\hat f$ via linear algebra

Pick a basis $e_1, \dots, e_k$ for the space of natural splines (or any space containing it). Write $f = \sum_j c_j e_j$. Then $f(x_i) = [A c]_i$ where $A_{ij} = \langle e_j, \eta_i \rangle = e_j(x_i)$, and $\|f^{(m)}\|_2^2 = c^\top B c$ where $B_{ij} = \int_0^1 e_i^{(m)} e_j^{(m)}$. The objective becomes $$J = Y^\top Y - 2 Y^\top A c + c^\top A^\top A c + \lambda c^\top B c,$$ whose gradient vanishes at $$\hat c = (A^\top A + \lambda B)^{-1} A^\top Y.$$ Plugging $\hat c$ back into the basis gives $\hat f = \sum_j \hat c_j e_j$.

Ridge regression has appeared in disguise. The roughness penalty $\lambda c^\top B c$ is a weighted $\ell_2$ penalty on the coefficients, and $\hat c$ is a ridge estimator with penalty matrix $B$ instead of $I$. The connection runs deep: every smoothing spline is a ridge regression in an appropriate basis.

B-splines

Choosing the basis $\{e_j\}$ cleverly speeds up computation. The truncated-power basis $(x - x_i)_+^{2q-1}$ is mathematically clean but produces a dense matrix $B$. B-splines are an alternative: compactly supported basis functions, each one nonzero on only a few adjacent knot intervals. With B-splines, $A$ is sparse and $B$ is banded — $B_{ij} = 0$ when $|i - j| \geq 2$ for the simplest case. Solving the banded linear system takes $O(n)$ time instead of $O(n^3)$. This is what makes large-scale smoothing splines practical.

Pause — cross-validating $\lambda$

The same warnings as for bandwidth apply. A plug-in $\lambda$ is tricky because it depends on unknown derivatives of $f$. Generalized cross-validation (GCV) is a closed-form approximation to leave-one-out CV that is standard for splines: minimize $\mathrm{RSS}(\lambda) / (1 - \mathrm{tr}(S_\lambda)/n)^2$ over $\lambda$, where $S_\lambda$ is the smoother matrix mapping $Y$ to $\hat Y$. It is fast, automatic, and usually decent. Like all CV methods, it can be wobbly when $n$ is small or the signal-to-noise ratio is low. Plot $\hat f$ at a few $\lambda$ on either side of the GCV optimum before trusting it.

18.4 Density estimation

The same machinery applies, with a small twist, to estimating an unknown density $f$ from i.i.d. samples $X_1, \dots, X_n \sim Q$.

The empirical distribution is too rough

The first thing you might try is the empirical distribution $$\hat Q(A) = \frac{1}{n} \#\{i : X_i \in A\},$$ which puts mass $1/n$ at each observation. It is a perfectly good estimator of the distribution function $Q$. But if $Q$ has a smooth density $f$, $\hat Q$ has no density at all — it is a sum of point masses. It is the analogue of the linear-interpolant $\hat h$ from Section 18.1: correct on average but too rough to be useful.

The kernel density estimator

Smooth $\hat Q$ by convolution with a kernel. Take $W$ a symmetric probability density, $b > 0$, and define $$\hat f(x) = \frac{1}{b} \int W\!\left(\frac{x - t}{b}\right) d\hat Q(t) = \frac{1}{nb} \sum_{i=1}^n W\!\left(\frac{x - X_i}{b}\right).$$ You place a small bump of width $b$ on top of each observation and add them up. The result is the kernel density estimator. Width $b$ is the bandwidth, with the same role as before.

Bias, variance, optimal bandwidth — the same story

Under mild regularity, expanding by Taylor: $$E \hat f(x) = f(x) + \tfrac{1}{2} b^2 f''(x) \int t^2 W(t) \, dt + o(b^2),$$ $$\mathrm{Var}(\hat f(x)) = \frac{f(x) \|W\|_2^2}{nb} + o(1/(nb)).$$ Integrate to get $$\mathrm{IMSE} = \frac{\|W\|_2^2}{nb} + \tfrac{1}{4} b^4 \|f''\|_2^2 \left(\int t^2 W\right)^2 + o(\cdots),$$ whose minimum over $b$ scales like $n^{-1/5}$, giving the optimal $\mathrm{IMSE} \sim n^{-4/5}$ — the same rate as for kernel regression. Once again, bias and variance are the two faces of the bandwidth knob, and the optimal balance is achieved at a rate slower than parametric but still going to zero.

Pause — cross-validation for densities

For density estimation, there is no obvious "prediction error" to cross-validate. Instead, the standard trick is to minimize an unbiased estimate of $\int (\hat f - f)^2 = \int \hat f^2 - 2 \int \hat f f + \int f^2$. The last term does not depend on $b$. The first term is computable from $\hat f$ alone. The middle term has unbiased estimator $\frac{2}{n} \sum_i \hat f^{(-i)}(X_i)$ where $\hat f^{(-i)}$ omits $X_i$. So the criterion to minimize is $$\int \hat f_b^2(x) \, dx - \frac{2}{n} \sum_i \hat f^{(-i)}_b(X_i).$$ This is called least-squares cross-validation for density estimation. Same flavor as leave-one-out for regression — different bookkeeping.

The spline analogue: log-densities

Can we run the spline machinery on density estimation directly? Not quite: $f$ has to be nonnegative and integrate to one, and minimizing a penalized log-likelihood without those constraints can blow up. The standard trick (Silverman, 1982) is to parametrize $f$ through its logarithm $\theta = \log f$ and maximize a penalized log-likelihood $$J(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \theta(X_i) - \int_0^1 e^{\theta(x)} dx - \lambda \|\theta^{(m)}\|_2^2.$$ The first term wants the density large at observed points; the second term enforces the integral constraint via a Lagrange-like mechanism; the third is the familiar roughness penalty. One can show $J$ is strictly concave (so the maximizer is unique when it exists), and that for sensible $\lambda$ the maximizer is consistent at rates close to $n^{-(4m-1)/(4m+1)}$ in supremum norm.

Common pitfall — bandwidth equals scale

For kernel density estimation in one dimension, "optimal $b$" formulas like Silverman's rule of thumb $b \approx 1.06 \hat\sigma n^{-1/5}$ are popular shortcuts. They are tuned to Gaussian densities; for bimodal or heavy-tailed distributions they badly oversmooth — fusing two peaks into one. Always plot the kernel estimate at several bandwidths around the rule-of-thumb value before declaring a finding. The same warning applies in kernel regression: do not trust a single bandwidth blindly.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 18

The chapter in seven bullets:

  1. In nonparametric regression $Y_i = f(x_i) + \epsilon_i$, the unknown $f$ is treated as a smooth function rather than a member of a finite-parameter family.
  2. Kernel regression estimates $f$ by a locally weighted average of responses: $\hat f(x) = \sum_i v_i(x) Y_i$ with weights concentrated within a bandwidth $b$ of $x$.
  3. The bias–variance tradeoff is the heart of every smoothing method. Bias scales like $b^2$ and is driven by $f''$; variance scales like $1/(nb)$. The optimum balances them at $b \asymp n^{-1/5}$, giving the canonical nonparametric rate $\mathrm{IMSE} \asymp n^{-4/5}$.
  4. Cross-validation is the standard practical tool for choosing the bandwidth or smoothing parameter, because the asymptotically optimal formulas depend on unknown derivatives of $f$.
  5. Hilbert space theory — norms, inner products, completeness, projections, orthonormal bases — provides the language for treating $f$ as a vector in an infinite-dimensional space.
  6. Smoothing splines arise as the minimizer of penalized least squares $\sum (Y_i - f(x_i))^2 + \lambda \int (f^{(m)})^2$. The minimizer always lives in a finite-dimensional space of natural splines, so the problem collapses to ridge regression in a B-spline basis.
  7. Kernel density estimation applies the same convolution idea to the empirical distribution, producing $\hat f$ with the same $n^{-4/5}$ rate and the same bias–variance structure. A penalized log-likelihood approach extends the spline machinery to densities.
