Chapter 14

General Linear Model

One framework — regression, ANOVA, designed experiments — built on a single picture: the data vector projected onto a subspace.

Book pages: 269–300 ~80 min reading time Prereqs: Ch. 1 (random vectors), basic linear algebra

Why this model dominates statistics

If you open any applied statistics paper from the last sixty years, the odds are overwhelming that somewhere in it you'll find a sentence like "we fit a linear model" or "we ran a one-way ANOVA". These are not two different techniques. They are the same technique, dressed in different clothes.

The unifying object is the equation $$Y = X\beta + \epsilon. \tag{14.1}$$ Here $Y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the data vector, $X$ is an $n \times p$ matrix of known constants (the design matrix), $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is an unknown parameter vector, and $\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is unobserved random noise. The standard distributional assumption is that the errors are i.i.d. normal: $$\epsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I), \qquad \text{so that} \qquad Y \sim N(X\beta, \sigma^2 I). \tag{14.3}$$

What the equation is saying

$Y$ is a noisy observation of a signal that lives in a $p$-dimensional family of possibilities. The signal $X\beta$ is some linear combination of the columns of $X$; the noise $\epsilon$ kicks $Y$ away from that signal by a random amount in every direction. Statistics, in this chapter, is the art of looking at $Y$ and reading off the signal.

Two examples make the abstraction concrete. They will recur throughout the chapter.

Example 14.1 — quadratic regression

We model a response $Y_i$ as a quadratic function of an explanatory variable $x_i$: $$Y_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 x_i + \beta_3 x_i^2 + \epsilon_i, \quad i = 1, \dots, n.$$ The design matrix has rows $(1, x_i, x_i^2)$. "Linear model" refers to linearity in $\beta$, not in $x$; the $x_i^2$ term is fine because the parameters still enter linearly.

Example 14.2 — one-way ANOVA

We have three independent samples from $N(\beta_1, \sigma^2)$, $N(\beta_2, \sigma^2)$, $N(\beta_3, \sigma^2)$. Stacking the observations into $Y$ and using a design matrix whose columns are indicator vectors for the three groups, we recover $Y = X\beta + \epsilon$. The "treatment effects" $\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3$ are just the group means.

Pause

Regression and ANOVA look completely different in an introductory book — one is about lines, the other about comparing groups. The design matrix is the bridge. Once you pick $X$, the rest of the machinery (estimation, testing, intervals) doesn't care which problem you started with.

The mean vector $\xi$ — a cleaner parameter

The parameter $\beta$ is convenient to interpret but technically awkward. Why? Because when the columns of $X$ are linearly dependent, two different $\beta$'s can give the same mean for $Y$. Specifically, if $Xv = 0$ for some nonzero $v$, then $\beta$ and $\beta + v$ produce identical distributions for $Y$. The data cannot tell them apart.

The book's fix is to reparametrize. Let $$\xi \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} EY = X\beta.$$ If $c_1, \dots, c_p$ are the columns of $X$, then $\xi = \beta_1 c_1 + \cdots + \beta_p c_p$, so $\xi$ lies in the column space of $X$. We give that subspace a name: $$\omega \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \operatorname{span}\{c_1, \dots, c_p\} = \{X\beta : \beta \in \mathbb{R}^p\}.$$ $\omega$ is a vector subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ of dimension $r$ where $r = \operatorname{rank}(X) \leq p$.

Two parameters, one truth

$\beta$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^p$ and is what the applied scientist usually wants. $\xi$ lives in $\omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and is what the geometry wants. The map $\beta \mapsto X\beta = \xi$ is one-to-one precisely when $X$ has full rank $r = p$. When it doesn't, $\xi$ is still identifiable but $\beta$ is not — a warning sign for multicollinearity, which we return to.

14.1 Canonical form

Almost every result in this chapter becomes easy if we first rotate $Y$ into a coordinate system tailored to the subspace $\omega$. The trick is to pick an orthonormal basis $v_1, \dots, v_n$ for $\mathbb{R}^n$ such that the first $r$ vectors $v_1, \dots, v_r$ span $\omega$ and the remaining $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ span the orthogonal complement $\omega^\perp$.

Define the orthogonal matrix $O$ with columns $v_1, \dots, v_n$ (so $O'O = OO' = I$) and let $$Z = O'Y. \tag{14.4}$$ The vector $Z \in \mathbb{R}^n$ contains the coefficients of $Y$ in the new basis.

Geometric picture

Imagine $\mathbb{R}^n$ as a room. The subspace $\omega$ is a (flat) plane through the origin — perhaps a two-dimensional surface inside a three-dimensional room, if $n=3$ and $r=2$. We choose coordinate axes so that the first $r$ axes lie in the plane and the remaining $n-r$ axes are perpendicular to it. After rotation, the components $Z_1, \dots, Z_r$ measure where $Y$ sits "within the plane" and $Z_{r+1}, \dots, Z_n$ measure how far it sticks out.

Because rotations preserve the normal family, the rotated noise $\epsilon^* = O'\epsilon$ is still $N(0, \sigma^2 I)$. Setting $\eta = O' \xi$, we get $$Z \sim N(\eta, \sigma^2 I). \tag{14.5}$$ Now the magic: since $\xi$ lies in $\omega$ and $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ are orthogonal to $\omega$, $$\eta_{r+1} = \cdots = \eta_n = 0. \tag{14.6}$$

In canonical coordinates, the model is brutally simple. The first $r$ components $Z_1, \dots, Z_r$ are independent normals with unknown means $\eta_1, \dots, \eta_r$ and common variance $\sigma^2$. The last $n - r$ components $Z_{r+1}, \dots, Z_n$ are independent $N(0, \sigma^2)$ — pure noise. All of the signal is packed into the first $r$ coordinates.

Pause

Read that paragraph again. The whole linear model has collapsed into "$r$ noisy estimates of unknown means, plus $n - r$ leftover noise terms whose mean is known to be zero". Everything else — least squares, Gauss–Markov, $F$-tests — is bookkeeping on top of this picture.

The joint density factors into a clean exponential family with complete sufficient statistic $$\left( Z_1, \dots, Z_r, \sum_{i=1}^n Z_i^2 \right). \tag{14.7}$$ This is what justifies all the UMVU claims that follow.

14.2 Estimation

In the canonical form $EZ_i = \eta_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, r$. So $Z_i$ is the unbiased estimator of $\eta_i$, and since it is a function of the complete sufficient statistic, it is in fact the UMVU estimator.

Translating back: since $\xi = \sum_{i=1}^r \eta_i v_i$, the obvious estimator is $$\hat{\xi} = \sum_{i=1}^r Z_i v_i. \tag{14.8}$$ And the residual vector is $$e = Y - \hat{\xi} = \sum_{i=r+1}^n Z_i v_i. \tag{14.10}$$

Each piece of $Y$ has a job. The first $r$ coefficients build $\hat{\xi}$ — the estimate of the signal. The remaining $n - r$ coefficients build $e$ — the residual, which contains pure noise.

The geometric meaning: projection

$\hat{\xi} \in \omega$ and $e \in \omega^\perp$, and they add up to $Y$. So we have orthogonally decomposed $Y$ into "the part in $\omega$" plus "the part orthogonal to $\omega$".

By the Pythagorean theorem, for any other point $\tilde{\xi} \in \omega$, $$\|Y - \tilde{\xi}\|^2 = \|\hat{\xi} - \tilde{\xi}\|^2 + \|e\|^2 \;\geq\; \|e\|^2 = \|Y - \hat{\xi}\|^2.$$ So $\hat{\xi}$ is the unique point in $\omega$ closest to $Y$. This is the geometric definition of orthogonal projection, and the map $Y \mapsto \hat{\xi}$ is a linear operator we'll write as $$\hat{\xi} = PY.$$ The matrix $P$ is the projection matrix onto $\omega$ — also affectionately known as the hat matrix, because it puts a hat on $Y$.

Definition — projection matrix

An $n \times n$ matrix $P$ is the orthogonal projection onto a subspace $\omega$ if $PY$ is the closest point in $\omega$ to every $Y \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Equivalently, $P$ is symmetric and idempotent ($P^2 = P$). In our basis, $P = v_1 v_1' + \cdots + v_r v_r'$.

Why $P^2 = P$?

Projecting twice is the same as projecting once. If you're already on the floor, dropping to the floor again doesn't move you. Algebraically: $P\hat{\xi} = \hat{\xi}$ because $\hat{\xi} \in \omega$, and so $P^2 Y = P(PY) = P\hat{\xi} = \hat{\xi} = PY$ for every $Y$.

Least squares — same answer, different starting point

An alternative motivation for $\hat{\xi}$ doesn't mention canonical bases at all. Pick $\hat{\beta}$ to minimize the sum of squared errors $$\|Y - X\beta\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl[ Y_i - (X\beta)_i \bigr]^2. \tag{14.11}$$ Setting partial derivatives to zero gives the normal equations: $$X'Y = X'X\hat{\beta}. \tag{14.12}$$ When $X$ has full rank ($r = p$), $X'X$ is invertible and $$\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1} X' Y. \tag{14.13}$$ Then $\hat{\xi} = X\hat{\beta} = X(X'X)^{-1} X' Y$, so the projection matrix has the explicit formula $$P = X(X'X)^{-1} X'. \tag{14.14}$$

Two routes to the same place

Canonical-form route: rotate $Y$ until $\omega$ is the first $r$ coordinate axes, keep the first $r$ coefficients, rotate back. Least-squares route: write the squared-error criterion in terms of $\beta$ and minimize. Both produce $\hat{\xi}$. The first is conceptually clean; the second is what you actually compute.

14.3 Gauss–Markov theorem

Up to now we assumed the errors were i.i.d. normal. The next theorem says we can weaken normality almost entirely and still get a strong optimality statement — provided we restrict attention to linear estimators.

Relax the assumptions: only require $E\epsilon = 0$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon) = \sigma^2 I$. No normality. The $\epsilon_i$ may not even share a distribution. A linear estimator of some scalar function $a' \xi$ is any estimator of the form $b' Y = b_1 Y_1 + \cdots + b_n Y_n$.

Theorem 14.4 — Gauss–Markov

Suppose $EY = X\beta$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(Y) = \sigma^2 I$. Then for every constant vector $a$, the least-squares estimator $a' \hat{\xi}$ of $a'\xi$ is unbiased and has uniformly minimum variance among all linear unbiased estimators.

Least squares is BLUE — what's blue?

BLUE stands for Best Linear Unbiased Estimator. "Best" means smallest variance, "linear" means $b'Y$, "unbiased" means $E b'Y = a'\xi$. Gauss–Markov says least squares wins inside this restricted arena, even when we drop the normality assumption that gave us UMVU in the previous section.

The catch is in the word "linear". Drop that restriction, and least squares might be beaten — if the noise is heavy-tailed, the sample median can have smaller variance than the sample mean, and the median is not a linear function of $Y$. Gauss–Markov is a statement about a class of estimators, not a universal optimality claim.

The proof sketch is short. Using the identity $\operatorname{Cov}(AY) = A \operatorname{Cov}(Y) A'$ from Chapter 1, the variance of any linear estimator $b' Y$ is $\sigma^2 \|b\|^2$. The least-squares estimator $a' \hat{\xi} = a' P Y = (Pa)' Y$ has variance $\sigma^2 \|Pa\|^2$. Under normality, both estimators are unbiased and $a'\hat{\xi}$ is UMVU, so $\|Pa\|^2 \leq \|b\|^2$. But that inequality involves only second moments of $Y$, which are the same whether or not $Y$ is normal — hence it holds in the relaxed setting too.

Example 14.5 — when nonlinear beats linear

Take $Y_i = \beta + \epsilon_i$ with $\epsilon_i$ i.i.d. from the double-exponential (Laplace) density $f(x) = e^{-\sqrt{2}|x|/\sigma} / (\sigma \sqrt{2})$. The conditions of Gauss–Markov hold, and the sample mean $\bar Y$ is the best linear unbiased estimator with variance $\sigma^2/n$. But the sample median $\tilde Y$ has asymptotic variance $\sigma^2/(2n)$ — roughly half as large. The median is nonlinear, so Gauss–Markov says nothing about it; the median wins because the Laplace distribution has heavier tails than the normal.

14.4 Estimating $\sigma^2$

We have an estimator for $\xi$. We also need one for the noise variance $\sigma^2$. In canonical form, $Z_{r+1}, \dots, Z_n$ are i.i.d. $N(0, \sigma^2)$, so $EZ_i^2 = \sigma^2$ for those indices. The natural estimator averages their squares: $$S^2 = \frac{1}{n - r} \sum_{i=r+1}^n Z_i^2. \tag{14.18}$$

This $S^2$ is unbiased and (being a function of the complete sufficient statistic) UMVU. Better, it has a geometric expression that doesn't require constructing the basis. Since $e = \sum_{i=r+1}^n Z_i v_i$ and the $v_i$ are orthonormal, $\|e\|^2 = \sum_{i=r+1}^n Z_i^2$, giving $$S^2 = \frac{\|e\|^2}{n - r} = \frac{\|Y - \hat{\xi}\|^2}{n - r}. \tag{14.20}$$

Where do the degrees of freedom come from?

We divide by $n - r$, not $n$. Reason: we used $r$ "degrees of freedom" to estimate the $r$ unknown components of $\hat{\xi}$. The residual lives in the $(n - r)$-dimensional space $\omega^\perp$, and that is the number of independent squared deviations contributing to $\|e\|^2$. Dividing by $n - r$ gives an unbiased estimator; dividing by $n$ would underestimate $\sigma^2$.

Because $\hat{\xi}$ is a function of $Z_1, \dots, Z_r$ and $e$ is a function of $Z_{r+1}, \dots, Z_n$, they are independent. And $$\frac{(n - r) S^2}{\sigma^2} = \sum_{i=r+1}^n (Z_i / \sigma)^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-r}. \tag{14.21}$$ These two facts — independence of $\hat{\xi}$ and $S^2$, plus the chi-square distribution of $S^2$ — are what power every confidence interval and $F$-test in the rest of the chapter.

Confidence intervals for linear functions of $\xi$

The standard deviation of $a' \hat{\xi}$ is $\sigma \|Pa\|$, estimated by $\hat{\sigma}_{a'\hat\xi} = S \|Pa\|$. Dividing the centered estimator by this estimated standard deviation gives a $t$-statistic with $n - r$ degrees of freedom, yielding the interval $$\Bigl( a'\hat{\xi} - \hat{\sigma}_{a'\hat\xi} \, t_{\alpha/2, n - r}, \;\; a'\hat{\xi} + \hat{\sigma}_{a'\hat\xi} \, t_{\alpha/2, n - r} \Bigr)$$ with coverage $1 - \alpha$. When $X$ has full rank, the $i$th coordinate of $\beta$ has interval $$\Bigl( \hat{\beta}_i - S \sqrt{[(X'X)^{-1}]_{ii}} \, t_{\alpha/2, n-p}, \;\; \hat{\beta}_i + S \sqrt{[(X'X)^{-1}]_{ii}} \, t_{\alpha/2, n-p} \Bigr). \tag{14.22}$$

14.5 Simple linear regression

Time to make all of this concrete with the simplest non-trivial example. Specify $$Y_i = \beta_1 + \beta_2 (x_i - \bar x) + \epsilon_i, \quad i = 1, \dots, n,$$ with $\epsilon_i$ i.i.d. $N(0, \sigma^2)$ and $\bar x = n^{-1} \sum x_i$. The reason for centering the $x$'s is that it makes the columns of $X$ orthogonal — which makes the algebra clean. The design matrix is $$X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_1 - \bar x \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n - \bar x \end{pmatrix}.$$

Because $\sum (x_i - \bar x) = 0$, the columns are orthogonal and $$X'X = \begin{pmatrix} n & 0 \\ 0 & s_{xx} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (X'X)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/n & 0 \\ 0 & 1/s_{xx} \end{pmatrix},$$ where $s_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$. Then $$\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1} X' Y = \begin{pmatrix} \bar Y \\[2pt] \sum Y_i (x_i - \bar x) / s_{xx} \end{pmatrix},$$ with covariance matrix $$\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\beta}) = \sigma^2 (X'X)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma^2/n & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma^2/s_{xx} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{14.23}$$

What centering bought us

$\hat{\beta}_1$ and $\hat{\beta}_2$ are uncorrelated. The intercept (the value of the regression at $x = \bar x$) and the slope are estimated independently. Without centering, the two estimates would be correlated, which would muddle their interpretation. This is a small example of why thoughtful design matters even before any data are collected.

The residual sum of squares can be written in terms of the sample correlation $$\hat\rho = \frac{\sum (Y_i - \bar Y)(x_i - \bar x)}{\sqrt{\sum(Y_i - \bar Y)^2 \, s_{xx}}}$$ as $$S^2 = \frac{1}{n - 2} \sum (Y_i - \bar Y)^2 (1 - \hat\rho^2),$$ which exposes the slogan: $\hat\rho^2$ is the proportion of variation in $Y$ "explained" by the linear relationship with $x$. This is your $R^2$.

Common pitfall — extrapolation

The confidence interval for the fitted value $\hat{\beta}_1 + \hat{\beta}_2(x - \bar x)$ has width proportional to $\sqrt{1/n + (x - \bar x)^2 / s_{xx}}$. That widens as $x$ moves away from $\bar x$. Far outside the observed range, the interval doesn't widen enough — because it assumes the model is still linear out there. Predicting next year's housing prices from last decade's data, or extrapolating a dose-response curve beyond tested doses, can fail in ways no confidence interval will warn you about.

Common pitfall — multicollinearity

When two columns of $X$ are highly correlated, $X'X$ is close to singular, $(X'X)^{-1}$ has huge diagonal entries, and the standard errors of $\hat{\beta}_i$ explode. Predictions $\hat{\xi} = X\hat{\beta}$ can still be fine — the geometry $\xi \in \omega$ is unaffected — but the individual coefficients become uninterpretable. Don't try to read "the effect of weight controlling for BMI" off a model where weight and BMI are nearly proportional.

Common pitfall — omitted-variable bias

If the true mean depends on a variable $w$ that you forgot to include, your fitted slope on $x$ absorbs the influence of $w$ to the extent that $w$ correlates with $x$. The estimator is no longer unbiased for the parameter you think it is. Gauss–Markov assumes you have the right model; it says nothing about what happens when the design matrix is misspecified.

14.6 Noncentral $F$ and $\chi^2$ distributions

Two definitions to set up before the next section. Both arise as distributions of quadratic forms in normal random vectors with non-zero means.

Definition 14.7 — noncentral chi-square

If $Z_1 \sim N(\delta, 1)$ and $Z_2, \dots, Z_p$ are independent $N(0,1)$, then $W = \sum_{i=1}^p Z_i^2$ has the noncentral chi-square distribution with $p$ degrees of freedom and noncentrality $\delta^2$, written $W \sim \chi^2_p(\delta^2)$.

More usefully, if $Z \sim N_p(\mu, I)$, then $Z'Z \sim \chi^2_p(\|\mu\|^2)$ — the noncentrality depends only on $\|\mu\|^2$, not on the direction of $\mu$. This follows by rotating $\mu$ to lie along the first coordinate axis. Generalizing, if $Z \sim N_p(\mu, \Sigma)$ with $\Sigma$ positive definite, then $$Z' \Sigma^{-1} Z \sim \chi^2_p(\mu' \Sigma^{-1} \mu).$$

Definition 14.10 — noncentral $F$

If $V \sim \chi^2_k(\delta^2)$ and $W \sim \chi^2_m$ are independent, then $$\frac{V/k}{W/m} \sim F_{k,m}(\delta^2),$$ the noncentral $F$-distribution with degrees of freedom $k$ and $m$ and noncentrality $\delta^2$. When $\delta^2 = 0$ this reduces to the ordinary $F_{k,m}$.

Why we need the noncentral versions

Under the null hypothesis, our test statistic will follow $F_{k,m}$ (central). Under the alternative, it follows $F_{k,m}(\delta^2)$ — the noncentral version, with $\delta^2$ measuring how far the truth is from the null. The noncentral distribution is what we need for power calculations: how often does the test reject when the null is false? That probability depends on $\delta^2$.

14.7 Testing in the general linear model

This is the chapter's main payoff. We are going to test whether the data are consistent with a smaller subspace $\omega_0 \subset \omega$.

Setup: assume $Y \sim N(\xi, \sigma^2 I)$ with $\xi \in \omega$, and let $\omega_0$ be a $q$-dimensional subspace of $\omega$, $0 \leq q < r$. Test $$H_0: \xi \in \omega_0 \quad \text{versus} \quad H_1: \xi \in \omega \setminus \omega_0.$$

Null hypotheses of this form arise constantly. "$\beta_1 = \beta_2$" forces a one-dimensional constraint on $\beta$, cutting $\omega$ down to a smaller subspace $\omega_0$. "All slopes are zero" forces every non-intercept coefficient to vanish, collapsing $\omega$ to a one-dimensional subspace (the constant functions).

The $F$-statistic — comparing two fits

Let $\hat{\xi} = PY$ be the projection onto $\omega$ (the unrestricted fit) and $\hat{\xi}_0 = P_0 Y$ the projection onto $\omega_0$ (the restricted fit). The unrestricted model fits at least as well, so $\|Y - \hat{\xi}\|^2 \leq \|Y - \hat{\xi}_0\|^2$. The question is whether the improvement is convincing or is just noise.

The test statistic is $$T = \frac{n - r}{r - q} \cdot \frac{\|Y - \hat{\xi}_0\|^2 - \|Y - \hat{\xi}\|^2}{\|Y - \hat{\xi}\|^2}.$$ By the Pythagorean theorem (since $Y - \hat{\xi} \in \omega^\perp$ and $\hat{\xi} - \hat{\xi}_0 \in \omega$ are orthogonal), $$\|Y - \hat{\xi}_0\|^2 = \|Y - \hat{\xi}\|^2 + \|\hat{\xi} - \hat{\xi}_0\|^2,$$ so the statistic simplifies to $$T = \frac{\|\hat{\xi} - \hat{\xi}_0\|^2}{(r - q) S^2}. \tag{14.24}$$

What $T$ is measuring

Numerator: the squared distance between the two fitted means $\hat{\xi}$ and $\hat{\xi}_0$, scaled by the number of additional dimensions allowed under the full model. Denominator: the residual variance estimate. $T$ is large when constraining $\xi$ to $\omega_0$ forces a fit that is much worse than the unrestricted fit — relative to ordinary noise.

Theorem 14.11 — the $F$ distribution of $T$

Under the general linear model, $T \sim F_{r-q,\, n-r}(\delta^2)$ with noncentrality $$\delta^2 = \frac{\|\xi - P_0 \xi\|^2}{\sigma^2}. \tag{14.25}$$ Under $H_0$, $\xi \in \omega_0$ so $P_0 \xi = \xi$ and $\delta^2 = 0$; the statistic follows the central $F_{r-q, n-r}$.

Proof idea. Choose the orthonormal basis $v_1, \dots, v_n$ so that $v_1, \dots, v_q$ span $\omega_0$ and $v_1, \dots, v_r$ span $\omega$. Then $\hat{\xi}_0 = \sum_{i=1}^q Z_i v_i$, $\hat{\xi} = \sum_{i=1}^r Z_i v_i$, and the difference is $\hat{\xi} - \hat{\xi}_0 = \sum_{i=q+1}^r Z_i v_i$, whose squared length is $\sum_{i=q+1}^r Z_i^2$. The residual squared length is $\sum_{i=r+1}^n Z_i^2$. Since the $Z_i$ are independent, numerator and denominator are independent, and standard chi-square algebra delivers the $F$-distribution claim with the stated noncentrality.

This is the $F$-test. It is the workhorse hypothesis test of applied statistics — the test printed at the bottom of every regression output, the test that decides "is this added predictor worth keeping?", "is this factor significant?", "do these groups differ?".

Example — analysis of variance ($F$-test for one-way ANOVA)

In Example 14.2, $\omega$ has dimension $r = 3$ (one parameter per group). Testing $H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta_3$ collapses $\omega$ to the one-dimensional space of constant vectors, so $q = 1$. The numerator $\|\hat{\xi} - \hat{\xi}_0\|^2$ becomes $\sum_k n_k (\bar Y_k - \bar Y)^2$ — the between-group sum of squares. The denominator $S^2$ involves the within-group sum of squares $\sum_k \sum_{i \in k} (Y_{ki} - \bar Y_k)^2$. The ratio is the classical ANOVA $F$-statistic. The whole table you see in a one-way ANOVA printout is a bookkeeping device for this single $T \sim F_{r-q, n-r}$.

Example 14.12 — comparing two regression slopes

Two populations, each with a regression through the origin: $Y_i = x_i \beta_1 + \epsilon_i$ for the first $n_1$ observations and $Y_i = x_i \beta_2 + \epsilon_i$ for the next $n_2$. Test $H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2$. Algebra yields $$T = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_1} x_i^2 \;\; \sum_{i=n_1+1}^n x_i^2}{S^2 \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2} \, (\hat{\beta}_1 - \hat{\beta}_2)^2,$$ which under $H_0$ follows $F_{1, n-2}$. The noncentrality under $H_1$ is the same expression with $\beta$'s in place of $\hat{\beta}$'s and $\sigma^2$ in place of $S^2$ — useful for sample-size calculations.

14.8 Simultaneous confidence intervals

A researcher who reports twenty 95% confidence intervals must expect about one of them to fail. If only the "interesting" ones are reported, the overall error rate balloons. Simultaneous confidence intervals address this: bands that collectively cover all the parameters of interest with the stated probability.

In one-way ANOVA with $p$ groups of size $c$, the least-squares estimates are the group means $\hat{\beta}_m = \bar Y_{m\cdot}$, independent and distributed $N(\beta_m, \sigma^2 / c)$. $S^2 = \|Y - \hat{\xi}\|^2 / (p(c-1))$, with $(n - r) S^2 / \sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{p(c-1)}$.

The studentized maximum modulus and the studentized range

To cover all $p$ individual means simultaneously, take intervals $$I_k = \left( \hat{\beta}_k - \frac{S}{\sqrt{c}} q, \;\; \hat{\beta}_k + \frac{S}{\sqrt{c}} q \right)$$ where $q$ is the upper $\alpha$ quantile of the studentized maximum modulus distribution — the distribution of $\max_k |Z_k| / \sqrt{W}$ for independent $Z_k \sim N(0,1)$ and $m W \sim \chi^2_m$. The simultaneous coverage probability $P(\beta_k \in I_k, \forall k) = 1 - \alpha$ does not depend on $\beta$ or $\sigma$.

To cover all pairwise differences $\beta_j - \beta_i$, replace the studentized maximum modulus with the studentized range, the distribution of $(\max_k Z_k - \min_k Z_k)/\sqrt{W}$. This is Tukey's HSD ("honest significant difference") procedure.

Scheffé's method — the general recipe

A more general approach due to Scheffé handles any linear function of $\xi$ (or of $\beta$, when $X$ is full rank). Let $\psi = A\xi$ for some $q \times n$ matrix $A$ with linearly independent rows (after possibly replacing $A$ by $AP$, which doesn't change $\psi$). The least-squares estimator is $\hat{\psi} = A\hat{\xi}$, with covariance $\sigma^2 B$ where $B = AA'$ is positive definite.

By Lemma 14.9, $(\hat{\psi} - \psi)' B^{-1} (\hat{\psi} - \psi) / \sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_q$, independent of $(n - r) S^2 / \sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n - r}$. So $$\frac{(\hat{\psi} - \psi)' B^{-1} (\hat{\psi} - \psi)}{q S^2} \sim F_{q, n - r},$$ giving a confidence ellipse for $\psi$: $$P\Bigl( (\hat{\psi} - \psi)' B^{-1} (\hat{\psi} - \psi) \leq q S^2 F_{\alpha, q, n-r} \Bigr) = 1 - \alpha.$$

Then, by the Schwarz inequality, the linear functional $h' \psi$ falls inside the random interval $$\Bigl( h' \hat{\psi} - \hat{\sigma}_{h'\hat\psi} \sqrt{q F_{\alpha, q, n-r}}, \;\; h' \hat{\psi} + \hat{\sigma}_{h'\hat\psi} \sqrt{q F_{\alpha, q, n-r}} \Bigr)$$ simultaneously for every $h \in \mathbb{R}^q$, with probability exactly $1 - \alpha$. Here $\hat{\sigma}_{h'\hat\psi} = S \sqrt{h' B h}$.

What Scheffé really gives you

A single random ellipse containing the true $\psi$ with probability $1 - \alpha$. Projecting that ellipse onto any direction $h$ gives a confidence interval for $h' \psi$. All those intervals are valid simultaneously — you may go data-snooping after the fact and the global coverage probability is unchanged. The price for this freedom is that the intervals are wider than they would be for any single pre-specified $h$.

Example 14.15 — confidence bands for a regression line

In simple linear regression, the value of the regression function at $x$ is $\beta_1 + \beta_2(x - \bar x)$. Applying Scheffé with $\psi = \beta$ (so $q = 2$) gives the band $$\hat{\beta}_1 + \hat{\beta}_2 (x - \bar x) \pm S \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x - \bar x)^2}{s_{xx}}} \sqrt{2 F_{\alpha, 2, n-2}}$$ containing the true regression line at every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ simultaneously with probability $1 - \alpha$. This is the curving "hourglass" band around a regression fit; the width grows as $x$ moves away from $\bar x$ — and that growth is the price of guaranteeing simultaneous coverage.

Example 14.16 — contrasts in ANOVA

A contrast in one-way ANOVA is a linear combination $a'\beta = a_1 \beta_1 + \cdots + a_p \beta_p$ with $\sum a_i = 0$. Contrasts compare weighted averages of means, e.g. $\beta_3 - \beta_1$ or $\frac{1}{2}(\beta_1 + \beta_3) - \frac{1}{2}(\beta_2 + \beta_4)$. The Scheffé intervals over all contrasts are $$a'\hat\beta \pm \frac{S \|a\|}{\sqrt c} \sqrt{(p - 1) F_{\alpha, p-1, p(c-1)}},$$ valid simultaneously for every contrast $a$. Wider than any single $t$-interval would be, but they buy full post-hoc freedom.

Summary — what you learned in Chapter 14

The whole chapter, in eight bullets:

  1. The general linear model $Y = X\beta + \epsilon$ unifies regression and ANOVA. The mean $\xi = X\beta$ lives in the column space $\omega$ of $X$.
  2. In canonical form, a rotation puts the unknown signal into the first $r$ coordinates and pure noise into the remaining $n - r$ — making everything that follows transparent.
  3. The least-squares estimator $\hat{\xi} = PY$ is the orthogonal projection of $Y$ onto $\omega$. When $X$ has full rank, $P = X(X'X)^{-1}X'$ and $\hat{\beta} = (X'X)^{-1}X'Y$.
  4. Residuals $e = Y - \hat{\xi}$ lie in $\omega^\perp$, orthogonal to the fit; this decomposition powers Pythagorean arguments throughout.
  5. Gauss–Markov: without normality, $a'\hat{\xi}$ is BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator) for $a'\xi$.
  6. $S^2 = \|e\|^2 / (n - r)$ is the UMVU estimator of $\sigma^2$, independent of $\hat{\xi}$, and $(n-r)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-r}$.
  7. The $F$-test $T = \|\hat{\xi} - \hat{\xi}_0\|^2 / ((r-q) S^2)$ tests nested hypotheses $H_0: \xi \in \omega_0$ versus $H_1: \xi \in \omega$; under $H_0$, $T \sim F_{r-q, n-r}$, and under $H_1$, $T \sim F_{r-q, n-r}(\delta^2)$ with $\delta^2 = \|\xi - P_0\xi\|^2 / \sigma^2$.
  8. Simultaneous intervals (studentized max modulus, studentized range, Scheffé) protect against multiple-comparisons inflation, at the cost of wider individual intervals.
