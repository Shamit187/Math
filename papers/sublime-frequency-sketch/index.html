Sublime: Sublinear Error & Space for Unbounded Skewed Streams

Navid Eslami, Ioana O. Bercea, Rasmus Pagh, Niv Dayan Proc. ACM Manag. Data 4, 3 (SIGMOD), Article 239, June 2026
Abstract Modern stream processing systems need to track how often each key appears in a data stream, in real time. Exact counting is too expensive, so systems use compact probabilistic structures called frequency estimation sketches. But mainstream sketches fail in two ways: they waste memory on skewed workloads (most counters hold tiny values, yet every counter is sized for the maximum), and their estimation error grows at least linearly as the stream lengthens — a stream that doubles in length doubles the error. Sublime is a framework that fixes both problems simultaneously. It uses short counters that grow on demand, keeping extensions in the same CPU cache line. It also expands its total counter count as the stream grows, achieving sublinear error and sublinear memory relative to the stream length. Applied to both Count-Min Sketch and Count Sketch, Sublime outperforms all prior art in accuracy and memory while matching or beating their speed.

1. Introduction

Tracking how often individual items appear in a fast-moving data stream is one of the most fundamental problems in stream processing. Query optimizers need frequency estimates to plan joins; network monitors count packets per source to spot anomalies; social media platforms count mentions to surface trending content. In every case the stream arrives too fast and grows too large to store verbatim, so only a sketch — a compact summary — is kept.

Definition: Frequency Estimation Sketch A frequency estimation sketch (FE sketch) is a compact, probabilistic data structure that processes a stream of key-value pairs and answers queries of the form "approximately how many times has key x appeared so far?" It uses much less memory than an exact counter table, at the cost of introducing bounded estimation errors.

The paper identifies two concrete problems with every existing FE sketch and then introduces Sublime as a unified solution:

Problem 1 — Skew Real-world streams are skewed: a handful of keys appear far more often than all others (a heavy-tailed, often Zipfian, distribution). Existing sketches allocate a fixed, uniform number of bits to every counter, sized for the worst-case maximum value. The vast majority of counters — those holding small counts — waste all of their high-order bits storing zeros. This memory could instead be used for more counters, improving accuracy.
Problem 2 — Unbounded Growth All existing FE sketches are allocated at a fixed size. As the total number of keys in the stream $N$ grows, the estimation error grows with it — at minimum linearly in $N$. You cannot rebuild a bigger sketch from scratch: the stream is gone. There is no way to maintain tight error bounds as the stream grows indefinitely.

The name Sublime is a deliberate double pun: it refers to the sublinear scaling of both error and memory, and invokes Kant's philosophical sense of awe at something vast or infinite — resonating with the theme of accommodating indefinitely growing data.

2. Background

2.1 Data Streams and the Turnstile Model

A stream is a sequence of key-value pairs $\langle (x_1, \Delta_1), (x_2, \Delta_2), \ldots \rangle$ , where each $\Delta_i$ is either $+1$ (an insertion) or $-1$ (a deletion). This is the General Turnstile Model. Key $x$'s true count is $f(x)$ — its total insertions minus deletions. The total count across all keys is $N = \sum_x f(x) = \sum_i \Delta_i$ .

Intuition Think of $N$ as a running total of all insertions minus deletions. It grows with every insertion and shrinks with every deletion. All error bounds in this paper are expressed in terms of $N$, so when the paper says error grows "linearly with $N$," it means: if your stream doubles in total weight, your maximum error also doubles.

2.2 Count-Min Sketch (CMS)

The Count-Min Sketch (Cormode & Muthukrishnan, 2005) is the simplest and most widely deployed FE sketch. Its core structure is $d$ independent counter arrays, each of width $w$. Each array has its own pairwise-independent hash function $h_i$.

CMS Operations

Insert key $x$: for each array $i = 1,\ldots,d$, increment counter at position $h_i(x)$ by 1.

Delete key $x$: decrement counter at position $h_i(x)$ in each array by 1.

Query key $x$: return $\min_i \text{counter}[i][h_i(x)]$ . Taking the minimum removes positive bias from hash collisions.

Because multiple keys can hash to the same counter, CMS can overestimate but never underestimate a key's count. The expected error of a single array is $N/w$ (the total stream weight divided by the number of counters). Using $d$ independent arrays and taking the minimum boosts confidence exponentially. Formally, for any key $x$:

$$\Pr\!\left(|\hat{f}(x) - f(x)| \le e \cdot \tfrac{N}{w}\right) \ge 1 - e^{-d}$$

Intuition: why d arrays? With a single array, one unlucky collision — a heavy hitter mapping to the same counter as your query key — can blow up the estimate. With $d$ independent arrays, an estimate is only badly wrong if every array has a bad collision simultaneously. The probability of that happening decays exponentially in $d$. In practice, $d=3$ gives roughly 95% confidence.

2.3 Skewed Distributions and the Zipf Law

Many real-world streams follow a Zipfian distribution: the $k$-th most popular key appears proportionally to $1/k^\alpha$ times, for some exponent $\alpha > 0$. Higher $\alpha$ means more skew: the top key appears exponentially more often than the rest. The Kosarak dataset (news portal clicks) has $\alpha \approx 3.5$; WebDocs (words in English text) has $\alpha \approx 1.0$.

Example: memory waste under skew Suppose a stream has 1 million distinct keys and after processing, the maximum counter value is $2^{30}$ (about 1 billion). A standard CMS uses 32-bit counters throughout. But 999,900 of those counters hold values below 1,000 — they need only 10 bits. The remaining 22 bits per counter are wasted. With 1 million counters, that is 22 million bits (about 2.75 MB) of pure overhead — memory that could hold more counters and reduce error.

3. Two Core Problems in Depth

3.1 Why Prior Skew-Handling Methods Fail

Three families of approaches attempt to handle skew. The paper shows each one either compromises accuracy or performance — and crucially, all of them make the growth problem (Problem 2) worse, not just equally bad.

Counter Sharing Arranges counters in a hierarchy. Short counters at the bottom hold low-order bits; when they overflow, they increment a shared parent counter. After $N$ insertions, each counter spills into $\Theta(\log(N/w))$ levels. But those upper-level counters are shared among many keys, so overflows at the top inflate estimates for all keys sharing that ancestor — the error blows up by a factor of $\Theta(\log(N/w))$. That is super-linear growth, worse than standard CMS's linear growth.
Counter Merging (e.g., SALSA) Starts with 8-bit counters. When a counter overflows, it merges with its neighbor to form a 16-bit counter, then 32-bit, and so on. The error grows as $\Theta\!\left(\min\!\left(\log\tfrac{N}{w}, 32\right)\cdot\tfrac{N}{w}\right)$ . Again super-linear until counters reach 4 bytes, then it caps out. Merging also incurs higher metadata overhead and makes deletions complicated.
Hybrid Methods Track heavy hitters in a separate hash table; let CMS handle the rest. But the keys remaining in CMS may still be skewed. Promoting a new heavy hitter is error-prone because CMS collisions can make infrequent keys look frequent.
Key Finding All prior skew-handling methods increase the rate at which error grows with $N$ compared to plain CMS. Sublime is the first to handle skew without exacerbating the growth problem.

3.2 The Unbounded Growth Problem

In a standard CMS with $w$ counters, the expected estimation error for any key is $N/w$. Since $w$ is fixed at allocation time, this error grows linearly with the stream length $N$. A stream of 100 million items has 100× the error of a stream of 1 million items, with the same sketch.

One obvious fix is to allocate a large sketch from the start. But if $N$ is unknown (which it usually is), you either over-provision and waste memory, or under-provision and suffer high errors. Another fix — rebuilding a larger sketch from scratch — is impossible: the original stream is gone and cannot be rescanned.

Pause and Think Why can't you just merge two sketches to double the number of counters? Think about what happens to the hash functions. If key $x$ was mapped to counter $h(x) \bmod w$ in the old sketch, which counter should it map to in a sketch of size $2w$? Simply concatenating two identical copies of the sketch handles this cleanly — read on!

4. The Sublime Framework

Sublime has two independent, composable ideas:

  • VALE (Variable-Length Encoding) — fixes Problem 1 by giving each counter a variable number of bits, sized to fit its actual value.
  • Expansion/Contraction — fixes Problem 2 by doubling the number of counters whenever the stream grows past a threshold, achieving sublinear error.

4.1 VALE: Variable-Length Counter Encoding

VALE (VAriable-Length Encoding) is the mechanism by which Sublime avoids storing wasted high-order bits in counters that hold small values. The core idea: every counter starts as a short fixed-width integer called a stub. If the stub overflows, it is extended by allocating additional bits in an extension pool located in the same CPU cache line as the stub.

4.1.1 Chunks and Cache Lines

The counter array is partitioned into contiguous chunks, each occupying a single 512-bit CPU cache line. Within each chunk:

  • The first $c \cdot s$ bits store $c$ stubs of length $s$ bits each.
  • The next $c$ bits form an overflows bitmap: bit $i$ is 1 if counter $i$ has an extension.
  • The remaining bits form the extension pool, storing the extensions of all overflowing counters, packed contiguously.
Why the same cache line? When you query a key, the CPU fetches the relevant chunk from memory into the L1 cache. If the stub and its extension both live in the same 512-bit cache line, reading the extension costs zero additional cache misses — the data is already there. Prior methods that store extensions elsewhere (or use hierarchies spanning many cache lines) pay extra cache miss penalties on every query, which dominates query latency at scale.

4.1.2 Extension Encoding: Base-3 Fragments

Extensions are stored as a sequence of 2-bit fragments. Three of the four possible 2-bit values encode digits in base 3: $00 \to 0_3,\ 10 \to 1_3,\ 01 \to 2_3$ . The fragment $11$ is a delimiter marking the end of each extension. Digits are stored least-significant first.

Example: encoding 5 as an extension

Suppose a counter's stub holds 6 lower-order bits, and the higher-order bits encode the value 5. Write 5 in base 3: $5 = 1 \cdot 3^1 + 2 \cdot 3^0$ , so the digits (least-significant first) are $v_0 = 2,\ v_1 = 1$. The extension is: fragment for $2_3$ = $01$, then fragment for $1_3$ = $10$, then delimiter $11$. So the bit pattern is $01\ 10\ 11$. Decoding: reconstruct $3^0 \cdot 2 + 3^1 \cdot 1 = 2 + 3 = 5$ . Correct!

Why base 3? Because the delimiter needs one 2-bit pattern (11), leaving exactly three patterns for data (00, 01, 10). Base 3 is the densest encoding possible in this scheme.

4.1.3 Locating an Extension with Rank and Select

Extensions are stored compactly in order of counter index. To find the extension for counter $i$, VALE needs to skip over all extensions belonging to counters before $i$. It does this in two steps using classical bitmap operations:

Rank and Select Given a bitmap $B$:
  • rank(i, B) = number of 1-bits strictly before position $i$.
  • select(m, B) = position of the $m$-th 1-bit.
On modern x86 CPUs, these operations are implemented using the POPCNT, PDEP, and TZCNT instructions — each executing in a single clock cycle on 64-bit words.

Step 1: Compute $m = \text{rank}(i, \text{overflows})$ . This counts how many of the counters before $i$ have overflowed — equivalently, how many extensions precede the target extension in the pool.

Step 2: Construct a delimiters bitmap from the extension pool by ANDing it with a right-shifted copy of itself, then masking to keep only even-position bits. Each 1 in this bitmap marks the end of one extension's delimiter (11). Then $\text{select}(m, \text{delimiters})$ gives the exact bit position where the $m$-th extension ends — which is where the target extension begins (read right-to-left).

Why this is fast A chunk's extension pool fits in 1–2 machine words. The entire rank-and-select workflow applies bitwise operations to those 1–2 words. There are no loops, no pointer chasing, no branches. The CPU processes the entire lookup in a handful of instructions that run pipelined in parallel. Compared to a hierarchical method that traverses multiple levels with cache misses at each level, this is dramatically faster.

4.1.4 Overflow Handling: Tails

In rare cases — many heavy hitters hashing into the same chunk — the extensions may outgrow the available space in the cache line. VALE handles this by switching the chunk to tails mode: it allocates an external array of 32-bit integers (one per counter), repurposes the extension pool space to store a pointer to this array, and sets a 1-bit mode flag in the chunk. Heavy-hitter counters stored in tails benefit from fast binary arithmetic (no base-3 encoding needed), which actually speeds up updates for the most-written counters.

4.1.5 Adaptive Tuning of s and c

VALE has two parameters: $s$ (stub length in bits) and $c$ (counters per chunk). The optimal values depend on workload skew, which changes as the stream evolves. Sublime monitors two signals: if more than 1% of chunks have switched to tails mode (extensions are growing too large), or if there are more than 2 unused bits per counter (extensions are shrinking), it triggers a retuning pass.

During retuning, Sublime scans the arrays, builds a coarse histogram of counter bit-lengths, and applies Chebyshev's inequality to find the combination of $s$ and $c$ that minimizes the predicted memory footprint. The optimal stub length ends up being slightly longer than the average counter length, so that most counters fit in their stubs and extensions are rare. Retuning is amortized: it requires many more updates than the total counter count before triggering, so its per-update cost is negligible.

Intuition: what the optimal stub length buys you If $s$ is too short, many counters overflow and fill the extension pool, wasting space on base-3 encoding overhead and delimiters. If $s$ is too long, each stub consumes more bits than the counter value needs. The sweet spot is a stub that fits the average value: the few large values pay a small base-3 overhead, and the many small values waste nothing. Experiment 3 shows adaptive tuning saves up to 5× memory over a fixed tuning.

4.2 Expansion and Contraction: Handling Unbounded Growth

The second half of Sublime addresses the problem that a fixed-size CMS has error proportional to $N/w$. The insight: if $w$ can grow along with $N$, the ratio $N/w$ can be kept small.

4.2.1 How Expansion Works

When the total count $N$ exceeds a threshold, Sublime expands each counter array by concatenating it with an exact copy of itself, doubling the array length. The new array of $2w$ counters has the left half and right half both initialized to the counts from before expansion. This is cheap: it's a sequential memory copy with no rehashing.

Why copying works: consistent hashing Sublime uses a hash function that maps key $x$ to an array of size $w$ by taking the $\log_2 w$ low-order bits of its hash. After expansion to $2w$, the key maps to the $\log_2 2w$ low-order bits. The extra bit is either 0 (left half — same as before) or 1 (right half — the copy). So every key still points to a counter that holds an accurate copy of its pre-expansion count. Future insertions will update only one of the two copies, gradually making them diverge in accuracy for new data, but the historical counts are preserved.

4.2.2 The Size Function and the Tradeoff Space

The rate of expansion is controlled by a size function $W(N)$, which specifies the target number of counters per array when the total count is $N$. The default is a sublinear power:

$$W(N) = \frac{N^\alpha}{\epsilon}, \quad \alpha \in [0,1),\ \epsilon > 0$$

With the default $\alpha = 1/2$, the array size grows as $\sqrt{N}$, and expansions are triggered each time $N$ quadruples (at $N = 4, 16, 64, \ldots$). The expected estimation error then becomes:

$$\mathbb{E}[E(N)] \le O(1) \cdot \epsilon \cdot N^{1-\alpha}$$

With $\alpha = 1/2$, this is $O(\sqrt{N})$ — sublinear in $N$! Compare this to a static CMS, which has error $O(N)$ for the same memory budget. A larger $\alpha$ gives even lower error at the cost of more memory. The tradeoff space is continuous and tunable: applications can pick any $\alpha$ to balance accuracy against memory usage.

Theorem 4.1 (Error Bounds for SublimeCMS) When using size function $W(N)$ and a single counter array on a stream of total count $N$, the expected estimation error $E(N)$ satisfies:

$$\mathbb{E}[E(N)] \le \begin{cases} O(1)\cdot\epsilon\, N^{1-\alpha} & \text{if } W(N)=N^\alpha/\epsilon,\; \alpha\in[0,1) \\ \log_2 N \cdot \epsilon & \text{if } W(N)=N/\epsilon \end{cases}$$

Compared to a static CMS allocated upfront with $W(N)$ counters with foreknowledge of $N$, these bounds are higher by at most a constant (sublinear case) or logarithmic (linear case) factor — a tiny price for not needing to know $N$ in advance.
Intuition: why a log factor in the linear case If you grow $w$ linearly with $N$, you might hope for constant error. But expansions copy old counters, and those copies accumulate over log many rounds of doubling. Each round adds a small constant to the sum of counter values, and there are $\log N$ rounds, giving the $\log N$ factor. Section 5 proves this is the best any expandable sketch can do — it is an information-theoretic lower bound, not a deficiency of Sublime.

4.2.3 Contraction: Handling Deletions

When many keys are deleted, the stream shrinks and the sketch becomes oversized. Sublime contracts by reverting to a smaller sketch from before the last expansion. The naive approach — folding the halves of the array onto each other — would double the counter values (since both halves hold copies). Instead, Sublime records the sketch state just before each expansion. To contract, it:

  1. Subtracts the pre-expansion snapshot from each half of the current array, yielding a deltas array of recent changes per counter.
  2. Adds these deltas to the corresponding counters of the pre-expansion sketch.
  3. Discards the current sketch and continues with the updated pre-expansion sketch.

The contraction threshold is set to the midpoint between the two most recent expansion thresholds, preventing rapid oscillation. Keeping the snapshot record increases memory by at most 2×, but the savings from contracting after many deletions outweigh this cost.

4.3 Applying Sublime to Count Sketch

Count Sketch (Charikar et al., 2002) is the unbiased cousin of CMS. Where CMS always overestimates, Count Sketch introduces both positive and negative errors that cancel on average, yielding unbiased frequency estimates. This makes it suitable for join size estimation and covariance estimation, where multiplying biased estimates compounds errors.

Count Sketch assigns each key $x$ a random update direction $\sigma(x) \in \{+1,-1\}$ via a second hash function. Insertions increment by $\sigma(x)$ and queries return the counter value multiplied by $\sigma(x)$. The expected interference from any other key $y$ on $x$'s counter is zero, since $y$ is equally likely to push in either direction. CS returns the median of estimates across arrays (rather than the minimum).

The error bound for Count Sketch is expressed in terms of the squared $\ell_2$-norm of the frequency vector: $F = \sum_y (f(y))^2$ . The estimation variance is $V = F/w$, and the standard deviation of the error is $\sqrt{V}$.

SublimeCS applies VALE to Count Sketch by storing the absolute value of each counter using VALE and appending a single sign bit to each stub. For expansion, SublimeCS tracks $F$ rather than $N$ — it expands when $F$ doubles. To maintain an accurate, high-confidence estimate of $F$, SublimeCS maintains 64 AMS sketches (a standard technique for approximating $F$) and updates them using SIMD instructions.

5. Memory Analysis

5.1 Upper Bound on Memory

The key lemma shows how efficiently VALE encodes a single counter value:

Lemma 5.1 (VALE Encoding Cost) With stubs of length $s$ bits, VALE encodes a counter value $v$ in:
  • $s$ bits if $v < 2^s$ (fits in the stub — no extension needed), or
  • at most $4 + 1.26\cdot\log_2 v$ bits if $v \ge 2^s$ (stub plus extension).

The constant 1.26 arises from $2\log_3 2 = 2/\log_2 3 \approx 1.262$ — the overhead of base-3 encoding and delimiters relative to binary. This is only 26% more bits per value than the information-theoretic minimum.

Theorem 5.3 (Memory Footprint of Sublime) With a sublinear power size function $W(N) = N^\alpha/\epsilon$ , SublimeCMS uses approximately:

$$d \cdot W(N) \cdot \left(4.41 + 1.26\cdot\log_2\!\tfrac{N}{W(N)}\right) \text{ bits}$$

With a linear size function, the $\log_2(N/W(N))$ term becomes $\log_2(N\log N/W(N))$ . In both cases memory is sublinear in the stream length.

5.2 Lower Bounds: Sublime is Near-Optimal

The paper proves matching lower bounds by a reduction to filter lower bounds. A filter is a compact data structure that tests set membership (e.g., a Bloom filter). It is known that a filter supporting a set of growing size $n$ with false positive rate $\delta$ must use at least $n\cdot[\Omega(\log 1/\delta + \log\log n) - \Theta(1)]$ bits at some point during processing.

The reduction: given an FE sketch with error $E(N)$ and confidence $1-\delta$, simulate a filter over a set of size $n = N/(E(N)+1)$ by inserting each set element $E(N)+1$ times. A query to a non-member returns at most $E(N)$ with probability $1-\delta$, while a member always exceeds this threshold. The filter lower bound then applies.

Theorems 5.4 & 5.5 (Memory Lower Bounds) Any FE sketch processing growing streams with error bound $E(N)$ and confidence $1-\delta$ must use at least $$\frac{N}{E(N)}\cdot\!\left[\Omega\!\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\log\frac{N}{E(N)}\right)-\Theta(1)\right]$$ bits of memory at some point while processing the stream. This holds for both one-sided (Theorem 5.4, CMS-like) and two-sided (Theorem 5.5, CS-like) error sketches.

Comparing Theorem 5.3 to Theorem 5.5, Sublime's actual memory footprint is within a small logarithmic factor of the information-theoretic minimum. No expandable sketch can do significantly better.

6. Experiments

6.1 Setup

All experiments run on a Fedora 41 machine with an Intel Xeon w7-2495X (4.8 GHz, 24 cores), 80 KB L1 cache and 2 MB L2 per core, 45 MB shared L3, and 64 GB RAM. All code is single-threaded with $d=3$ arrays (yielding approximately 95% confidence). Three real-world datasets:

  • Kosarak: 8M clicks from a news portal (Zipf exponent ≈ 3.5 — very skewed).
  • WebDocs: 300M words from 1.7M English documents (Zipf exponent ≈ 1.0 — moderately skewed).
  • CAIDA: 27M anonymized network 5-tuples from 2018 backbone links (Zipf exponent ≈ 2.0).

Accuracy is measured by Average Absolute Error (AAE): the mean of $|\hat{f}(x) - f(x)|$ across all distinct keys. Baselines include standard CMS, SALSA (counter merging), Waving sketch (hybrid), Stingy sketch (counter sharing), Tailored sketch (probabilistic increments), and Coding sketch (compressed hierarchy).

6.2 Experiment 1: Accuracy and Speed vs. Memory (Skew)

SublimeCMS achieves the lowest estimation error at every memory budget on all three datasets, outperforming all baselines by up to an order of magnitude. Compared to SALSA (the next best in accuracy), SublimeCMS reduces error by 40% while being up to 3× faster on insertions and 2.4× faster on queries. Coding sketch is excluded from speed comparisons after showing insertion and query latencies 2–7 orders of magnitude slower than all other methods.

6.3 Experiment 2: Accuracy under Varying Skew

Across Zipf exponents from 0.0 (uniform) to 3.2 (highly skewed), SublimeCMS provides the lowest error at every skew level — outperforming competitors by an order of magnitude for uniform workloads and by 3× for highly skewed ones. Because SublimeCMS is the only tunable baseline (besides Coding sketch), it adapts its stub length to the observed skew: shorter stubs for more skewed workloads (where counter values are small), longer stubs for more uniform workloads.

6.4 Experiment 3: Memory Savings from Adaptive Tuning

Comparing adaptive VALE tuning against a fixed tuning ( $c=64$, $s=6$ ) across varying skew levels shows that adaptive tuning saves up to 5× in memory. Even at high skew where the fixed version happens to perform reasonably, the adaptive version still uses 10% less memory. Adaptive tuning contributes less than 3% of total processing time.

6.5 Experiment 4: Accuracy and Memory under Data Growth

All baselines are allocated 32 KB and the WebDocs stream is processed, measuring error and memory-per-key as the stream grows to 300 million items. SublimeCMS is the only sketch exhibiting sublinearly growing error: with $\alpha=0.5$ it improves over the best baseline by one order of magnitude; with $\alpha=0.75$ by two orders; with $\alpha=1.0$ by three orders of magnitude. Memory-per-key decreases as the stream grows (sublinear memory), while all other baselines show constant or increasing memory-per-key. Expansions consume only 0.5% of total processing time (they are simple sequential copies).

6.6 Experiment 5: Contractions

The CAIDA dataset is inserted and then keys are deleted in random order. SublimeCMS maintains error up to two orders of magnitude lower than CMS of the same initial size. As keys are deleted, SublimeCMS contracts to free memory, reaching 2–3 orders of magnitude lower memory footprint than CMS while remaining more accurate — despite the reduced counter count slightly raising error. Contractions take less than 0.5% of total execution time.

6.7 Experiment 6: SublimeCS (Unbiased Estimates)

Applied to Count Sketch, SublimeCS achieves lower errors than all unbiased baselines at every memory budget on CAIDA, with faster insertions and comparable query speeds. Stingy sketch with Count Sketch (StingyCS) and Coding sketch with Count Sketch (CodingCS) are slower and less accurate.

6.8 Experiment 7: Expanding by Variance in SublimeCS

For Count Sketch, expanding based on the squared $\ell_2$-norm $F$ (rather than raw key count $N$) maintains stable tail errors across skew levels. Expanding based on $N$ yields tail errors that grow with skew, because $F$ grows faster than $N$ under skew (due to the squared summands). The variance-based expansion correctly triggers more frequent expansions when skew is high.

6.9 Experiment 8: Join Size Estimation

On a 100 GB TPC-H workload (lineitem and orders tables joined on ORDERKEY), both SublimeCMS and SublimeCS outperform plain CMS and CS by multiple orders of magnitude. Expandable Sublime variants further outperform their fixed-size counterparts by up to an order of magnitude. SublimeCS provides the most accurate join estimates, since multiplying unbiased estimates does not compound errors — the key advantage of Count Sketch over CMS in multiply-and-aggregate workloads.

7. Conclusion

Sublime is the first framework to make frequency estimation sketches simultaneously memory-efficient under skew and accurate as the stream grows indefinitely. Its two mechanisms — VALE for variable-length counter encoding and threshold-triggered expansion with consistent hashing — are largely independent and composable, making Sublime broadly applicable. The paper demonstrates Sublime for CMS, Count Sketch, and (in an appendix) Misra-Gries.

The theoretical analysis shows Sublime achieves error and memory within a small logarithmic factor of the information-theoretic lower bound for any expandable sketch. Empirically, it reduces error by 1–3 orders of magnitude over baselines while maintaining equal or superior throughput.

Broader Significance Sublime marks a conceptual shift from "allocate a fixed sketch and accept degrading accuracy" to "start small, grow as needed, and maintain tight guarantees forever." The same approach should generalize to cardinality sketches, quantile sketches, and graph sketches — wherever a fixed-size approximation structure faces an unbounded stream. The authors flag parallelism as an interesting open problem: merging thread-local sketches is straightforward, but the interaction of concurrent updates with VALE's chunk tuning and expansion/contraction events requires care.
