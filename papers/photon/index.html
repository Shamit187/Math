Photon: Fault-tolerant and Scalable Joining of Continuous Data Streams

Rajagopal Ananthanarayanan, Venkatesh Basker, Sumit Das, Ashish Gupta, Haifeng Jiang, Tianhao Qiu, Alexey Reznichenko, Deomid Ryabkov, Manpreet Singh, Shivakumar Venkataraman ACM SIGMOD 2013, New York, NY, USA
Abstract Web-based enterprises process events generated by millions of users interacting with their websites. Rich statistical data distilled from combining such interactions in near real-time generates enormous business value. This paper describes the architecture of Photon, a geographically distributed system for joining multiple continuously flowing streams of data in real-time with high scalability and low latency. The system fully tolerates infrastructure degradation and datacenter-level outages without any manual intervention. Photon guarantees that there will be no duplicates in the joined output (at-most-once semantics) at any point in time, that most joinable events will be present in the output in real-time (near-exact semantics), and exactly-once semantics eventually. Deployed in Google's advertising system, Photon processes millions of events per minute at peak with an average end-to-end latency of less than 10 seconds.

1. The Business Problem

Every time someone searches on Google, a query event is generated. Every time someone clicks an advertisement shown alongside those search results, a click event is generated. These two events — the query and the click — are causally related: you cannot understand a click without knowing the query that preceded it, because the query record contains the advertiser, the ad text, the auction parameters, and the price information needed to bill the advertiser correctly.

Joining these two events in real-time is Photon's job. The joined record — a joined click event — combines the fields from both the click and the original query into a single log entry. These joined logs are used to compute advertiser billing, to generate real-time performance dashboards, to detect click fraud, and to provide advertisers with near-instant feedback on their campaigns.

Intuition — Why Not Just Do This in a Database?

You might wonder: why build a dedicated distributed system? Why not just load clicks and queries into a database and run a join query? The answer is scale and latency. At peak, Photon processes millions of events per minute. The query log stream is orders of magnitude larger than the click stream — tens of terabytes per day — and the volume grows continuously. A batch join over the full dataset takes hours. Photon delivers join results in under 10 seconds on average. Furthermore, the two streams arrive at different datacenters in different orders, with unpredictable delays, and a database join would require both streams to be fully materialized before the join can run. Photon joins events as they arrive, even when the primary stream (query events) has not yet reached the datacenter where the click is being processed.

The stakes are high in both directions. Joining a click event twice means billing an advertiser twice — a serious financial error and a violation of trust. Missing a click event entirely means Google loses the corresponding revenue. The business requirements translate into strict engineering constraints: no duplicates ever (at-most-once in real-time) and no losses except under catastrophic failure (near-exact real-time, exactly-once eventually). These two constraints together define Photon's central design challenge.

2. Why Stream Joining Is Hard

Stream joining looks straightforward on paper: given two streams sharing a common key, match records from one stream with records from the other. In a controlled environment with reliable delivery and synchronized clocks, this is manageable. In a geo-distributed system running on commodity hardware, at advertising scale, it becomes a gauntlet of failure modes.

Definition — The Four Hard Properties

Photon must simultaneously satisfy four properties that are individually achievable but jointly difficult:
Exactly-once semantics: each click is joined and written to output exactly once, never zero times, never twice.
Datacenter-level fault tolerance: the system continues operating without manual intervention during complete datacenter outages, which may last hours or days.
High scalability: the system must handle millions of events per minute today and grow gracefully.
Low latency with unordered, delayed streams: clicks arrive out of order relative to queries, and query logs may be delayed relative to click logs by minutes or longer.

The delayed primary stream property deserves special attention. In a traditional database join, the foreign key must always exist in the primary table — a click cannot exist without its query. In Photon's streaming world, a click may arrive at a logs datacenter before the corresponding query log has been shipped there. The click event knows the query_id of the originating query, but the query record is not yet locally available. Photon must defer joining the click until the query arrives, potentially retrying for hours. This is fundamentally different from how most stream processing frameworks handle joins, which typically require both streams to be approximately synchronized in time.

3. The Semantics Ladder

The paper introduces a useful framework for reasoning about output correctness guarantees, which we can think of as a ladder of increasing strength.

Definition — Three Semantic Levels

At-most-once semantics: no event appears in the output more than once, at any point in time. Duplicates are impossible. Events may be missing (lost), but what appears is always correct. Photon guarantees this in real-time — at any snapshot of the output, no click has been counted twice.

Near-exact (near-complete) semantics: in real-time, nearly all joinable events are present in the output. The paper targets 99.9999% of events joined within a few seconds. A small fraction may be temporarily missing due to delays or processing backlogs, but they will appear eventually.

Exactly-once semantics: every joinable event appears in the output exactly once. This is the gold standard. Photon provides this guarantee eventually — given enough time (a few hours), all joinable events that have not been discarded as too old will be joined exactly once.

The insight is that these three levels can coexist: you can have at-most-once in real-time (no duplicates ever) while converging toward exactly-once over time (no permanent losses). The mechanism that makes this possible is the IdRegistry.

4. The IdRegistry: Coordination via Paxos

The IdRegistry is the heart of Photon's correctness guarantee. It is a globally consistent, fault-tolerant key-value store that tracks the set of event identifiers (click_ids) that have already been joined. Before any worker writes a joined click event to the output logs, it must successfully register the click_id in the IdRegistry. The IdRegistry enforces a simple invariant: each key can be written at most once.

This transforms the deduplication problem into a distributed consensus problem. Two workers in two different datacenters may simultaneously attempt to join the same click. Whichever one successfully inserts the click_id into the IdRegistry first wins; the other receives a failure response and discards the event. Because the IdRegistry uses Paxos consensus, this decision is globally consistent — there is no scenario where both workers believe they won.

Definition — Paxos and the IdRegistry

The IdRegistry is built on top of PaxosDB, a fault-tolerant key-value store that uses the Paxos consensus algorithm to synchronously replicate state across multiple datacenter replicas. Each replica maintains an identical in-memory key-value store constructed by replaying the same sequence of submitted values. At any instant, at most one replica is the Paxos master; only the master can submit updates. If the master fails, PaxosDB automatically elects a new master. This guarantees that even if one or two datacenters go dark, the IdRegistry remains available and consistent.

The IdRegistry stores event_ids as keys and, crucially, also stores a token for each entry — a globally unique identifier consisting of the joiner's server IP, process ID, and a timestamp. This token solves a subtle problem: what happens if a joiner successfully registers a click_id in the IdRegistry but then crashes before writing the joined event to the output log? The click_id is now registered as "done" but the output is missing the click. The token allows a verification system to detect this: if the click_id is in the IdRegistry but the corresponding joined event is absent from the output, the system can check whether the token's joiner (identified by IP and process ID) is still running. If not, it deletes the IdRegistry entry and re-injects the click for reprocessing — without causing duplicates, because only one token can ever hold a given click_id at a time.

Pause and Reflect — The Atomicity Gap

There is an inherent atomicity gap between two actions: writing the click_id to the IdRegistry, and writing the joined click event to the output log. In a single-machine system, you would wrap both in a transaction. Across a distributed system with two different storage systems (PaxosDB and the log file system), you cannot. Photon bridges this gap by making the first action (IdRegistry write) the canonical commitment point, and making the second action (log write) recoverable via the token mechanism. If the log write fails, the token allows reconstruction. This is a beautiful example of reducing a hard atomicity requirement to a weaker idempotency requirement.

5. Scaling the IdRegistry

The Paxos protocol is inherently high-latency across geographic regions. A Paxos commit requires the master to send a proposal to all replicas and wait for acknowledgments from a majority. If the IdRegistry replicas are in different US geographic regions — say east coast and west coast — the round-trip time for one commit can exceed 100 ms. This would limit throughput to fewer than 10 transactions per second per IdRegistry shard, orders of magnitude below Photon's requirements of tens of thousands of events per second.

Two complementary techniques address this: server-side batching and sharding.

Definition — Server-Side Batching

Each IdRegistry server shard has a single background thread — the registry thread — that continuously dequeues incoming RPC requests from an in-memory queue, batches them together, and submits them as a single Paxos commit. Multiple client requests are combined into one large PaxosDB transaction. The registry thread also performs application-level conflict resolution within a batch: if two requests try to insert the same click_id, it resolves the conflict locally before submitting to Paxos, returning success to one client and failure to the other. This batching dramatically increases throughput without increasing per-event latency, because the registry thread processes requests as fast as they arrive.

Definition — Sharding with Timestamp-Based Routing

Events with different click_ids are processed independently — no event depends on another's outcome. Photon exploits this by partitioning the click_id space across many IdRegistry server shards. Each click_id is deterministically assigned to a shard by hashing. Each shard is an independent PaxosDB instance, so shards process events in parallel without coordination. To allow the number of shards to change dynamically (as traffic grows), Photon uses a timestamp-based sharding configuration: the configuration specifies a future timestamp $t_2$ at which the new shard count takes effect. Events with timestamps before $t_2$ use the old shard count; events from $t_2$ onward use the new count. This ensures that the shard assignment for any given event_id is always deterministic, preventing duplicates during resharding.

The IdRegistry also implements garbage collection. Since click events older than $N$ days cannot be joined (their query records have expired), the IdRegistry deletes entries older than $N$ days. The garbage collection boundary timestamp is maintained consistently across all shards using a background thread that periodically updates the boundary using TrueTime, Google's globally synchronized clock service. The boundary is monotonically non-decreasing, preventing races.

6. Single-Datacenter Pipeline

Within a single datacenter, the Photon pipeline consists of three components: the dispatcher, the joiner, and the EventStore. These components work together to join incoming click events with their corresponding query events.

Definition — The Dispatcher

The dispatcher is the entry point for click events. It continuously monitors the directory where click log files are written, reads new events as they arrive, and feeds them to joiners. The dispatcher maintains persistent per-file state on local GFS (Google File System): which files it has seen, and the next byte offset to read in each file. This state survives dispatcher restarts and ensures no click event is skipped.

The dispatcher's most important optimization is a pre-flight IdRegistry lookup: before sending a click to a joiner, the dispatcher checks whether the click_id already exists in the IdRegistry. If it does, the click has already been joined (probably by the other datacenter's pipeline), and the dispatcher discards it without involving a joiner. This eliminates the majority of duplicated work, since both datacenters process overlapping click streams.

Definition — The Joiner

The joiner is a stateless RPC server that performs the actual join logic. When the dispatcher sends a click, the joiner: (1) extracts the query_id from the click; (2) sends an asynchronous RPC to the EventStore to look up the query; (3) if the query is found, passes both records to an adapter function that merges them into a joined event; (4) attempts to register the click_id in the IdRegistry; (5) if successful, writes the joined event to the output log. Joiners are stateless — any joiner can handle any click, so the dispatcher uses RPC-based load balancing to distribute clicks evenly. If a joiner fails mid-process, the dispatcher retries by sending the click to a different joiner.

The retry logic at the dispatcher uses exponential backoff. If a click cannot be joined — because the query is not yet in the EventStore — the dispatcher retries with increasing delays. A click is marked as unjoinable only after a threshold number of retries and if the click is old enough that the query is unlikely ever to arrive. In practice, unjoinable events represent a very small fraction of all events.

Intuition — Statelessness as a Reliability Strategy

The joiner's statelessness is a deliberate reliability choice. In an earlier version of Photon's architecture, the joiner and the EventStore were combined in one component, and workers had affinity for specific clicks (because random disk seeks were needed to find query log files). This affinity meant that a joiner failure stalled all the clicks it was responsible for. By making joiners stateless and building the CacheEventStore for fast in-memory lookups, any joiner can handle any click. The loss of one joiner is invisible — the dispatcher simply retries at a different joiner.

7. Multi-Datacenter Fault Tolerance

The key insight enabling datacenter-level fault tolerance is simple: run the same Photon pipeline independently in multiple datacenters simultaneously. Each datacenter has its own dispatcher, joiner pool, and EventStore. Both pipelines read from local copies of the click and query logs (which are replicated to multiple datacenters by GFS). Both pipelines try to join every click. The IdRegistry — shared across datacenters via Paxos — ensures that even if both pipelines succeed in joining the same click, only one of them can register the click_id and write it to output.

Example — Surviving a Datacenter Disaster

Figure 9 of the paper plots the join throughput of two datacenter pipelines (DC1 and DC2) over a period that includes a real datacenter disaster. When both DCs are healthy, each handles roughly half the total events. When DC1 goes down (a sharp drop to zero), DC2 automatically assumes the full load — within the time it takes the dispatcher to notice and begin retrying. No manual operations are required. When DC1 comes back, its dispatcher reads the IdRegistry to identify which clicks have already been joined by DC2 and skips them. This catch-up is fast because most clicks are discarded at the pre-flight IdRegistry check, not sent to joiners.

The IdRegistry deployment uses five Paxos replicas in three geographic regions of the US. The replicas are configured such that a Paxos commit succeeds when three of five replicas acknowledge — meaning the system can tolerate one entire geographic region going down without impacting IdRegistry availability. The round-trip latency between replicas in adjacent regions is under 100 ms, allowing the IdRegistry to sustain the required throughput with server-side batching.

8. The EventStore

The EventStore is the component that answers the question: "Given a query_id from a click event, what was the original query?" It must handle lookups at high throughput and low latency, even though query log files are large, numerous, and not indexed by query_id in their raw form.

Photon implements two EventStore variants, used in tandem. The CacheEventStore is an in-memory distributed hash table (similar in spirit to Memcached) keyed by query_id. A reader process continuously reads query log files sequentially and populates the cache. Since clicks typically occur within seconds to minutes after their query, and since the cache stores several minutes of recent queries, the vast majority of click lookups are satisfied from the cache without touching disk.

When the CacheEventStore misses — for delayed clicks or during cache catch-up — the request falls through to the LogsEventStore. This component maintains a persistent log file map in Bigtable: a mapping from query_id (represented as a structured key encoding ServerIP, ProcessID, and Timestamp) to the byte offset in the query log file where that event is located. The log file map allows LogsEventStore to seek directly to the approximate location in the log file and read a small amount of data sequentially to find the exact event. Entries are written to the log file map by the same reader process that populates the CacheEventStore, at regular intervals. This design converts expensive random seeks on large log files into short sequential reads.

Intuition — The Two-Level EventStore Design

Think of the CacheEventStore and LogsEventStore as a two-level hierarchy analogous to CPU cache and main memory. The CacheEventStore (fast, volatile, small) handles the common case: recent queries that correspond to clicks happening now. The LogsEventStore (slower, persistent, large) handles the uncommon case: old or delayed queries. By tuning the cache size to hold several minutes of query data, Photon keeps the LogsEventStore request rate nearly flat even as click throughput grows — the cache hit rate improves as volume increases, because more clicks fall within the cache's temporal window.

9. Performance Results

Photon has been in production for more than a year at the time of the paper and has survived multiple planned and unplanned datacenter outages without impacting end-to-end latency. The production deployment has the following characteristics:

End-to-end latency. The paper reports that 90% of events are joined in less than 7 seconds. This is measured as the time from when the click event is logged to when the joined click event is written to the output log. The latency is stable over a 30-day measurement period and does not degrade during datacenter outages.

Datacenter outage behavior. When one datacenter suffers a disaster, the other datacenter seamlessly takes over full processing. The combined join throughput from both datacenters remains essentially flat during the transition. The dispatcher in the recovering datacenter catches up quickly from the backlog because the pre-flight IdRegistry check discards most events that the other datacenter already processed — reducing the effective work of catch-up to a small fraction of total event volume.

IdRegistry batching effectiveness. Figure 10 of the paper shows the throughput of a single IdRegistry shard with and without server-side batching. Without batching, the shard handles a few hundred client write requests per second. With batching, throughput increases by roughly a factor of 30, reaching hundreds of PaxosDB transactions per second. The key metric here is that the shard's Paxos commit rate stays low (6–12 transactions per second) even as it handles far more client requests, because many client requests are collapsed into each Paxos commit.

Scale numbers. At peak, Photon processes millions of events per minute, consumes terabytes of click log data per day and tens of terabytes of query log data per day, and runs more than a thousand IdRegistry shards per datacenter with thousands of dispatchers and joiners.

10. Design Lessons

The paper closes with a section on design lessons, which are unusually candid and transferable to other systems. Several deserve special attention.

Lesson 1 — Minimize Critical State

To build a scalable distributed system on Paxos, you need to minimize the critical state managed by the system and restrict it to metadata only. Writing data through Paxos is expensive because it requires synchronous replication across wide-area network links. In Photon, the only data managed by Paxos is the set of event_ids in the IdRegistry — pure metadata. The actual event data (queries and clicks) is handled by GFS and Bigtable, which are designed for high-throughput bulk data. This separation of concerns is the key to Photon's scalability.

Lesson 2 — Dynamic Resharding Must Be First-Class

The IdRegistry is the central bottleneck of the system. If the number of shards is fixed, the system's maximum throughput is fixed. Photon's timestamp-based sharding mechanism allows the shard count to increase at any time without violating the determinism properties required for correctness. This was not an afterthought — the authors emphasize that dynamic resharding must be a first-class design consideration, not a feature bolted on after deployment.

Lesson 3 — Batching Across Streams Has Asymmetric Costs

Some stream processing frameworks recommend batching events together to improve throughput. This works when all events in a batch can be committed atomically. In Photon's case, a click can only be committed once its corresponding query is available. If you batch 100 clicks together, and one of them is waiting for a delayed query, the entire batch is blocked. Photon instead processes clicks individually and relies on server-side batching at the IdRegistry layer (where all events are independent of each other). The lesson: batching is only safe when all events in a batch have the same dependencies.

11. Impact and Legacy

Photon represents one of the first production deployments of a geo-distributed stream joining system with exactly-once guarantees and automatic datacenter-level fault tolerance. Prior systems either required all events to be sorted by a shared timestamp (making them incompatible with the delayed primary stream problem), operated within a single datacenter, or required human intervention to recover from large-scale outages.

The architectural pattern Photon pioneered — using a Paxos-backed deduplication registry as the coordination backbone, with independent pipelines per datacenter providing redundancy — has influenced subsequent streaming systems. The insight that exactly-once semantics can be implemented by externalizing state into a consistent store (rather than building it into the stream processor itself) reappears in Apache Kafka's exactly-once delivery guarantees (introduced in 2017) and in frameworks like Apache Flink's state backends. The IdRegistry is conceptually a precursor to the idempotency keys and transactional outbox patterns widely used in modern distributed systems.

From a practical standpoint, Photon directly powers Google's advertiser billing. Every click on a Google advertisement, if it generates revenue, passes through a system architecturally descended from what this paper describes. At the scale of Google's advertising business — which generates well over 100 billion dollars in annual revenue — correctness and availability of this joining pipeline are not academic concerns.
