Scientific Paper · SIGMOD 2015

k-Shape: Efficient and Accurate Clustering of Time Series

John Paparrizos, Luis Gravano ACM SIGMOD 2015 · Melbourne, Victoria, Australia
Abstract Time-series data is everywhere — ECG readings, stock prices, sensor streams, audio waveforms — and clustering is among the most important tools for making sense of it. But most clustering methods for time series suffer a painful trade-off: methods based on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) are accurate but extremely slow, while methods using Euclidean distance are fast but miss sequences that are similar in shape yet differ in phase or amplitude. k-Shape proposes a different approach rooted in cross-correlation: it defines a shape-based distance (SBD) that is scale- and shift-invariant without requiring any parameter tuning, and it derives a novel centroid computation method (shape extraction) consistent with that distance. The resulting algorithm runs in time linear in the number of sequences, outperforms all scalable clustering algorithms in accuracy across 48 benchmark datasets, and achieves accuracy comparable to PAM+cDTW while being two orders of magnitude faster.

1. Clustering Time Series by Shape

What does it mean for two time series to be "similar"? Consider two ECG recordings of the same patient's heartbeat taken on different days. The overall shape — a sharp spike, then a drop, then a gentle rise — is nearly identical. But one recording might start its measurement window a few milliseconds earlier (a phase shift), and the two recordings might use different voltage scales (an amplitude difference). A human cardiologist would immediately recognize them as the same heartbeat pattern. A simple Euclidean distance — measuring point-by-point differences — would declare them very different.

The clustering problem for time series is: given $n$ sequences and a target number of clusters $k$, partition the sequences so that sequences with similar shapes land in the same cluster, regardless of phase, amplitude, or baseline offset. The standard objective (minimizing within-cluster sum of squared distances) is:

$$P^* = \arg\min_P \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{\vec{x}_i \in p_j} \text{dist}(\vec{x}_i, \vec{c}_j)^2$$

where $\vec{c}_j$ is the centroid of cluster $p_j$. This is NP-hard for $k \geq 2$ in general, so heuristics (like k-means' iterative refinement) are used to find a local optimum.

Intuition — Why k-means fails on raw time series

Standard k-means uses Euclidean distance (ED) and computes centroids as arithmetic means. Neither is appropriate for shape-based time-series clustering. ED compares sequences point-by-point, so even a tiny phase shift (a sequence shifted one step to the right) can produce a huge distance. And the arithmetic mean of shifted sequences produces a blurry, averaged-out waveform that does not look like any of the actual shapes — a phenomenon well known in signal processing. The paper's key challenge is replacing both distance and centroid computation simultaneously, in a consistent way.

2. Distortions and Invariances

Before designing a distance measure, the paper catalogues the distortions that time-series sequences commonly exhibit:

Scaling and translation. Two sequences may have the same shape but different amplitudes (scaling: multiply by $a$) and different baselines (translation: add $b$). For example, temperature sensors in Celsius and Fahrenheit record the same physical signal. A distance measure invariant to these transforms can identify the underlying pattern regardless of units.

Shift invariance. Two sequences may record the same event but start their measurement window at different times. Heartbeats start at different points in the cardiac cycle. Speech sounds may be time-aligned differently across recordings. A shift-invariant distance should treat these as similar.

The standard approach to removing scaling and translation is z-normalization: subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation so that every sequence has mean $0$ and standard deviation $1$. After z-normalization, a sequence $\vec{x}$ becomes:

$$\vec{x}' = \frac{\vec{x} - \mu}{\sigma}$$

where $\mu$ is the mean and $\sigma$ is the standard deviation. The remaining distortion to handle is phase shift, which k-Shape addresses through cross-correlation.

3. Cross-Correlation and SBD

Cross-correlation is a classical signal-processing tool that measures the similarity between two signals as one is slid over the other. Given two sequences $\vec{x} = (x_1, \ldots, x_m)$ and $\vec{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_m)$, a shift of $s$ applied to $\vec{x}$ produces:

$$\vec{x}_{(s)} = \begin{cases} (0,\ldots,0,x_1,\ldots,x_{m-s}), & s \geq 0 \\ (x_{1-s},\ldots,x_m, 0,\ldots,0), & s < 0 \end{cases}$$

The cross-correlation sequence $CC_w(\vec{x}, \vec{y})$ is the length-$(2m-1)$ sequence of inner products between $\vec{y}$ (fixed) and each shift of $\vec{x}$:

$$R_k(\vec{x},\vec{y}) = \begin{cases} \sum_{l=1}^{m-k} x_{l+k} \cdot y_l, & k \geq 0 \\ R_{-k}(\vec{y},\vec{x}), & k < 0 \end{cases}$$

The value of $CC_w$ at position $w$ answers: "how similar is $\vec{x}$ to $\vec{y}$ when $\vec{x}$ is shifted by $s = w - m$ steps?" The optimal shift is the $w$ that maximizes this.

Definition — Shape-Based Distance (SBD)

To make the cross-correlation invariant to scaling, the paper uses the coefficient normalization $NCC_c$, which divides the cross-correlation by the geometric mean of the autocorrelations at lag 0:

$$NCC_c(\vec{x}, \vec{y}) = \frac{CC_w(\vec{x},\vec{y})}{\sqrt{R_0(\vec{x},\vec{x}) \cdot R_0(\vec{y},\vec{y})}}$$

This normalization produces values in $[-1, 1]$. The shape-based distance is then:

$$SBD(\vec{x}, \vec{y}) = 1 - \max_w \left( NCC_c(\vec{x}, \vec{y}) \right)$$

SBD takes values in $[0, 2]$, with 0 indicating perfect shape similarity. It is shift-invariant because we maximize over all shifts, and scale/translation-invariant because we z-normalize before computing.

Intuition — What SBD is really asking

SBD asks: "Among all ways to slide one sequence over the other, what is the best alignment?" Then it converts that best alignment score into a distance. If two sequences have exactly the same shape but are shifted by 5 time steps, SBD will shift one sequence by 5 and find perfect overlap, returning distance 0. Euclidean distance would find a large distance because it compares the wrong pairs of points.

4. Efficient Computation via FFT

The naive computation of $CC_w(\vec{x}, \vec{y})$ for all values of $w$ requires $O(m^2)$ time, where $m$ is the sequence length. For long sequences (thousands of time steps), this is prohibitively slow.

The key insight is that cross-correlation is equivalent to convolution, and the convolution theorem states that convolution in the time domain equals pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain. So the full cross-correlation sequence can be computed as:

$$CC(\vec{x}, \vec{y}) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left\{\mathcal{F}(\vec{x}) \cdot \overline{\mathcal{F}(\vec{y})}\right\}$$

where $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) and the bar denotes complex conjugate. Using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), both the forward and inverse DFTs cost only $O(m \log m)$, so the overall SBD computation reduces from $O(m^2)$ to $O(m \log m)$. For $m = 1{,}000$, that is roughly a $100\times$ speedup.

Implementation detail — Padding to power-of-two length

FFT is fastest when the input length is a power of two. When computing $CC(\vec{x}, \vec{y})$, the output has length $2m - 1$. The paper pads both sequences with zeros to reach the next power of two above $2m - 1$ before applying the FFT. This does not change the mathematical result (zero-padding does not alter correlation values) but can dramatically speed up the FFT computation in practice.

5. Shape Extraction: Finding the Centroid

A clustering algorithm needs not just a distance measure but also a way to compute a centroid — a representative sequence for a cluster. For standard Euclidean distance, the arithmetic mean works perfectly: it minimizes the sum of squared Euclidean distances. But for SBD, the arithmetic mean is wrong: averaging sequences that have been aligned to different phases produces a blurry mixture that does not represent any real shape.

The shape extraction algorithm solves this correctly. It casts centroid computation as an optimization problem: find the sequence $\vec{\mu}_k^*$ that maximizes the sum of squared $NCC_c$ values to all sequences in the cluster:

$$\vec{\mu}_k^* = \mathop{\arg\max}_{\vec{\mu}_k} \sum_{\vec{x}_i \in P_k} \left(\frac{\max_w CC_w(\vec{x}_i, \vec{\mu}_k)}{\sqrt{R_0(\vec{x}_i,\vec{x}_i)\cdot R_0(\vec{\mu}_k,\vec{\mu}_k)}}\right)^2$$

After z-normalizing the sequences, the paper shows this simplifies to maximizing a Rayleigh Quotient:

$$\vec{\mu}_k^* = \mathop{\arg\max}_{\vec{\mu}_k} \frac{\vec{\mu}_k^T \cdot M \cdot \vec{\mu}_k}{\vec{\mu}_k^T \cdot \vec{\mu}_k}$$

where $M = Q^T \cdot S \cdot Q$ is a real symmetric matrix constructed from the aligned sequences (with $S = \sum_{\vec{x}_i \in P_k} \vec{x}_i \cdot \vec{x}_i^T$ and $Q = I - \frac{1}{m}O$ a centering matrix). The maximizer of the Rayleigh Quotient is the eigenvector of $M$ corresponding to its largest eigenvalue.

Intuition — Why eigenvectors give the best centroid

The Rayleigh Quotient $\vec{\mu}^T M \vec{\mu} / \vec{\mu}^T \vec{\mu}$ is maximized by the principal eigenvector of $M$ — this is a classical result in linear algebra. Intuitively, the principal eigenvector of the "scatter matrix" of aligned sequences captures the direction of maximum variance, which corresponds to the shared shape. The paper's Algorithm 2 computes this by extracting the first eigenvector of $M$ after aligning all sequences to the current centroid estimate. This is far more principled than taking the arithmetic mean.

6. The k-Shape Algorithm

With SBD and shape extraction in hand, k-Shape is simply an iterative refinement algorithm in the spirit of k-means, but using these two components instead of Euclidean distance and arithmetic mean. The algorithm repeats until cluster assignments stabilize or the iteration limit (100) is reached:

Algorithm — k-Shape (one iteration)

Refinement step: For each cluster $j \in [1,k]$, collect all sequences currently assigned to cluster $j$ and run ShapeExtraction (Algorithm 2) to compute the new centroid $C(j)$.

Assignment step: For each sequence $\vec{x}_i$, compute $SBD(C(j), \vec{x}_i)$ for all $k$ centroids and assign $\vec{x}_i$ to the cluster with the smallest SBD.

The complexity of one iteration is dominated by two costs. The assignment step computes $n \cdot k$ SBD values, each costing $O(m \log m)$, for total $O(n \cdot k \cdot m \log m)$. The refinement step computes one eigendecomposition of an $m \times m$ matrix per cluster, costing $O(k \cdot m^3)$. In practice $m \ll n$, so the assignment step dominates, giving overall complexity $O(n \cdot k \cdot m \log m)$ per iteration — linear in the number of sequences.

Pause and check — What makes k-Shape special?

Many previous approaches modified either the distance measure (replacing ED with DTW or cDTW) or the centroid computation (replacing arithmetic mean with DBA) — but not both consistently. k-Shape is the first algorithm that derives both the distance (SBD) and the centroid computation (shape extraction via Rayleigh Quotient) from the same underlying mathematical object — the cross-correlation — and that does so efficiently using the FFT. The consistency of distance and centroid is what produces well-separated, homogeneous clusters.

7. Experimental Results

The authors evaluate k-Shape on all 48 publicly available class-labeled time-series datasets from the UCR repository, spanning domains from astronomy to biology to finance. Sequence lengths range from 24 to 1,882 time steps; dataset sizes from 56 to 9,236 sequences. All methods are evaluated on a cluster of 10 servers running for two months continuously.

The evaluation metric is the Rand Index — the fraction of sequence pairs that are either in the same cluster and the same class, or in different clusters and different classes. Higher is better. The 1-nearest-neighbor classification accuracy is used for evaluating the distance measure SBD alone.

Key findings for the SBD distance measure: SBD is statistically equivalent to cDTW (the best-performing DTW variant) in accuracy, while being 73× to 3533× faster depending on the DTW variant. For k-Shape as a complete clustering algorithm: it outperforms all scalable methods (k-means variants) with statistical significance and achieves a mean Rand Index of 0.772 versus 0.745 for k-AVG+SBD (the next best scalable method). Among non-scalable methods (which compute full dissimilarity matrices and cannot scale to large datasets), only PAM+cDTW achieves similar accuracy, but it requires tuning cDTW's warping window and is 313× to 4175× slower than k-Shape.

Example — ECG dataset result

On the ECGFiveDays dataset (ECG recordings on different days), k-Shape achieves a 1-NN classification accuracy of 98.9% — dramatically higher than cDTW's 79.7%. This dataset exemplifies the case where k-Shape excels: sequences that are nearly identical in shape but recorded with different phase alignments, where cross-correlation-based alignment vastly outperforms point-by-point comparison.

8. Impact

k-Shape established cross-correlation as a first-class citizen in time-series data mining. Before this paper, the field had largely polarized into two camps: researchers who used DTW (accurate but slow, requiring tuning) and practitioners who used ED (fast but inaccurate on shifted sequences). k-Shape showed that a third path was available — one grounded in classical signal processing, free of parameters, and computationally efficient via the FFT.

The SBD distance and shape extraction centroid have been incorporated into several time-series analysis toolkits and are frequently cited as the baseline for domain-independent time-series clustering. The paper's experimental methodology — rigorous evaluation over all 48 UCR datasets with statistical testing using Friedman and Nemenyi tests — set a new standard for empirical rigor in the time-series community. A key lesson: it is not enough to show your method works on a few cherry-picked datasets; you need to demonstrate systematic superiority and quantify statistical significance.
