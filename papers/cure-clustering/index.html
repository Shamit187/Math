CURE: An Efficient Clustering Algorithm for Large Databases

Sudipto Guha, Rajeev Rastogi, Kyuseok Shim Proceedings of ACM SIGMOD, Seattle, WA, 1998
Abstract Clustering is one of the oldest tasks in data mining: group similar points together, separate dissimilar ones. But by 1998 the dominant algorithms had accumulated two well-known failure modes. Partitional algorithms (like k-means) assume clusters are roughly spherical and similarly sized — they break down on elongated or irregular shapes. Hierarchical algorithms are more flexible in shape but are often fragile in the presence of outliers and too slow for large databases. CURE — Clustering Using REpresentatives — is designed to overcome both weaknesses simultaneously. Each cluster is represented not by a single centroid but by a fixed number of well-scattered representative points that are then shrunk toward the cluster's mean by a fraction $\alpha$. The scattered points capture complex geometry; the shrinking dampens the influence of outliers. For large databases, CURE adds a pipeline of random sampling and partitioning that reduces computation from $O(n^2 \log n)$ for the full dataset to a fixed cost over a manageable sample — without sacrificing cluster quality.

1. Introduction

Clustering has a deceptively simple goal: given $n$ data points in a $d$-dimensional metric space, partition them into $k$ groups such that points within each group are more similar to each other than to points in other groups. The difficulty is that "similar" and "group" are not defined by the problem statement — they must be encoded in the algorithm's design choices.

The paper's motivating setting is large corporate databases — retail transaction logs, financial data, telecom records — where $n$ can be hundreds of thousands or millions of points, clusters can be elongated or irregularly shaped, and the data almost certainly contains noise (outlier) points that belong to no meaningful cluster at all. A clustering algorithm that works on clean, spherical toy data is not the same as an algorithm that works on this kind of data.

2. What Traditional Algorithms Get Wrong

To understand what CURE is fixing, it helps to see the two dominant paradigms and their failure modes.

Definition — Square-error (partitional) clustering

Minimize the total within-cluster squared distance to the cluster mean: $$E = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{p \in C_i} \|p - m_i\|^2$$ where $m_i$ is the mean of cluster $C_i$. This favors compact, spherical clusters of similar size.

The square-error criterion has a well-known pathology with unequal cluster sizes. Suppose your data has one large cluster and two small ones. The square-error objective can lower its value by splitting the large cluster into three pieces and merging it with the small ones, because the slight reduction in within-cluster variance in the large cluster is mathematically more valuable (it is weighted by many points) than the damage done to the small clusters. The algorithm is optimizing a mathematical objective that diverges from the actual goal.

Definition — Hierarchical clustering distance measures

Three common ways to measure the distance between two clusters $C_i$ and $C_j$:

  • $d_\text{mean}(C_i, C_j) = \|m_i - m_j\|$ — distance between centroids
  • $d_\text{ave}(C_i, C_j) = \frac{1}{n_i n_j} \sum_{p \in C_i} \sum_{p' \in C_j} \|p - p'\|$ — average pairwise distance
  • $d_\text{min}(C_i, C_j) = \min_{p \in C_i, p' \in C_j} \|p - p'\|$ — nearest-neighbor distance

Centroid-based distance ($d_\text{mean}$) inherits the spherical-cluster assumption — it works fine when clusters are compact but goes wrong for elongated shapes, which it tends to split. Nearest-neighbor distance ($d_\text{min}$) is great for non-spherical shapes but suffers from the "chaining effect": a sparse string of outlier points connecting two genuine clusters acts as a bridge, causing the algorithm to merge them prematurely. Both extremes are unsatisfying.

Intuition — CURE's middle ground

CURE's representative-point approach sits between the two extremes. A single centroid is too compact — it loses all shape information. Tracking all points is too sensitive — a single outlier near the boundary causes trouble. A fixed number of well-scattered points captures the cluster's geometry without letting any single rogue point dominate. Shrinking those points toward the centroid then softens the influence of points that ended up scattered far from the cluster core, which are typically outliers.

3. The CURE Algorithm

CURE is a hierarchical agglomerative clustering algorithm. It starts with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the closest pair of clusters until only $k$ clusters remain. The distinctive feature is how it defines "closest pair": not by the distance between centroids or nearest neighbors, but by the distance between the clusters' representative points.

The distance between two clusters $u$ and $v$ is:

$$\text{dist}(u, v) = \min_{p \in u.\text{rep},\; q \in v.\text{rep}} \text{dist}(p, q)$$

where $u.\text{rep}$ and $v.\text{rep}$ are the sets of representative points for clusters $u$ and $v$ respectively. This is the same structure as nearest-neighbor distance, but now "nearest neighbor" means the nearest of the representative points — not the nearest of all points. This gives flexibility without the full fragility of $d_\text{min}$.

Definition — Representative Points and Shrinking

For a cluster $u$ with mean $u.\text{mean}$, the $c$ representative points are chosen iteratively:

  1. The first representative is the point in $u$ farthest from the mean.
  2. Each subsequent representative is the point farthest from the set of already-chosen representatives.
  3. Each chosen point $p$ is then shrunk: its stored position becomes $p + \alpha \cdot (u.\text{mean} - p)$, moving it a fraction $\alpha$ of the way toward the mean.
The result is $c$ points spread across the cluster's extent, each pulled slightly inward. $\alpha = 0$ gives the original scattered points; $\alpha = 1$ collapses all representatives to the mean (recovering centroid-based clustering).

4. Representative Points and Shrinking

Why does shrinking work? The key intuition is about outliers. An outlier is a point that happens to be near a cluster but does not truly belong to it. When choosing the farthest-first scattered points, outliers tend to be selected — they are, by definition, far from the cluster core. Without shrinking ($\alpha = 0$), these outlier representatives sit at the fringe and make the cluster look larger than it is, causing inappropriate merges with neighboring clusters. After shrinking, outlier representatives move significantly toward the center (because they are far from the mean, the shift $\alpha \cdot (\text{mean} - p)$ is large), while inlier representatives barely move. The shrinking is a simple, elegant way to downweight extremes without explicitly identifying them.

The paper experimentally finds that $\alpha$ in the range 0.2 to 0.7 consistently identifies the correct clusters on their test datasets, regardless of the exact value. Very small $\alpha$ (near zero) degenerates toward the all-points approach and is sensitive to outliers. Large $\alpha$ (near one) degenerates toward centroid-based clustering and fails on elongated clusters.

Example — Elongated clusters

Consider a dataset with two elongated ellipsoidal clusters connected by a thin chain of outlier points. With centroid-based distance (BIRCH), the centroids of both ellipsoids are pulled toward their respective long axes, and the algorithm merges them prematurely. With nearest-neighbor distance, the chain of outlier points acts as a bridge and the two ellipsoids merge. CURE with $\alpha = 0.3$ and $c = 10$ correctly separates them: the representatives capture the geometry (so the clusters look elongated, not spherical), and the shrinking dampens the chain effect (the outlier points in the chain, now shrunk inward, are no longer as close to the other cluster's representatives).

5. Scaling to Large Databases

The hierarchical clustering algorithm described in Section 3 has time complexity $O(n^2 \log n)$ for general dimensionality and $O(n^2)$ for low dimensionality. For databases of hundreds of thousands of points, this is prohibitively slow. CURE addresses this with a pipeline:

  1. Random sampling. Draw a random sample $S$ of size $s$ from the full database. The sample is the input to the clustering algorithm. Chernoff bounds guarantee that for an appropriate sample size, with high probability the sample contains at least $f \cdot |u|$ points from every cluster $u$ — that is, a constant fraction of each cluster's members.
  2. Partitioning. Divide the sample into $p$ partitions of size $s/p$. Partially cluster each partition until the number of clusters in each partition reduces to $n/(pq)$ for a constant $q$.
  3. Outlier elimination (first pass). After partial clustering, discard very small clusters (those with 1 or 2 points).
  4. Final clustering. Run a second pass to cluster the partial clusters from all partitions into the final $k$ clusters.
  5. Labeling data on disk. The final $k$ clusters have representative points. Scan the full database and assign each point to the cluster whose representatives are closest.

The partitioning step reduces the time complexity of the first clustering pass from $O\!\left(\tfrac{n^2}{p^2}\cdot\tfrac{q-1}{q}\cdot\log\tfrac{n}{p}\right)$ — an improvement factor of approximately $\frac{q-1}{pq} + \frac{1}{q^2}$ over clustering without partitioning. With $p = 5$ partitions and $q = 3$, the speedup relative to the naïve single-pass approach is substantial.

Theorem 4.1 — Sample size bound

For a cluster $u$, if the sample size $s$ satisfies: $$s \geq fN + \frac{N}{|u|}\!\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right) + \frac{N}{|u|}\sqrt{\!\left(\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)^2 + 2f|u|\log\frac{1}{\delta}}$$ then the probability that the sample contains fewer than $f|u|$ points belonging to cluster $u$ is less than $\delta$.

6. Handling Outliers

Outlier detection is built into CURE's pipeline at two points. In the first pass (after partially clustering each partition), any cluster containing only 1 or 2 points is classified as an outlier cluster and discarded. This works because outlier points, by definition, merge slowly — they are far from other points — and as a result accumulate few neighbors during the early hierarchical merging steps.

Near the end of the clustering (when few clusters remain), a second outlier elimination pass is run. At this stage, very small groups are again removed. The paper acknowledges that the exact fraction of initial clusters at which the first elimination triggers is dataset-dependent; empirically, a fraction of around $1/3$ of the initial clusters works well.

Random sampling also passively helps with outliers: a random sample drawn from a dataset with sparse outliers will, with high probability, include very few outlier points (since they make up a small fraction of the whole). The effects of outliers are therefore doubly dampened: they are underrepresented in the sample, and those that do appear tend to form small singleton clusters that are eliminated.

7. Complexity

The worst-case time complexity of CURE's hierarchical clustering algorithm on input of size $n$ is $O(n^2 \log n)$. For low-dimensional data (two dimensions), this reduces to $O(n^2)$. The space complexity is $O(n)$ because both the heap and the kd-tree require linear space.

When CURE is run on a random sample of size $s$ (rather than the full dataset of size $n$), the time complexity of the clustering phase is $O(s^2 \log s)$ and the labeling phase is $O(n)$. Since $s$ is a fixed fraction of the dataset (around 2.5% in the paper's experiments), the clustering phase cost is essentially constant as $n$ grows, while the labeling phase grows linearly — giving CURE an overall near-linear scaling behaviour for the total pipeline.

8. Experimental Results

Experiments used two datasets of 100,000 points each. Dataset 1 contains one large cluster, two small circles, and two ellipsoids connected by a chain of outliers — specifically designed to stress-test algorithms. Dataset 2 contains 100 equal-size clusters arranged on a grid, matching BIRCH's strong case.

Key findings on clustering quality:

  • BIRCH fails to identify Dataset 1's clusters: it splits the large cluster and merges the ellipsoids, because it uses centroid-based distances and the centroids of the ellipsoids happen to be close to the large cluster's centroid.
  • The MST method (all-points, nearest-neighbor distance) merges both ellipsoids into one cluster because the chain of outlier points bridges them.
  • CURE with default parameters ($s = 2500,\; c = 10,\; \alpha = 0.3$) correctly finds all five clusters in Dataset 1.
  • On Dataset 2 (compact equal-size clusters, BIRCH's favorable case), CURE also clusters correctly, confirming it does not sacrifice performance on easy cases.

Key findings on execution time:

  • CURE's execution time is always lower than BIRCH's for the same dataset, even though BIRCH was specifically designed for scalability.
  • As the number of points grows from 100,000 to 500,000, CURE's execution time grows slowly (since its sample size stays fixed at 2500), while BIRCH's time grows much more rapidly because BIRCH scans the entire dataset during preclustering.
  • With 5 partitions, CURE's clustering time on the full data is approximately 20 seconds; BIRCH takes over 120 seconds on the same machine for 500,000 points.

9. Legacy and Impact

CURE's influence spread in two directions. In algorithms, the idea of representing a cluster by multiple scattered points inspired subsequent work on density-based and model-based clustering in high dimensions. The representative-point idea is recognizable in modern spectral clustering (which uses eigenvectors to characterize cluster shape) and in k-medoids variants. The use of Chernoff bounds to justify random sampling as a preprocessing step became standard methodology for large-scale data mining algorithms throughout the 2000s.

In systems, the pipeline of sample → partial cluster → eliminate outliers → full cluster → label-from-disk became a template for scalable unsupervised learning. The key architectural insight — that you do not need to cluster everything to learn the cluster structure — was absorbed into stream clustering algorithms, mini-batch k-means, and eventually online learning frameworks. Scikit-learn's AgglomerativeClustering and HDBSCAN both trace conceptual lineage to this work.

The shrink parameter $\alpha$ is also worth reflecting on as a design philosophy. Rather than trying to explicitly detect and remove outliers before clustering, CURE absorbs outlier robustness directly into the distance measure. This is an early example of a principle that recurs throughout modern machine learning: instead of preprocessing away bad data, design representations and objective functions that are intrinsically less sensitive to it.
