Scientific Paper · SIGMOD 1996

Implementing Data Cubes Efficiently

Venky Harinarayan, Anand Rajaraman, Jeffrey D. Ullman ACM SIGMOD International Conference on Management of Data, Montreal, Canada, 1996
Abstract Decision support applications involve complex queries on very large databases. Since response times should be small, query optimization is critical. Users typically view the data as multidimensional data cubes. Each cell of the data cube is a view consisting of an aggregation of interest, like total sales. The values of many cells are dependent on the values of other cells. A powerful optimization technique is to materialize some or all of these cells rather than compute them from raw data each time. In this paper, we investigate the issue of which cells (views) to materialize when it is too expensive to materialize all views. A lattice framework is used to express dependencies between views. We present greedy algorithms that work off this lattice and determine a good set of views to materialize. The greedy algorithm performs within a small constant factor of optimal. We then consider the most common case of the hypercube lattice and examine the choice of materialized views for hypercubes in detail.

1. Introduction

Decision support systems (DSS) power business intelligence: a retail chain wants to know total sales by product, by region, by week, or by any combination of those dimensions. These aggregation queries are conceptually simple — they are just GROUP-BY queries in SQL — but on a warehouse with hundreds of millions of rows, even a single GROUP-BY can take minutes to hours to compute from scratch. The standard solution is precomputation: materialize the results of the most important queries in advance, so that user queries can be answered by reading a small precomputed table rather than re-scanning billions of raw rows.

The problem is that you cannot precompute everything. With $n$ dimensions, there are $2^n$ possible aggregations. For the TPC-D benchmark with 3 dimensions (part, supplier, customer), that is 8 views — manageable. But commercial warehouses often have 10–20 dimensions, giving $2^{20}$ or more possible views, and each view may itself contain millions of rows. You must choose wisely, and this paper tells you how.

2. The Data Cube and Its Views

Consider the TPC-D benchmark database. There are three dimensions: part, supplier, and customer. The measure of interest is sales: the total sales revenue $SP$ for each transaction. For each cell $(p, s, c)$ in the 3-D data cube, we store the sales of part $p$ bought from supplier $s$ by customer $c$.

Users also want aggregations: what are the total sales of part $p$ across all suppliers? This query corresponds to the cell $(p, ALL, ALL)$, where ALL is a special value meaning "summed over all values of this dimension." Any cell containing an ALL in one or more of its components is a dependent cell: its value can be derived by aggregating other cells of the cube. Cells with no ALL components require looking at the raw data to compute.

Example — The 8 TPC-D Views

With dimensions part (p), supplier (s), customer (c), the 8 possible GROUP-BY views and their approximate sizes in the TPC-D 1 GB database are:

1. GROUP BY part, supplier, customer — 6 million rows (the full cube)
2. GROUP BY part, customer — 6 million rows
3. GROUP BY part, supplier — 0.8 million rows
4. GROUP BY supplier, customer — 6 million rows
5. GROUP BY part — 0.2 million rows
6. GROUP BY supplier — 0.01 million rows
7. GROUP BY customer — 0.1 million rows
8. GROUP BY (none) — 1 row (grand total)

View 1 (the full cube) is always materialized, since no other view can answer queries about individual (part, supplier, customer) triples.

A key observation: view 5 (GROUP BY part) can be answered from view 2 (GROUP BY part, customer) by further aggregating over customers. So if view 2 is materialized, answering a query for view 5 costs only 6 million rows (the size of view 2), not the full raw data scan. But answering view 5 directly from the materialized view 5 costs only 0.2 million rows. This trade-off — how much to materialize to speed up how many queries — is precisely the optimization problem this paper solves.

3. The Lattice Framework

The dependence structure between views forms a mathematical lattice. Formally, let $(L, \preceq)$ be the set of views (queries) with a dependence relation: say $Q_1 \preceq Q_2$ if $Q_1$ can be answered using the results of $Q_2$. We say $Q_1$ is dependent on $Q_2$. A view with fewer grouping attributes is dependent on views with more grouping attributes: for instance, (part) depends on (part, customer), because you can compute total sales per part by summing the already-computed sales per (part, customer) pair.

Definition — Lattice of Views

A collection of views forms a lattice $(L, \preceq)$ if any two elements have a least upper bound (join) and a greatest lower bound (meet) under the $\preceq$ ordering. The top element is the view that groups by all attributes — the full raw data (or the full cube). Any query can be answered from the top element, possibly at great cost. The bottom element is the view that groups by no attributes — the single grand total.

In a hypercube with $n$ attributes, the lattice diagram is literally an $n$-dimensional hypercube: views at rank $i$ group by exactly $i$ attributes, and there are $\binom{n}{i}$ such views. With hierarchical dimensions (e.g., a time dimension organized as day → week → month → year), the lattice becomes the direct product of the individual dimension lattices, capturing the drill-down and roll-up operations familiar to OLAP users.

4. The Cost Model

To answer a query $Q$, the system finds the cheapest materialized view $Q_A$ such that $Q \preceq Q_A$ (i.e., $Q_A$ is an ancestor of $Q$ in the lattice). The paper adopts a linear cost model: the cost of answering $Q$ from $Q_A$ is the number of rows in the table for $Q_A$. This is motivated empirically: the paper shows (Figure 5) that on TPC-D data, the relationship between view size and query time is nearly linear, with formula $T = m \cdot S + c$ where $T$ is running time, $S$ is the size of the view used to answer the query, $c$ is a negligible fixed overhead, and $m$ is a ratio roughly constant across different view sizes.

The goal is to choose a set $S$ of views to materialize (subject to constraints) that minimizes the average query cost — equivalently, maximizes the total benefit of the chosen views.

5. The Greedy Algorithm

The optimization problem — choose the best $k$ views to materialize — is NP-complete in general (by reduction from Set Cover). The authors therefore turn to a greedy heuristic. The algorithm is elegant:

The Greedy Algorithm

S = {top view};
for i = 1 to k do begin
   select the view v not in S such that B(v, S) is maximized;
   S = S ∪ {v};
end
The resulting S is the greedy selection.

The key is the benefit function $B(v, S)$, which measures how much materializing view $v$ reduces the total query cost, given that the views in $S$ are already materialized. For each view $w$ that $v$ can help answer (i.e., $w \preceq v$): let $u$ be the lowest-cost view in $S$ that is an ancestor of $w$. If $C(v)$ (the cost of view $v$) is less than $C(u)$, then materializing $v$ saves $C(u) - C(v)$ for each query that asks for view $w$. The total benefit is summed over all views $w$ that would benefit: $$B(v, S) = \sum_{w \preceq v,\; C(v) < C(u)} \bigl(C(u) - C(v)\bigr)$$

Worked Example — Greedy on a Small Lattice

Consider 8 views (a through h) with space costs as annotated in Figure 7 of the paper. The top view a has cost 100 and is always materialized. We want to pick 3 more views.

Round 1: Compute benefit for each non-selected view. View b has benefit 250 (it reduces the cost of evaluating 5 views, each by 50, from 100 down to 50). b wins. Select b.

Round 2: Recalculate benefits given $S = \{a, b\}$. View f has the highest benefit (70). Select f.

Round 3: View d has the highest remaining benefit (60). Select d.

Final selection: a, b, d, f. This reduces total evaluation cost from 800 (if only a is materialized) to 420 — and this selection happens to be globally optimal for this example.

Applying the greedy algorithm to TPC-D

On the full TPC-D lattice (Figure 10 in the paper), the greedy algorithm selects views in the following order (with total time and space after each selection): first the full cube $cp$ (mandatory), then views ns, nt, c, p — each giving large benefit reductions — then views with diminishing returns. After selecting the first 5 views beyond the top, the total query time is nearly minimized while total space is barely larger than the mandatory top view. This demonstrates vividly that a small number of well-chosen materializations provides most of the possible benefit.

6. Performance Guarantees

The greedy algorithm is not just a heuristic that works well in practice — it comes with a provable approximation guarantee. The authors prove:

Theorem 4.1 — Greedy Approximation Guarantee

For any lattice, let $B_\text{greedy}$ be the benefit of $k$ views chosen by the greedy algorithm, and let $B_\text{opt}$ be the benefit of an optimal set of $k$ views. Then: $$\frac{B_\text{greedy}}{B_\text{opt}} \geq 1 - \left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^k$$ As $k \to \infty$, the ratio $\left(\frac{k-1}{k}\right)^k$ approaches $1/e$, so $B_\text{greedy}/B_\text{opt} \geq 1 - 1/e$ ≈ 63%. This bound is tight: the authors construct lattices showing the ratio can approach $1 - 1/e$ arbitrarily closely.

The proof mirrors the classical analysis of the greedy set cover algorithm. Interestingly, Chekuri (citing Feige 1996) shows this is not merely a proof artifact: no deterministic polynomial-time algorithm can achieve a better approximation ratio than greedy unless P = NP. The greedy algorithm is therefore, in a strong sense, the best possible efficient algorithm for this problem.

Intuition — why 63% and not better?

The bad case for greedy occurs when the lattice has a "star" structure: one expensive but high-benefit view at the top, and many independent chains of views below. The greedy algorithm picks the top view first (correct), then is forced to spread its remaining budget across many chains, getting only one chain perfectly. An optimal algorithm might pick the two best chains. With $k = 2$, this gives a greedy/optimal ratio of $3/4$ in the worst case, and this can approach $1-1/e$ as the chains grow arbitrarily long. In practice, real data cube lattices tend not to be star-shaped, and greedy performs much closer to optimal than the theoretical bound suggests.

7. The Hypercube Lattice

The most common lattice in practice is the hypercube: there are $n$ attributes, each of equal domain size $r$, and views are identified by which subset of attributes they group by. The size of a view that groups by $i$ attributes is approximately $r^i$ when $r^i \geq m$ (where $m$ is the number of raw data points), and approximately $m$ otherwise (since every cell has data).

The view size grows exponentially with the number of grouping attributes until it hits a "cliff" at rank $\lceil \log_r m \rceil$, after which it stabilizes at $m$. This means: materializing views above the cliff gives no space saving over the top view. The authors identify three key tradeoff points:

Time-Space Tradeoffs for the Hypercube

Let $k = \lceil \log_r m \rceil$ (the cliff rank) and $j$ be the rank that maximizes $\binom{n}{j} r^j$.

  • If $k > j$: Space = $(2r^{7/(r+1)})^n$, Time = $(2r^{7/(r+1)})^n$ — both are polynomial in $m$.
  • If $k < j$ and $k \leq n/2$: Space = $m$, Time = $m \cdot 2^n$ — space minimal but time exponential in $n$.
  • If $k < j$ and $k > n/2$: Space = $m$, Time = $\binom{n}{j} r^j$ — the time-optimal solution under minimal space.

8. Why This Paper Matters

This paper established the foundational theory for what we today call OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) and materialized view selection. Its contributions are threefold.

First, the lattice framework is a clean mathematical vocabulary for describing when one query can be answered using another's results. This framework handles not just flat hypercubes but hierarchical dimensions (the time dimension with day/week/month/year) via direct products of individual dimension lattices. Every subsequent paper on view selection uses some variant of this framework.

Second, the greedy algorithm is both practical and theoretically justified. The 63% approximation guarantee means you can use greedy with confidence that you are not making a catastrophic choice. Commercial OLAP systems — including Microsoft Analysis Services and IBM DB2 OLAP Server — implemented variants of this greedy approach.

Third, the analysis of the hypercube gives concrete guidance for practitioners: there is a natural "cliff" in view sizes, and materializing views above the cliff is wasteful. The paper characterizes three natural operating points on the time-space tradeoff curve, giving database administrators a principled way to reason about how much storage buys how much query speed.

Limitation — the uniform query assumption

The linear cost model and the benefit calculation both assume that all queries arrive with equal probability — i.e., that users are equally likely to ask for any view in the lattice. In practice, certain views (e.g., sales by product category by week) are queried far more often than others (e.g., sales by individual raw product by exact second). The paper acknowledges this and shows the fix is simple: weight each view by its query frequency when computing the benefit. The greedy algorithm then achieves the same 63% approximation guarantee on weighted benefit. The deeper issue — estimating query frequencies in advance — is a query workload problem that the paper correctly leaves to future work.
