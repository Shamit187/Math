Scientific Paper · VLDB 1994

Fast Algorithms for Mining Association Rules

Rakesh Agrawal, Ramakrishnan Srikant Proceedings of the 20th International Conference on Very Large Data Bases (VLDB), Santiago, Chile, 1994
Abstract We consider the problem of discovering association rules between items in a large database of sales transactions. We present two new algorithms — Apriori and AprioriTid — that are fundamentally different from the known algorithms AIS and SETM. Empirical evaluation shows that these algorithms outperform the known algorithms by factors ranging from three for small problems to more than an order of magnitude for large problems. We also show how the best features of the two proposed algorithms can be combined into a hybrid algorithm, AprioriHybrid. Scale-up experiments show that AprioriHybrid scales linearly with the number of transactions and has excellent scale-up properties on both the transaction size and the number of items in the database.

1. Introduction

The 1993 Agrawal-Imielinski-Swami paper introduced the association rule problem and proposed a first algorithm. That algorithm used a statistical estimation heuristic to predict whether a candidate itemset would be large before counting it, balancing passes over the data against wasted measurement. It worked, but it was complex: the estimation could be wrong, leading to either extra passes (missed large itemsets) or wasted counting effort (measured small itemsets).

Agrawal and Srikant ask a different question in this paper: is there a cleaner structural property of large itemsets that we can exploit — one that requires no statistical estimation at all? The answer is yes, and it leads to the Apriori principle, one of the most elegant ideas in data mining. The resulting algorithms are simpler, faster by orders of magnitude, and guarantee completeness without any heuristic estimation.

2. The Apriori Principle

Definition — Support and Large Itemsets

Let $\mathcal{I}$ be the set of all items and $\mathcal{D}$ the transaction database. An association rule is an implication $X \Rightarrow Y$, where $X \subset \mathcal{I}$, $Y \subset \mathcal{I}$, and $X \cap Y = \emptyset$. The rule holds in $\mathcal{D}$ with confidence $c$ if $c\%$ of transactions containing X also contain Y. It has support $s$ if $s\%$ of all transactions contain $X \cup Y$. An itemset with support at least minsup is called a large itemset; all others are small. An itemset of size $k$ is called a k-itemset.

The Apriori Principle — Anti-Monotonicity of Support

Any subset of a large itemset must itself be large. Equivalently, if an itemset is small (support below minsup), then every superset of it is also small. This single observation transforms the search problem. In the 1993 algorithm, the algorithm had to guess which extensions might be large. With the Apriori principle, the algorithm knows for certain: if itemset AB is small, then ABC, ABD, ABCE, and every other superset cannot possibly be large — they are pruned without any counting at all.

The proof is immediate: if $X \cup Y$ appears in some transaction, then X also appears in that transaction (since $X \subseteq X \cup Y$). So every transaction containing the superset also contains the subset. The support of the subset can only be greater than or equal to the support of the superset. If the superset's support is at least minsup, the subset's must be too; if the subset's support is below minsup, the superset's must be even lower.

Pause — how powerful is this?

Suppose there are 1000 items. The total number of possible itemsets is $2^{1000}$ — incomprehensibly large. But suppose only 50 individual items have support above minsup. Then the only possible large itemsets can contain subsets of those 50 items — down to $2^{50}$ candidates. If the data is sparse (which real retail data usually is), even most of those are pruned immediately. In practice, the total number of large itemsets is often in the thousands, not the billions. Anti-monotonicity is the key that makes this feasible.

3. Algorithm Apriori

The Apriori algorithm proceeds in passes indexed by itemset size $k$. In pass $k$, it counts candidate $k$-itemsets $C_k$ (candidates for being large), identifies which ones are large (forming the set $L_k$), and uses $L_k$ to generate candidates $C_{k+1}$ for the next pass. The algorithm terminates when no new large itemsets are found.

Algorithm Apriori — Pseudocode

1.  $L_1 = \{\text{large 1-itemsets}\}$
2. for $k = 2$; $L_{k-1} \neq \emptyset$; $k\!+\!+$ do begin
3.    $C_k$ = apriori-gen($L_{k-1}$);  // new candidates
4.   forall transactions $t \in \mathcal{D}$ do begin
5.      $C_t$ = subset($C_k, t$);  // candidates in t
6.     forall candidates $c \in C_t$ do  c.count++;
7.   end
8.    $L_k = \{c \in C_k \mid c.\text{count} \geq \text{minsup}\}$
9. end
Answer = $\bigcup_k L_k$

The apriori-gen function: Join then Prune

The candidate generation step — apriori-gen — is the heart of the algorithm. Given $L_{k-1}$, it produces a superset of all large $k$-itemsets. It works in two steps:

Join step: Self-join $L_{k-1}$ with itself. Two itemsets $p$ and $q$ are joined if they share their first $k-2$ items (in sorted order) and differ only in the last item. The resulting candidate is the union of the two itemsets. In SQL terms:

Example — Join Step SQL 
insert into C_k
select p.item_1, ..., p.item_{k-1}, q.item_{k-1}
from L_{k-1} p, L_{k-1} q
where p.item_1 = q.item_1, ..., p.item_{k-2} = q.item_{k-2},
      p.item_{k-1} < q.item_{k-1};

This generates exactly the minimal superset of all possible large $k$-itemsets, because any large $k$-itemset must have two large $(k-1)$-itemset subsets that differ in exactly one item, and those two subsets will be joined.

Prune step: Delete from $C_k$ any candidate $c$ that has some $(k-1)$-subset not in $L_{k-1}$. By the Apriori principle, such a candidate cannot be large. This pruning step can eliminate a substantial fraction of candidates before any database scanning.

Example — apriori-gen in action

Suppose $L_3 = \{\{1,2,3\}, \{1,2,4\}, \{1,3,4\}, \{1,3,5\}, \{2,3,4\}\}$. The join step produces $C_4 = \{\{1,2,3,4\}, \{1,3,4,5\}\}$ plus others. But the prune step removes $\{1,3,4,5\}$ because $\{1,4,5\}$ is not in $L_3$. Only $\{1,2,3,4\}$ survives as a candidate.

Counting candidates efficiently: the hash-tree

For each transaction, the algorithm needs to find which candidates in $C_k$ are subsets of the transaction. Doing this naively — checking every candidate against every transaction — is too slow. Instead, candidates are stored in a hash-tree. Each interior node at depth $d$ hashes on the $d$-th item of a candidate. Leaves store actual itemsets. Given a transaction, the subset function descends the tree, hashing on items in the transaction in order, and returns all matching candidates in time proportional to the number of candidates found — much faster than linear search through all of $C_k$.

4. Algorithm AprioriTid

Apriori scans the original database in every pass. For large values of $k$, however, the number of candidate itemsets shrinks dramatically, and most transactions contain no candidate at all. Scanning the entire database is then wasteful. AprioriTid addresses this by replacing later scans of the database with scans of a much smaller structure called $\overline{C}_k$.

Each entry in $\overline{C}_k$ has the form $\langle TID, \{X_k\} \rangle$, where TID is a transaction identifier and $X_k$ is the set of candidate $k$-itemsets contained in that transaction. For $k = 1$, $\overline{C}_1$ is essentially the original database. For $k > 1$, $\overline{C}_k$ is generated from $\overline{C}_{k-1}$: for each transaction, the algorithm looks at all candidates it contained in pass $k-1$ and checks whether their extensions form candidates in $C_k$.

Intuition — when AprioriTid wins

In early passes, most transactions contain many candidate itemsets and $\overline{C}_k$ is actually larger than the database — using AprioriTid would slow things down. In later passes, most transactions contain few or no candidate itemsets, so $\overline{C}_k$ shrinks far below the database size. At this point, AprioriTid wins decisively because it avoids reading the full database. The optimal strategy — which the authors call AprioriHybrid — is to use Apriori for early passes and switch to AprioriTid once the estimated size of $\overline{C}_k$ fits in memory.

5. AprioriHybrid

AprioriHybrid uses a heuristic to estimate the size of $\overline{C}_k$ at the end of each pass, based on the support counts of the current candidates:

$$|\overline{C}_k| \approx \sum_{\text{candidate } c \in C_k} \text{support-count}(c) + |\mathcal{D}|$$

If this estimate falls within available memory, the algorithm switches to AprioriTid in the next pass. Switching back from AprioriTid to Apriori requires an extra cost of adding TIDs to $\overline{C}_{k+1}$, so the algorithm also adds a condition: the number of large $(k+1)$-itemsets must be fewer than the number of large $k$-itemsets (indicating the search space is converging). Once switched, the algorithm stays on AprioriTid.

6. Performance Evaluation

The authors generated synthetic datasets to mimic the retailing environment. Each dataset is parameterized by: $|D|$ (number of transactions), $|T|$ (average transaction size), $|I|$ (average size of maximal potentially large itemsets), and $N$ (number of items). Dataset names encode these parameters: T10.I4.D100K means average transaction size 10, average large itemset size 4, and 100,000 transactions.

Key experimental findings

Apriori vs. AIS: Apriori was always faster than AIS, the algorithm from the 1993 paper. The advantage grew with problem size — from a factor of ~2 at high minimum support to over an order of magnitude at low support thresholds.

Apriori vs. SETM: SETM (another prior algorithm that used SQL joins) was consistently slower than Apriori by a factor of 10 or more, and was simply too slow to run on large datasets with transaction size 20.

AprioriTid vs. Apriori: For small problems with short transactions, AprioriTid did roughly as well as Apriori. For larger problems, AprioriTid degraded because its $\overline{C}_k$ sets grew too large to fit in memory and needed external sorting.

AprioriHybrid: Almost always better than either algorithm alone, and scales linearly with the number of transactions, with execution time decreasing slightly as the number of items grows (because average per-item support falls, yielding fewer large itemsets).

The fundamental explanation for why AIS and SETM are slower: both generate candidates on-the-fly as the database is read. This means a candidate is generated each time a large itemset appears in a transaction. Apriori generates candidates only once, using $L_{k-1}$, before any database scanning. As Figure 5 in the paper shows, the size of the candidate set $C_k$ in Apriori collapses dramatically after the second pass, because the Apriori pruning step eliminates candidates whose subsets are not all large. By pass 3, almost every candidate Apriori counts turns out to be large — virtually no wasted effort.

Caveat — the second pass is expensive

In pass 2, the candidate set $C_2$ is the cross-product of $L_1$ with itself — potentially very large. The paper notes this is an unavoidable cost: you cannot know which pairs of items are large without counting them. However, from pass 3 onward, the Apriori pruning dramatically reduces the candidate set, and the algorithm becomes extremely efficient. Users working with very large item sets and very low support thresholds should expect pass 2 to dominate total runtime.

7. Why Apriori Became the Standard

The Apriori algorithm has been cited tens of thousands of times and is the basis of essentially every textbook treatment of association rule mining. Its elegance comes from the complete separation of the algorithm into distinct, clean phases: generate-all-candidates from the previous level's large itemsets, scan the database to count them, discard those below minsup. There are no heuristics, no estimation, no risk of missing a large itemset due to a bad prediction. The anti-monotone pruning guarantee is mathematically clean and intuitively obvious once stated.

Subsequent work extended Apriori in many directions: FP-growth (2000) avoided repeated database scans by compressing the database into a trie; ECLAT (2000) used vertical data formats. But all of these exploited the same anti-monotone principle. The concept of support as a measure of interestingness also generalized beyond itemsets — to sequences (GSP, 1995), graphs (gSpan, 2002), and labeled trees — always with the same pruning logic at the core.

The deeper lesson

The reason Apriori is so influential is not merely that it is fast. It is that it makes the right abstraction: support as a measure that is monotone downward. Once you have that abstraction, the level-by-level search strategy is forced on you. The same pattern — find a monotone property, search level by level, prune aggressively — appears in constraint satisfaction, SAT solving, and many other combinatorial search problems. Apriori is a beautiful instance of a very general principle.
