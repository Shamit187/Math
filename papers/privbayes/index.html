PrivBayes: Private Data Release via Bayesian Networks

Jun Zhang, Graham Cormode, Cecilia M. Procopiuc, Divesh Srivastava, Xiaokui Xiao ACM SIGMOD 2014 · Snowbird, UT, USA
Abstract Privacy-preserving data publishing is increasingly important as data owners wish to share datasets for social analysis, medical research, and public transparency without exposing individual private information. The gold standard of protection is differential privacy, but existing differentially private methods collapse on high-dimensional data: the noise required to hide any individual swamps the actual signal in the data. PrivBayes sidesteps this by learning a low-degree Bayesian network over the sensitive database — a compact graphical model of attribute correlations — and then releasing noisy estimates of only the low-dimensional conditional distributions implied by that network. The network plus its noisy conditionals are then used to sample an arbitrarily large synthetic dataset. Because all noise is injected into low-dimensional marginals rather than the full high-dimensional space, the signal-to-noise ratio remains healthy even for tables with dozens of columns. Experiments on four real-world datasets show PrivBayes consistently outperforming prior methods for both marginal query answering and SVM classification tasks.

1. The Privacy Problem

Imagine a hospital wants to release a dataset of patient records for public health research. Each row represents one patient; each column is an attribute — age, diagnosis, postcode, employment status, income bracket. The hospital wants researchers to be able to study correlations — for instance, that diabetic patients in a certain age group tend to have lower incomes. But it cannot release the raw table: anyone could look up a specific individual and infer private facts about them.

The naive fix — add random noise to every cell — produces a table that is useless for analysis because the noise swamps every interesting pattern. A smarter fix releases aggregate statistics (counts and averages) rather than individual rows. But as soon as you have many attributes, even releasing all pairwise counts requires an exponentially growing number of queries, and the noise on each one compounds.

Intuition — The curse of dimensionality in private release

A table with $d$ binary attributes has a domain of size $2^d$. Directly releasing the full distribution over all attribute combinations requires noising $2^d$ cells. With $d = 30$ attributes, that is over a billion cells. With $n$ rows in the database, the average count per cell is $n / 2^d$, which for $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ is roughly $10^{-3}$. Any Laplace noise large enough to provide privacy completely obliterates a signal that small. The published table is essentially random — useless.

PrivBayes offers an escape: rather than trying to characterize the full joint distribution over all $d$ attributes simultaneously, it identifies a small set of low-dimensional marginal distributions whose product closely approximates the full joint. Noise is added only to these low-dimensional distributions, where the signal-to-noise ratio is manageable.

2. Differential Privacy

Before seeing how PrivBayes works, we need to understand the privacy guarantee it aims to provide. Differential privacy (DP) is a mathematical definition introduced by Dwork et al. that has become the accepted gold standard. Its appeal is that it makes no assumptions about what an attacker knows — even an adversary who already knows all but one record in the database gains almost no information about that remaining record from the published output.

Definition — epsilon-Differential Privacy

A randomized algorithm $G$ satisfies $\varepsilon$-differential privacy if, for every pair of datasets $D_1$ and $D_2$ that differ in exactly one tuple, and for every possible output $O$:

$$\Pr[G(D_1) = O] \leq e^{\varepsilon} \cdot \Pr[G(D_2) = O]$$

The parameter $\varepsilon$ (the privacy budget) controls the trade-off: smaller $\varepsilon$ means stronger privacy and more noise; larger $\varepsilon$ means weaker privacy but higher accuracy. Typical published values range from $\varepsilon = 0.1$ (strong) to $\varepsilon = 1{-}2$ (moderate).

Two mechanisms implement DP in practice. The Laplace mechanism works for numeric functions: it adds i.i.d. noise drawn from a Laplace distribution $\text{Lap}(\lambda)$ with scale $\lambda = S(F)/\varepsilon$, where the sensitivity $S(F)$ is the maximum change in $F$'s output when one tuple is altered. The exponential mechanism works for categorical choices: it samples an output proportional to $\exp(f_s(D, \omega)/2\Delta)$ where $f_s$ is a quality score and $\Delta$ is a scale factor tied to its sensitivity.

Intuition — Why the Laplace mechanism works

Think of sensitivity as asking: "If I change one person's record, how much can the answer change?" For a count query ("how many people are diabetic?"), one person can shift the answer by at most 1, so sensitivity is 1. Adding Laplace noise with scale $1/\varepsilon$ is enough. For a query over all $2^d$ cells of a high-dimensional histogram, the sensitivity is still 1 per cell, but you need that noise in every cell — a crippling amount of total noise.

3. Bayesian Networks as a Tool

A Bayesian network over a set of attributes $\mathcal{A}$ is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where each node represents one attribute and each directed edge encodes a conditional dependence. The key property is the Markov condition: given its parents, each attribute is conditionally independent of all its non-descendants. This lets the full joint distribution factor into a product of low-dimensional conditionals.

Formally, if each attribute $X_i$ has parent set $\Pi_i$ in the network, then:

$$\Pr[\mathcal{A}] = \prod_{i=1}^{d} \Pr[X_i \mid \Pi_i]$$

Example — A five-attribute Bayesian network

The paper uses an example with attributes: age, education, workclass, title, and income. In the learned network, the factorization reads:

$$\Pr[A,B,C,D,E] = \Pr[\text{age}] \cdot \Pr[\text{edu} \mid \text{age}] \cdot \Pr[\text{wc} \mid \text{age}] \cdot \Pr[\text{title} \mid \text{age, edu, wc}] \cdot \Pr[\text{income} \mid \text{wc, title}]$$

Instead of one 5-dimensional table (perhaps millions of cells), we have five small conditional tables, the largest being 3-dimensional. This is what makes noise addition tractable.

The degree of a Bayesian network is the maximum number of parents any attribute has. A degree-$k$ network involves only $d$ marginal distributions, each at most $k+1$ dimensional. PrivBayes uses small $k$ (often 1–3) to keep the dimensionality of each conditional low, which keeps the Laplace noise manageable.

4. The PrivBayes Algorithm

PrivBayes runs in three phases, each with a clear role. The privacy budget $\varepsilon$ is split evenly: half goes to learning the network structure, half to noising the conditional distributions.

Phase 1 — Network Learning (uses epsilon over 2)

Build a degree-$k$ Bayesian network $\mathcal{N}$ that best captures the correlations in the sensitive database $D$, without exceeding $\varepsilon/2$ privacy cost. The output is a set of attribute-parent pairs $(X_1, \Pi_1), \ldots, (X_d, \Pi_d)$.

Phase 2 — Noisy Conditionals (uses epsilon over 2)

For each attribute-parent pair $(X_i, \Pi_i)$, materialize the joint distribution $\Pr[X_i, \Pi_i]$ from the data, inject Laplace noise with scale $\frac{4(d-k)}{n\varepsilon}$, clip negatives to zero, renormalize, and derive the noisy conditional $\Pr^*[X_i \mid \Pi_i]$. These $d$ noisy conditionals form the set $\mathcal{P}^*$.

Phase 3 — Data Synthesis (no privacy cost)

Use $\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathcal{P}^*$ to sample synthetic tuples one attribute at a time, in topological order. Because no new database access is required, this phase incurs zero additional privacy cost. Generate as many synthetic tuples as desired — typically $n$, the same as the original.

5. Learning the Network Privately

The key algorithmic challenge is Phase 1: how do you choose which edges to include in the Bayesian network without spending too much privacy budget? The natural quality criterion is mutual information — an edge from $\Pi$ to $X$ is valuable if knowing $\Pi$ tells you a lot about $X$. Mutual information between $X$ and $\Pi$ is:

$$I(X, \Pi) = \sum_{x \in \text{dom}(X)} \sum_{\pi \in \text{dom}(\Pi)} \Pr[X{=}x, \Pi{=}\pi] \log \frac{\Pr[X{=}x, \Pi{=}\pi]}{\Pr[X{=}x]\,\Pr[\Pi{=}\pi]}$$

The GreedyBayes algorithm (Algorithm 2 in the paper) builds the network iteratively: at each step it selects the attribute-parent pair $(X_i, \Pi_i)$ that maximizes mutual information, subject to the constraint that $|\Pi_i| \leq k$. Each such selection is made using the exponential mechanism so it satisfies $\varepsilon/2(d-1)$-DP. Over all $d-1$ iterations, composability of DP gives total cost $\varepsilon/2$.

Subtlety — Why not use mutual information directly as the score?

The exponential mechanism's effectiveness depends on the sensitivity-to-range ratio of the score function. Mutual information $I(X, \Pi)$ has sensitivity $S(I)$ that can be as large as $\log n / n$ while its range is $\log n$ — making the scaling factor $\Delta = 2(d-1)S(I)/\varepsilon$ very large and causing the mechanism to sample near-random pairs. PrivBayes instead defines a novel score function $F(X, \Pi)$ with sensitivity only $1/n$, independent of domain size, while still being positively correlated with mutual information. This is the paper's central technical innovation.

The score function $F(X, \Pi)$ is defined as the negative $L_1$ distance from the observed joint distribution to its nearest maximum joint distribution — a distribution that maximizes mutual information between $X$ and $\Pi$. By Theorem 2 in the paper, its sensitivity is exactly $1/n$, which makes the scaling factor small and the exponential mechanism sharply focused on high-information pairs.

6. Noisy Conditional Distributions

Once the network structure is fixed, Phase 2 constructs noisy conditional distributions for every attribute. For each $i \in [k{+}1, d]$, the algorithm (NoisyConditionals, Algorithm 1) materializes the joint $\Pr[X_i, \Pi_i]$ and injects Laplace noise with scale:

$$\lambda = \frac{4(d-k)}{n\varepsilon}$$

This scale is chosen so that each noisy conditional satisfies $\varepsilon/2(d-k)$-DP. Summing over all $d-k$ conditionals, the total cost is $\varepsilon/2$ by the composability property.

Intuition — Why low-dimensional noise is the key insight

Each conditional distribution $\Pr[X_i \mid \Pi_i]$ involves at most $k+1$ attributes. If each attribute has domain size 10, a degree-2 network involves at most $10^3 = 1{,}000$ cells. Average cell count is $n/1000$ — enormous compared to $n/2^d$ for the full joint. The noise needed is correspondingly smaller, and the signal survives. This is the entire point: work in low-dimensional spaces where the curse of dimensionality cannot reach you.

After noising, negative values are set to zero and each conditional is renormalized to sum to one. The paper proves (Lemma 3) that the resulting distributions are $\theta$-useful in the sense that the ratio of information scale to noise scale is at least $n\varepsilon / [(d-k) \cdot 2^{k+3}]$. This quantity guides the automatic selection of $k$.

7. Generating Synthetic Data

With the network $\mathcal{N}$ and the noisy conditionals $\mathcal{P}^*$ in hand, generating a synthetic tuple is simple: sample attributes in topological order. Attribute $X_1$ (which has no parents) is sampled from its noisy marginal. Each subsequent $X_i$ is sampled from $\Pr^*[X_i \mid \Pi_i]$, conditioned on the already-sampled values of its parents.

Because the network is a DAG and parents are always sampled before children, this is always well-defined. Crucially, this sampling phase never touches the original database $D$ — it uses only the already-published $\mathcal{N}$ and $\mathcal{P}^*$. So the third phase adds zero to the privacy cost, and you can generate as many synthetic rows as you want.

Pause and check — What exactly is being released?

The paper releases two things: (1) the network structure $\mathcal{N}$ — just a list of which attributes are parents of which — and (2) the noisy conditionals $\mathcal{P}^*$ — small probability tables, each covering only a few attributes. From these, anyone can sample synthetic rows without ever seeing the original data. The synthetic dataset looks like real records but is purely artificial. Downstream analysts can run any queries they like on it.

8. Results and Impact

The authors evaluate PrivBayes on four real-world datasets: Adult (45,222 individuals from the 1994 US Census, 15 attributes), NLTCS (21,574 long-term care survey records, 16 attributes), TPC-E (40,000 synthetic transaction records, 24 attributes), and BR2000 (38,000 Brazilian census records, 14 attributes). They test two tasks: answering marginal queries and training SVM classifiers.

For marginal query answering, PrivBayes is compared against three baselines: Laplace (noise on raw marginals), Fourier (noise on Fourier coefficients), and Contingency (contingency table approach). Across all datasets and privacy budgets from $\varepsilon = 0.05$ to $1.6$, PrivBayes has dramatically lower average variation distance from the true marginals — often by a factor of 2–5. The advantage is largest at small $\varepsilon$ (strong privacy) and large $\alpha$ (high-order marginals).

For SVM classification, PrivBayes generates one synthetic dataset that is used to train multiple classifiers simultaneously — spending the privacy budget only once. Competing methods (PrivateERM, PrivGene, Majority) must retrain independently for each classifier, dividing the budget. PrivBayes's misclassification rate is generally lower than all competitors except PrivateERM-Single (which uses the full $\varepsilon$ budget for one classifier at a time), and it degrades gracefully as privacy tightens.

Lasting impact

PrivBayes established a template that subsequent work — including NIST's differential privacy challenge winners — would follow: learn a graphical structure privately, then release only low-dimensional sufficient statistics. The paper's novel score function $F$ with sensitivity $1/n$ is a clean theoretical contribution that other researchers built on directly. By 2020, variants of PrivBayes were powering private data release at scale in government and industry.
