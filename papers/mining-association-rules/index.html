Scientific Paper · SIGMOD 1993

Mining Association Rules between Sets of Items in Large Databases

Rakesh Agrawal, Tomasz Imielinski, Arun Swami ACM SIGMOD International Conference on Management of Data, Washington DC, 1993
Abstract We are given a large database of customer transactions. Each transaction consists of items purchased by a customer in a single visit. This paper presents an efficient algorithm that generates all significant association rules between items in the database — for example, "90% of customers who buy bread and butter also buy milk." The algorithm uses buffer management and novel estimation and pruning techniques to balance the number of passes over the database against the cost of measuring candidate itemsets. Experiments on real sales data from a large retail company demonstrate the effectiveness of the approach.

1. Introduction

Supermarkets and large retailers accumulate enormous records of customer purchases at the transaction level. A transaction records the set of items bought by one customer on one visit. For decades, only aggregate summaries — total sales over a week, month, or year — were tractable for analysis. Bar-code technology changed that, making it feasible to store individual basket data for every transaction. The business question then becomes: what hidden regularities exist across these millions of baskets, and how do we find them automatically?

This paper introduces association rule mining as a precise computational problem. An association rule captures a co-purchase pattern of the form "customers who buy items in set X tend also to buy item Y." The classic motivating example: 90% of transactions that contain bread and butter also contain milk. Such rules can drive shelf layout, coupon design, catalog placement, and cross-marketing strategy. The authors frame this as a database problem — specifically, as a query over a very large table of binary vectors — and design an algorithm that scales to real retail data with tens of thousands of transactions and dozens of item categories.

Intuition — Why basket data is special

Each transaction is a binary vector: position k is 1 if the customer bought item k, 0 otherwise. With m items in the store, there are $2^m$ possible subsets. In a supermarket, m might exceed 1000, making brute-force enumeration completely infeasible. The challenge is to find, among this exponential space, only those subsets that appear in enough transactions to be statistically meaningful — and to do it without reading the entire database more times than necessary.

2. Formal Model

Let $\mathcal{I} = I_1, I_2, \ldots, I_m$ be the set of all items. A database T is a collection of transactions; each transaction t is a binary vector where $t[k] = 1$ if the customer bought item $I_k$ and $t[k] = 0$ otherwise. A transaction t satisfies an itemset X if $t[k]=1$ for all $I_k \in X$.

Definition — Association Rule

An association rule is an implication of the form $X \Rightarrow I_j$, where X is a set of items and $I_j$ is a single item not in X. The rule has confidence $c$ if at least $c\%$ of transactions that satisfy X also contain $I_j$. The rule has support $s$ if at least $s\%$ of all transactions satisfy $X \cup \{I_j\}$.

Support measures statistical significance: a rule with very low support is seen so rarely in the data that it might be a coincidence or simply not worth acting on commercially. Confidence measures the strength of the implication: given that a customer bought bread and butter, how reliably does that predict milk? The two thresholds, minsupport and minconfidence, are set by the user and reflect business priorities.

The authors decompose the rule-mining problem cleanly into two subproblems:

Problem Decomposition

Subproblem 1 — Large itemsets: Find all itemsets whose fractional transaction support exceeds minsupport. These are called large itemsets; all others are small.

Subproblem 2 — Rule generation: For each large itemset $Y = I_1 I_2 \ldots I_k$ with $k \geq 2$, generate all rules of the form $X \Rightarrow Y - X$ where X is a non-empty subset of Y with $k-1$ items, and the ratio of the support of Y to the support of X exceeds minconfidence.

Subproblem 2 is essentially free once you have the large itemsets and their support counts — it is a simple ratio check. The hard computational work is entirely in Subproblem 1, and that is where this paper focuses.

3. Discovering Large Itemsets

The algorithm makes multiple passes over the database. The key insight is a frontier set: at any point, the frontier is the collection of itemsets that are being extended during the current pass. In each pass, the algorithm generates candidate itemsets — extensions of frontier itemsets — and counts their support by scanning the database. After the pass, itemsets that exceed minsupport are declared large, and a new frontier is chosen for the next pass.

The trivial approach — measure every possible itemset in one pass — would require maintaining $2^m$ counters simultaneously, which is infeasible. The other extreme — measure only one new item per pass, advancing by one at a time — requires too many passes over the data. The algorithm seeks a middle ground, and three innovations make it practical: estimation, pruning based on remaining tuples, and pruning via synthesized functions.

Intuition — The estimation idea

Before the algorithm decides whether to measure a candidate itemset $X + Y$ in the current pass, it predicts whether it will turn out to be large. The prediction uses the statistical independence assumption: the expected support of $X + Y$ is approximately the support of X (measured in a previous pass) times the individual frequencies of each item in Y. Formally, if X was seen in $x$ tuples and we have processed $c$ tuples in the current pass, the estimated support $\bar{s}$ for $X + Y$ is:

$$\bar{s} = f(I_1) \times f(I_2) \times \ldots \times f(I_k) \times \frac{x - c}{\text{dbsize}}$$

where $f(I_j)$ is the relative frequency of item $I_j$ in the database, and $\frac{x-c}{\text{dbsize}}$ is the actual support of X in the remainder of the database yet to be scanned. If $\bar{s}$ is below minsupport, the algorithm predicts the candidate will be small and skips extending it further — a potentially large saving.

4. The Algorithm in Detail

The template algorithm is called LargeItemsets. It maintains two sets: L (confirmed large itemsets) and F (the frontier, starting with the empty itemset). Each iteration of the main loop performs one pass over the database:

Algorithm Sketch — LargeItemsets

Initialization: L = empty; F = {empty set}.
While the frontier F is non-empty:
  Scan every database tuple t. For each frontier itemset f contained in t, generate candidate itemsets $C_f$ as extensions of f that are also contained in t. Increment counters for each candidate.
  After the pass: for each candidate c, if $\text{count}(c)/\text{dbsize} \geq \text{minsupport}$, add c to L. Decide whether c should be a frontier itemset for the next pass (see Section 3.3 of the paper). Update F accordingly.

The candidate itemset generation procedure (the Extend function) avoids generating duplicates by ordering items and only extending with items that come later in the ordering than any item already in the itemset. The paper's worked example with $\mathcal{I} = \{A,B,C,D,E,F\}$ and frontier {AB} shows how a database tuple ABCDF yields candidates ABC (expected large), ABCF (expected large but cannot extend), and ABF (expected large but cannot extend), while ABD is expected small and not extended further.

Determining the frontier set

A subtle but important correctness issue: which itemsets should be placed in the frontier for the next pass? It is tempting to say "only the maximal large itemsets" — but this is wrong. Suppose frontier itemset AB was extended and both ABD and ABCD turned out large. Since ABD is non-maximal, it would be excluded from the next frontier. But then ABDF — a potentially large superset of ABD — would never be considered. The paper proves a lemma ensuring that only itemsets that were expected small but turned out large need to enter the frontier (since all extensions of expected-large itemsets have already been considered in the current pass).

Two pruning techniques

Remaining-tuples pruning: During a pass, after processing $c$ tuples, the algorithm knows that a candidate $X + Y$ has been seen in $s$ of those tuples. The maximum it could ever achieve, even if every remaining tuple contained it, is $\text{maxcount} = (x - c) + s$. If this maxcount is below $\text{minsupport} \times \text{dbsize}$, the candidate is pruned immediately, saving both memory and counting effort.

Pruning function optimization: The algorithm synthesizes linear functions of item indicators that serve as upper bounds on the support of an itemset family. For frontier itemset X, a pruning function has the form $w_1 I_{j_1} + w_2 I_{j_2} + \ldots + w_m I_{j_m} \leq \tau$, where item weights are set as $w_i = 2^{i-1}\epsilon$ for a small $\epsilon$. This allows the algorithm to discard candidates as soon as they are generated, before any counting.

Memory management

Because main memory may not hold all frontier and candidate itemsets simultaneously, the paper introduces a memory reclamation algorithm. When memory fills up, the algorithm first tries to move to disk those frontier itemsets whose extensions have not yet been generated. If that fails, it discards the candidate itemset with the most items (and all its siblings), promotes their parent into the frontier for the next pass, and disables further extensions of that parent in the current pass. The paper proves that even under these memory pressures, the algorithm maintains completeness (no large itemset is missed), no redundancy (no itemset is measured twice), and guaranteed progress (the algorithm always terminates).

5. Experiments

The algorithm was evaluated on real sales data from a large retail company: 46,873 customer transactions across 63 item departments. Running with a minimum support of 1% and minimum confidence of 50%, the algorithm discovered rules such as:

Example — Discovered Rules (confidence%, support%)

[Tires] ⇒ [Automotive Services] (98.80%, 5.79%)
[Auto Accessories], [Tires] ⇒ [Automotive Services] (98.29%, 1.47%)
[Auto Accessories] ⇒ [Automotive Services] (79.51%, 11.81%)
[Home Laundry Appliances] ⇒ [Maintenance Agreement Sales] (66.55%, 1.25%)
[Children's Hardlines] ⇒ [Infants and Children's Wear] (66.15%, 4.24%)

These rules are intuitive and commercially actionable. The timing on an IBM RS-6000/530H workstation was approximately 2 minutes 53 seconds of real time for this dataset. The experiments confirmed that the estimation procedure was highly accurate: most itemsets predicted to be large did turn out large, and vice versa. The pruning techniques eliminated a large fraction of candidate itemsets without measuring them, dramatically reducing wasted effort.

Pause and reflect

Notice the asymmetry between the two error modes. If the estimation procedure predicts an itemset is large but it turns out to be small, we wasted time counting it. If it predicts small but it turns out large, we might need an extra pass. The paper shows that on "boring" data (where statistical independence approximately holds), the algorithm terminates in very few passes. On "surprising" data it takes more passes — but those extra passes are revealing genuine structure that deviates from independence, which is arguably the most interesting output.

6. Why This Paper Matters

This 1993 paper is one of the foundational texts of the data mining field. It did several things simultaneously: it formalized the association rule problem in a clean mathematical framework; it showed that the problem could be decomposed into a tractable subproblem (finding large itemsets) and a trivial one (generating rules); it designed an algorithm with principled estimation and pruning; and it demonstrated real-world utility on actual retail data.

The concept of support as a threshold is particularly important. Without it, the space of possible rules is combinatorially explosive. With a minimum support threshold, the set of large itemsets is typically manageable, and the anti-monotone property — if an itemset is small, all its supersets must also be small — provides the key pruning principle that all subsequent work in this area would exploit. The follow-up paper by Agrawal and Srikant at VLDB 1994 (Apriori) made this principle the centerpiece of a dramatically cleaner algorithm. But the problem formulation and many of the key ideas — support, confidence, candidate generation, pruning — originate here.

Limitation to keep in mind

The paper's estimation procedure assumes statistical independence between items. In practice, retail data is highly correlated (people who buy diapers tend to buy baby food). When the independence assumption fails badly, the estimation accuracy drops and more passes are needed. The paper acknowledges this gracefully — more surprising data means more passes, but also more genuinely interesting rules — though later algorithms (Apriori in particular) would sidestep the estimation approach entirely in favor of a cleaner level-by-level strategy.
